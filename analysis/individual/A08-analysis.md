# A08 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

The note identifies the study's theme as curriculum renewal in higher education, with a specific analytical focus on the practical implementation process of new teaching proposals, rather than on curriculum design itself (EXPLICIT, section 2 of the note). The note explicitly frames the research problem as an imbalance in the existing literature: abundant scholarship addresses curriculum design and theoretical coherence, but there is reportedly little investigation into the difficulties of implementing curricula faithfully within real university contexts, and into how practical constraints end up distorting or altering the original design (EXPLICIT). The note states that the study is motivated by the observation that designing a new curriculum is a relatively straightforward process, whereas its effective practical implementation is much more challenging (EXPLICIT).

The note also records a stated relevance rationale: without understanding the practical barriers affecting curriculum execution, reforms risk remaining "on paper" only, and understanding these obstacles is presented as helping to avoid a widening gap between courses and advances in science, educational theory, and societal/labor-market demands (EXPLICIT, paraphrased from section 2 of the note).

## 2. Research objectives/questions

According to the note, the stated objective is to investigate the difficulties and barriers involved in the practical implementation of a renewed higher-education curriculum at one university, and to use the results to propose how institutions could better support such processes (EXPLICIT). Two research questions are explicitly recorded:
1. What are the barriers to curricular change?
2. How could university processes and practices be improved to better support curricular change?

(EXPLICIT, section 3 of the note.)

## 3. Context investigated

The note describes the empirical setting as the Bachelor of Youth Work course at a large, young Australian public university (founded in 1991), located in a state capital (EXPLICIT). The study reportedly spans a two-year period (2013–2015), covering two complete Action Learning cycles (EXPLICIT). The teaching team is described as lean: one full-time permanent academic (project leader), one part-time contracted instructor, four casual/sessional teachers who worked primarily as community field practitioners, and partial involvement from a university instructional designer (EXPLICIT).

## 4. Methodological approach

The note characterizes the study as a qualitative exploratory study based on the Action Learning method, operating in cycles composed of dialogic questioning, planning, action, data observation, reflection, and replanning (EXPLICIT). Data collection reportedly combined institutional data (retention, grades, student feedback drawn from university systems) with data collected directly by the team: annual evaluation meetings with the full teaching staff (with casual staff paid to participate while project funding was available), qualitative student feedback focused on what students actually learned, detailed analysis of samples of student work and assessments, and a systematic internal review of units and materials (EXPLICIT, section 5 of the note).

DERIVED: The combination of Action Learning as both the investigative method and, apparently, part of the pedagogical intervention itself (the note states Action Learning was also used "as practical and methodological support for continuous formative evaluation and collective teacher learning," section 4) suggests the study may not cleanly separate the role of researcher from the role of practitioner-reformer. This is a reasonable inference from the note's description of the author/project leader as simultaneously the course's permanent academic leading the reform, but the note does not explicitly discuss researcher-practitioner positionality as a methodological issue, so this remains an interpretive observation rather than an explicit claim in the note.

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note situates the article within critical curriculum literature, described as literature analyzing the relationship between knowledge, education, and power relations within the university (EXPLICIT). It reports a multi-layered conceptual approach combining five distinct frameworks (EXPLICIT, section 4 of the note):

- Barnett and Coate's (2005) framework, defining curriculum orientation across three learning domains: "knowing" (knowledge of the field), "acting" (competencies and practices), and "becoming/being" (how learning integrates with the self, particularly in professional ethical terms).
- Threshold Concepts (Meyer & Land, 2012), used at the discipline/unit level to identify which disciplinary concepts are crucial, harder to master, and structurally important, enabling content to be "pruned" and depth of theoretical focus prioritized.
- The Community of Inquiry (COI) framework (Garrison & Arbaugh, 2007), adopted specifically to organize pedagogical interactions and learning in the digital education environment.
- The SOLO taxonomy (Biggs & Tang, 2007), used for constructive alignment between assessment tasks and expected conceptual learning.
- Action Learning (Boshyk & Dilworth, 2010; Weinstein, 1998), used as practical/methodological support for continuous formative evaluation and collective teacher learning.

## 6. Object being investigated

The object under investigation, as recorded in the note, is the process and practical difficulties of implementing a curriculum reform — specifically the gap between "curriculum-as-designed" and "curriculum-as-enacted" (a distinction the note attributes to Barnett and Coate, quoted directly in section 10) — within a single course over a defined multi-year period. DERIVED: This positions the object of study not as curriculum content or design quality per se, but as an institutional and organizational process: how time allocation, staffing structures, documentation systems, and quality-assurance regimes interact with (and reportedly obstruct) pedagogical intentions. This inference follows directly from the note's repeated framing of "barriers" as arising from workload models, staffing precarity, and documentation templates rather than from flaws in the curricular design itself.

## 7. Main findings

The note groups the identified barriers into two main factors, plus a set of positive outcomes (EXPLICIT, section 6):

1. Lack of time, team stability, and curricular control:
   - The university's workload model allocated 39 hours for major unit redevelopment, but the team found actual time required was 200–250 hours per unit, not including subsequent adjustments (EXPLICIT, also directly quoted in section 10).
   - Of 11 required units in the course, the team directly managed only 7; the remaining 4 were shared with other courses and taught by faculty outside the project, and attempts to modify them failed or met resistance (EXPLICIT).
   - Heavy reliance on casual/sessional staff prevented distribution of material-writing workloads and overburdened the sole permanent instructor (the project leader), due to staff turnover risk (EXPLICIT).

2. Epistemological and institutional tension between curricular philosophies and university systems:
   - Internal record-keeping and quality systems, structured in response to requirements from the government agency TEQSA, required learning outcomes to be described in purely behavioral/positivist terms, which the note states made it impossible to formally register the "becoming/being" domain — described as crucial to practical professional ethical formation — because it could not be reduced to observable behaviors in the university's rigid forms (EXPLICIT).
   - Internal quality evaluations reportedly focused excessively on easily collected quantitative metrics (retention, satisfaction, under a "student-as-client" logic), which inhibited pedagogical experimentation due to instructors' fear of receiving scores below the averages required by management (EXPLICIT).

3. Positive results: despite the barriers, application of the methods reduced "over-stuffed" course content and raised first-year student retention to over 90% in on-campus classes, even amid a 30% increase in enrollment (from 102 to 134 students) (EXPLICIT).

## 8. Main arguments

Beyond the empirical findings, the note records several argumentative/interpretive claims attributed to the author (EXPLICIT, section 7):

- The scarcity of academic discussion about curriculum theories is argued to have allowed "atheoretical" technocratic views of curriculum to become integrated as if neutral within university bureaucratic systems.
- There is a chronic misalignment between an institutional positivist epistemology (favoring total control, metric predictability, and treating the student as a purchasing client) and social-constructivist/critical approaches (favoring fluid learning, development of subjective ethical virtues, and flexibility in response to student needs).
- Effective curriculum implementation requires real time and collaborative spaces for assimilation, which clashes with contemporary trends of academic work intensification and performativity, wherein instructors prioritize research over teaching to satisfy performance metrics.

DERIVED: Taken together, these arguments position the barriers identified in the findings (workload underestimation, documentation rigidity, metric-driven quality systems) not as isolated administrative problems but as symptoms of a deeper epistemological conflict between institutional (positivist/market) and pedagogical (critical/constructivist) logics. This connective reading is supported by the note's explicit pairing of the "epistemological and institutional tension" findings category (section 6) with the discussion's language of "chronic misalignment" between epistemologies (section 7).

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note explicitly records two contributions attributed to the study (section 8):

- Theoretical contribution: the study proposes and validates a multi-layered curricular model that integrates high-level pedagogical frameworks (Barnett and Coate) with practical unit- and assessment-level design and alignment tools (Threshold Concepts, COI, and SOLO).
- Methodological/practical contribution: the study demonstrates that Action Learning is a low-cost, high-utility strategy for the continuous professional development of higher-education instructors, particularly in fields where most instructors come directly from market/professional practice and lack formal pedagogical training.

## 10. Limitations

The note explicitly records two limitations (section 9):

- The study was conducted in a single specific course at a single university, has an exploratory character, and requires that generalization of findings depend on the reader's own judgment of similarity to their context.
- The lack of continued funding after the pilot project made it unfeasible to pay casual staff to participate in annual evaluation meetings, which reportedly diminished the reach of team improvement efforts.

Regarding future work, the note states that the text does not explicitly propose future studies in a dedicated section, but indirectly suggests that university management should investigate ways to create "buffers" against external regulatory requirements, to prevent reporting and documentation systems from stifling pedagogical innovation and the flexibility of course coordination (EXPLICIT, section 9).

## 11. Implications

DERIVED: The note's findings and arguments suggest institutional-level implications: that universities seeking curriculum renewal may need to revise workload models to reflect realistic redevelopment time, reduce reliance on precarious casual staffing for curriculum-critical work, and create documentation/quality-assurance mechanisms flexible enough to register non-behavioral learning domains such as "becoming/being." This is a reasonable extension of the explicit findings (workload underestimation, documentation rigidity, precarity) into practical institutional recommendations, though the note itself frames most of these as barriers identified rather than as a fully elaborated set of policy recommendations, apart from the "buffers" suggestion explicitly noted in section 9.

DERIVED: The finding that retention rose above 90% despite a 30% enrollment increase, combined with the described "over-stuffed" content reduction, could imply that curriculum reform on the "acting"/"knowing" domains had measurable positive effects even while the "becoming/being" domain remained bureaucratically invisible — suggesting that institutional quality metrics may register some positive effects of reform while systematically failing to register others. The note does not explicitly draw this connection between the metric-based results and the domain framework, so this is an interpretation rather than a stated conclusion.

## 12. Concepts relevant across the corpus

- The distinction between "curriculum-as-designed" and "curriculum-as-enacted" (attributed to Barnett and Coate) as a general lens for studying implementation fidelity.
- The three-domain curriculum model (knowing / acting / becoming-being) as a way of categorizing types of learning outcomes, including outcomes resistant to behavioral/positivist documentation.
- Threshold Concepts as a device for prioritizing disciplinary depth over content coverage.
- The tension between positivist/metric-driven institutional quality systems and constructivist/critical pedagogical philosophies.
- The concept of academic work "intensification and performativity" as a structural constraint on time available for teaching-related work.
- Reliance on casual/precarious academic staff as a structural barrier to continuity and collective capacity-building in curriculum work.
- Action Learning as a low-cost professional development and organizational-change method operating through iterative cycles (questioning, planning, action, observation, reflection, replanning).
- The idea of institutional "buffers" shielding pedagogical work from external regulatory/reporting demands.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

- The study's generalizability rests on a single course, in a single discipline (Youth Work), at a single university, over one specific two-year window; the note itself flags this as a limitation, but it is worth noting that the discipline (youth work) is described as staffed substantially by field practitioners without formal pedagogical training, which may make some findings (e.g., the value of Action Learning for teachers without pedagogical background) less transferable to disciplines with differently composed academic staff.
- The study's own workload-hours comparison (39 vs. 200–250 hours) is presented as a striking quantitative finding, but the note does not specify how these hours were measured, tracked, or validated (e.g., self-report, timesheets, estimation), which is relevant to assessing the strength of this claim.
- The positive outcome of retention above 90% is reported alongside the finding that the "becoming/being" domain could not be captured in documentation; DERIVED — this juxtaposition raises the question (not addressed in the note) of whether the reform's success is being partly evaluated using the very positivist/behavioral metrics (retention) that the article otherwise critiques as reductionist, creating a possible internal tension between the article's critique of metric-driven evaluation and its own use of a retention metric as evidence of success.
- The note frames the barriers largely as originating from external/institutional constraints (workload models, TEQSA-driven documentation, quality metrics) rather than from the multi-layered curricular model's own design choices; whether the model itself contributed to implementation difficulty (e.g., through complexity of integrating five distinct frameworks) is not explored in the note and represents an assumption left unchallenged within the available material.

## 17. Missing information

- The note does not specify the exact number of students or detailed demographic characteristics of the Youth Work cohort beyond enrollment figures (102 to 134 students).
- The note does not describe how "200-250 hours" of redevelopment time per unit was measured or by whom.
- The note does not provide detail on the specific qualitative feedback content from students beyond a general description that it focused on what students learned.
- The note does not specify which of the 11 required units were among the 7 managed directly by the team versus the 4 shared units, nor details of the resistance encountered when attempting to alter the shared units.
- The note does not report retention, satisfaction, or performance data for the four shared units not controlled by the team, precluding comparison between team-controlled and non-team-controlled units.
- The note does not elaborate on how the five theoretical frameworks (Barnett & Coate, Threshold Concepts, COI, SOLO, Action Learning) were operationally integrated in day-to-day curriculum design decisions, beyond describing their general purposes.
- The note does not specify sample sizes for the "detailed analyses of samples of student work" or the criteria used for that analysis.
- The note does not include information about peer review status, citation impact, or subsequent scholarly reception of the article.
