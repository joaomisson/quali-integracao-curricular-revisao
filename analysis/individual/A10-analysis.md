# A10 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

The note describes a study by Derek Thurber and Karen Bossen, published in *To Improve the Academy* (Vol. 44, No. 1, Winter 2025), titled "Exploring the potential role of educational developers in facilitating curriculum review processes" (DOI: https://doi.org/10.3998/tia.4500).

According to the note, the study addresses the role of educational developers (learning designers / teaching-and-learning specialists) as facilitators of curriculum review processes in higher education, specifically through the cultivation of faculty communities of practice. The note frames the underlying problem as follows: although curriculum review offers recognized academic benefits, it is not a widely consolidated practice in many areas of higher education. The note attributes this to faculty commonly facing "escassez de tempo e falta de conhecimento metodológico/especializado (expertise)" needed to plan and conduct curricular reviews autonomously, compounded by the fact that curriculum design and review tasks are frequently unrewarded or unrecognized within traditional academic workload models, generating resistance and confusion about who should take on this responsibility.

The note also records a stated relevance rationale: accreditation bodies (e.g., the Higher Learning Commission in the US), government agencies, and external demands for transparency and market competitiveness exert increasing pressure on higher education institutions to demonstrate program quality through measurable continuous improvement.

Evidence status: EXPLICIT (the theme, problem, and relevance rationale are stated directly in the note's "Tema e Problema de Pesquisa" section).

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note states the objective as investigating the usefulness and impact of educational developers in supporting the development of Communities of Practice (CoPs) among faculty, aimed at continuous curriculum improvement through facilitation of a structured review process.

The note records the research question (paraphrased from the note): if educational developers can share the burden and operational overload of facilitating the curriculum review process, will this support offer adequate opportunities for faculty to build communities of practice focused on curricular improvement in their own academic context?

Evidence status: EXPLICIT (both objective and question are explicitly recorded in the note).

## 3. Context investigated

The note reports that the study was conducted at the Mary Lou Fulton Teachers College (MLFTC), Arizona State University (ASU), during the spring 2021 semester. Participants were three full-time, tenured faculty responsible for the directive and teaching functions of a small, two-year, in-person master's program that graduated between 10 and 20 students annually.

The note explicitly flags this context as methodologically important later, in the limitations, noting that the environment investigated proceeded "sem interferências ou pressões institucionais por reformas compulsórias" — i.e., without institutional interference or pressure for compulsory reform, a comparatively unusual condition of stability that the note says facilitated the study's success.

Evidence status: EXPLICIT (institutional setting, participant count, and program size are stated directly in the note).

## 4. Methodological approach

The note characterizes the study as qualitative action research ("pesquisa-ação qualitativa"). The intervention is described as an adaptation of Yonnie Chyung's (2018) ten-step cyclical evaluation model, organized into three phases across four collaborative meetings over three months:

1. Identification phase: alignment of interests with faculty, who defined three priority analytical foci — students' sense of belonging, coherence of the academic curriculum, and employability pathways of alumni.
2. Planning phase: adaptation of Malone et al.'s (2012) general belonging scale to the program's context, and design of a curricular alignment mapping plan (based on Veltri et al., 2011) measuring dimensions of communication, saturation, and curricular engagement.
3. Implementation phase: systematic collection of student responses, faculty responses (assessing alignment of course-level objectives with overall program objectives), and extraction of historical ASU alumni survey data.

The note records that findings were compiled into a 15-page final report delivered to faculty, followed by a two-hour reflective discussion session in which developers facilitated but intervened minimally (less than 10% of total speaking time), intending to encourage autonomous faculty interaction.

For data analysis, the note states that after the intervention the three faculty participated in individual semi-structured interviews focused on perceptions of belonging, curriculum design, and the role of the review in the CoP. Recordings were transcribed verbatim and subjected to systematic thematic coding analysis using Atlas.ti software, structured according to descriptive and in vivo coding steps described by Saldaña (2021).

Evidence status: EXPLICIT (study type, intervention structure, phases, instruments, and analytic procedure are all explicitly recorded in the note).

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note identifies the following theoretical foundations:

- Communities of Practice (CoP) theory, attributed to Jean Lave (1991) and elaborated by Etienne Wenger (1998). The note records Wenger's three structuring pillars: domain (collective interest and shared identity), community (interactions, discussions, and mutual learning relationships), and practice (development of a shared repertoire of resources and intervention tools).
- Faculty Learning Communities (FLCs), a concept from Milton Cox (2004), described in the note as an interdisciplinary variant of CoPs within higher education, oriented toward fostering evidence-based pedagogical practices.
- Prior curriculum-mapping research (e.g., Uchiyama & Radin, 2009; Salmona & Smart, 2017), which according to the note shows that CoPs support curricular restructuring by enabling faculty to rethink everyday practices, but that such processes commonly fail due to practical implementation challenges: lack of faculty ownership, chronic scarcity of time and resources, and traditional barriers between disciplines.
- The concept of "boundary object," attributed to Wenger (1998) in the discussion section, used to interpret the function of the curricular review report as an artifact bridging faculty perspectives.

Evidence status: EXPLICIT (all theoretical references and their attributions are stated in the note).

## 6. Object being investigated

The object under investigation, as recorded in the note, is the process and effects of embedding educational developers as facilitators within a structured curriculum-review action-research cycle involving a specific three-person faculty team in one small graduate program. The unit of analysis appears to be the developer-facilitated review process itself and its downstream effects on faculty community formation, rather than the curriculum content per se.

Evidence status: DERIVED. The note does not explicitly name "the process and its effects on community formation" as the object of investigation, but this framing follows directly from combining the stated objective (section 2), the described intervention (section 4), and the results emphasizing CoP formation and the report's role (section 7); it aggregates explicit elements into an explicit-adjacent characterization rather than introducing new information.

## 7. Main findings

The note reports the following results:

- Fostering and consolidation of a CoP: all participants reportedly affirmed that participating in the process unified the group and activated a productive space for horizontal conversations about course improvement.
- The report functioned as a "point-at-able object": the alignment map and report served as tangible anchors; beyond the statistical data itself, the physical existence of the document established conditions that guided faculty reflection on students' academic trajectories.
- The facilitator's neutrality role: faculty reportedly highlighted that having external, neutral educational developers avoided the process appearing as an imposition of particular power agendas, which the note says fostered a comfortable and safe environment for self-analysis and qualified professional development.
- Barriers to sustainability: despite localized success, participants reportedly expressed clear concern about continuing and maintaining the curricular goals after the facilitators' departure. The note records that faculty indicated the likelihood of pursuing improvements on their own, without continued facilitation, would be low, due to competing daily demands.

Evidence status: EXPLICIT (these are stated directly under "Principais Resultados" in the note, including participant-attributed perceptions).

## 8. Main arguments

The note records the following argumentative moves in the discussion/conclusion:

- The curriculum review report functioned as a "boundary object" (per Wenger, 1998), providing a practical basis for faculty learning by visually illustrating how individual courses interrelate from students' perspective.
- Facilitator political neutrality is presented as vital: the note states that curricular processes in higher education generally arrive in a top-down, imposed manner, which tends to erode faculty trust and undermine CoP formation potential.
- The time investment of the developers is argued to be the very condition enabling CoP viability: the note states that producing the diagnostic and detailed maps consumed resources that faculty could not mobilize on their own given competing research and governance duties, and concludes that developer technical support "é a própria condição que permite a manifestação de uma CoP viável" (paraphrased: is the very condition that permits the manifestation of a viable CoP).
- The single-semester intervention format is argued to be insufficient to perpetuate ongoing engagement and proposed curricular changes.

Evidence status: EXPLICIT (these argumentative claims are attributed directly to the authors in the note's "Discussão e Conclusões" section).

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note records two claimed contributions:

- Practical: the study reportedly offers a replicable methodological design for pedagogical partnership between administrative instructional-design units (e.g., digital learning offices or faculty development centers) and academic departments, demonstrating how to relieve faculty of time and data-processing burdens to open space for refined pedagogical design discussions.
- Theoretical: the study reportedly expands understanding and practical application of Wenger's Community of Practice concepts within higher education, documenting in detail the utility of mixed-data analytic reports as direct catalysts ("boundary objects") for collective academic decision-making.

Evidence status: EXPLICIT (both contributions are stated directly under "Contribuições" in the note).

## 10. Limitations

The note records three explicit limitations:

1. Reduced sample scale: the study centered on a single small master's program with only three faculty members.
2. High consumption of specialized resources: the process reportedly required approximately 100 collective hours of work from the two educational developers over one semester — described in the note as a considerable operational amount that may be infeasible for some institutions.
3. Non-political context: the environment investigated proceeded without institutional interference or pressure for compulsory reform, an unusually stable scenario that the note states facilitated the study's success.

The note also records three future-work directions: (1) evaluating replicability with larger undergraduate/graduate departments and larger faculty bodies; (2) scientifically measuring the comparative impact of this deep-advisory model against other standardized faculty-development center services; (3) structuring and examining new long-term continuous facilitation formats, e.g., using commitment tools generated in meetings to establish annual or semesterly follow-ups.

Evidence status: EXPLICIT (limitations and future work are stated directly under "Limitações e Trabalhos Futuros").

## 11. Implications

Implications recorded in the note center on institutional resourcing and sustainability: the finding that ~100 developer-hours were needed for a single small-program cycle implies that scaling this model to larger departments would require proportionally larger investments of specialized staff time, a concern the note itself raises as a limitation and future-work item. The note's emphasis on facilitator neutrality as "vital" for building faculty trust suggests an implied institutional design implication that curriculum review processes should be structured with external, non-evaluative facilitators rather than being administered by supervisors or administrators internal to the reviewed program. The note also implies that one-off, single-semester interventions are inadequate and that sustained, cyclical facilitation models are needed for durable curricular reform.

Evidence status: DERIVED. These implications are reasonable extrapolations from the explicit findings and discussion content (resource intensity data, neutrality argument, insufficiency-of-one-time-intervention finding) but are not phrased in the note as a distinct "implications" section; they are inferred by connecting explicit results/discussion statements to their logical institutional consequences.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

- Small, single-site sample: the study's claims about CoP formation, facilitator neutrality, and boundary-object effects rest on interview data from only three tenured faculty in one small graduate program; the note itself flags this as a limitation, but it is worth noting that the discussion section's stronger causal-sounding claims (e.g., that developer support "is the very condition" enabling a viable CoP) go beyond what a three-participant, single-cycle action research design can typically establish as generalizable causation.
- The note records that the institutional context was explicitly non-political and free of compulsory-reform pressure — a condition the authors themselves acknowledge may have facilitated success. This raises an internal tension: the study's central argument for the value of developer-facilitated neutrality was tested precisely in a setting where political neutrality was less likely to be contested, potentially limiting the strength of the "neutrality is vital" claim under more politically fraught conditions.
- The reliance on faculty self-report (post-intervention interviews with the same three faculty who were the direct beneficiaries of the intervention) raises a question, not addressed in the note, about potential response or social-desirability bias in participants' evaluations of a process the developers themselves also facilitated and the researchers apparently co-designed.
- The note documents a stated concern by participants themselves that they are unlikely to sustain the reforms without continued facilitation, which sits in tension with the practice contribution claim that the model is "replicable" — replicability of the facilitation process does not necessarily imply sustainability of outcomes once facilitation ends, and the note does not resolve this tension explicitly, though it does flag it as a limitation/future-work item.
- The resource-intensity finding (~100 collective developer-hours per semester for a 3-faculty program) sits in tension with the practical contribution claim of offering a broadly "replicable" model; the note itself raises scalability as an open question for larger departments rather than asserting resolved replicability at scale.

Evidence status: DERIVED for all of the above (the tensions are identified by juxtaposing explicit statements within the note, such as the coexistence of "replicable" framing with acknowledged resource intensity and sustainability concerns).
