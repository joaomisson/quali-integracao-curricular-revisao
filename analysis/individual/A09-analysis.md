# A09 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

According to the note, the study addresses the concept of "curriculum viability," defined as the general state of health, well-being, and operational sustainability of a curriculum. The note reports that traditional curriculum evaluations restrict themselves to measuring quality by comparing a curriculum against established quality standards, an approach the note characterizes as documental/audit-based and as functioning like a mere checklist (a "reviewer posture"). The problem identified by the authors, as recorded in the note, is that this kind of formal verification does not identify the inhibitors that directly affect the practical attainment of such standards. As a consequence, the note explains, a curriculum can be formally approved in external audits for meeting certain basic standards while simultaneously harboring dysfunctions and chronic problems that go entirely unnoticed.

The note also records a stated rationale for relevance: the curriculum is described as a living, organic, and highly dynamic entity that, without structured and constant review and renewal, tends to deteriorate, becoming obsolete or riddled with operational problems. Exposing hidden barriers to curricular success is presented as essential to guide effective pedagogical reforms and ensure the sustainability of educational improvements.

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note states the objective as conducting a scoping review of the academic literature to jointly identify curricular quality standards and inhibiting factors, integrating them into a unified framework for comprehensively assessing curriculum well-being (curriculum viability). Two research questions are recorded verbatim in substance (translated in the note):
1. What, according to the literature, constitutes curricular quality standards?
2. What inhibiting factors of curricular quality have been reported in the scientific literature?

## 3. Context investigated

The note indicates the review is situated in medical/health professions education, evidenced by references to accreditation bodies cited in the Fundamentação section (WFME, LCME, GMC) and by the inclusion criteria restricting to educational research literature in English from 1992–2017. The note does not specify a particular country, institution, or specific level of medical education (undergraduate vs. postgraduate) as the object of the underlying primary studies; it describes the review as scoping the broader academic literature rather than a single institutional setting.

## 4. Methodological approach

The note describes the study as a scoping review conducted under the methodological framework of Arksey and O'Malley. Key elements recorded in the note include:
- Primary systematic search in Web of Science (covering 8 integrated sub-databases, including Web of Science Core Collection, MEDLINE, and SciELO Citation Index), cross-checked with Google Scholar to capture grey literature and ensure reproducibility; manual search, snowballing, and direct consultation with experts were also used.
- Search terms combined via Boolean OR/AND operators (e.g., curriculum, viability, quality, indicators, problems, diseases, inhibitors).
- Inclusion criteria: original research articles and systematic reviews in the field of educational research, written in English, published 1992–2017.
- Exclusion criteria: grey literature outside WOS peer-review standards, abstracts, isolated citations, editorials, conference reports, letters to the editor, book chapters, and purely conceptual/position essays.
- Screening: initial search yielded 1233 studies; after initial filtering and duplicate removal, 36 full-text articles were reviewed by two independent reviewers.
- Quality appraisal: texts were assessed using the validated Qualsyst checklist, with a minimum cutoff of 50% methodological quality adherence; inter-rater agreement reached Cohen's Kappa of 0.682 (described in the note as good correlation).
- Final sample: 13 articles after qualitative exclusions.
- Data analysis: two authors performed qualitative thematic analysis of the 13 articles, using structured open, axial, and selective coding to consolidate themes.

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note records an extensive historical grounding traced by the authors, including:
- Classical curriculum theory anchored in Bosco's (1971) four pillars: objectives, content, teaching methods, and evaluation.
- Learning theories/paradigms shaping curriculum: behaviorism, cognitivism, constructivism.
- Curriculum organization models: Tyler's (1949) prescriptive/structured, behaviorist-based model; Hilda Taba's (1962) interactive model emphasizing mutual interaction of components; Walker's (1971) descriptive/naturalistic model (platform of beliefs, deliberation, design).
- Modern teaching models/guidelines: Harden's (1986) ten-question model; Mager's instructional objectives; Gagne's (1965) learning principles and the ADDIE instructional design model; van Merrienboer's 4C/ID model for professional competency development via whole tasks.
- Abrahamson's (1978) "curriculum diseases" metaphor, using pathological/medical terminology: curriculo-sclerosis (extreme departmentalization and disputes over course-hour allocation), curriculum carcinoma (disproportionate overgrowth of one discipline over others), curriculo-arthritis (absence of communication forums among faculty), and iatrogenic curriculitis (abrupt, excessive modifications made in reaction to external pressures).
- Global accreditation/standards systems: WFME global standards, LCME accreditation guidelines, GMC clinical training norms.

## 6. Object being investigated

The object under investigation, as recorded in the note, is the body of published academic literature reporting curricular quality standards and inhibiting factors — i.e., a scoping review of secondary literature rather than a primary empirical study of a specific curriculum or institution. The unit of analysis for the review itself is the set of 13 articles that survived screening and quality appraisal.

## 7. Main findings

The note reports the following findings:
- Scarcity of joint treatment of inhibitors: all 13 sampled articles addressed and reported quality standards, but the note records that only 2 studies (Bendermacher et al., 2017 and Olson et al., 2013) discussed quality standards and inhibiting factors together.
- The thematic/structural analysis identified eight major areas of curricular impact, systematized into a "Framework for Assessing Curriculum Viability" compiling 37 quality standards and 19 inhibitors across these areas: (1) Educational Strategy and Content, (2) Students, (3) Faculty, (4) Assessment, (5) Educational and Work Environment, (6) Curricular Communication, (7) Technology, (8) Leadership. For each area the note lists specific example standards and inhibitors (e.g., under Leadership, inhibitors include lack of clearly assigned roles and leaders acting as monopolistic "gatekeepers" of information).

## 8. Main arguments

The note records a conceptual distinction the authors draw between "reviewers" and "interpreters" in program evaluation: the traditional audit-based approach ("reviewers") is limited to a judgment of whether a given standard was met, whereas the viability-oriented approach ("interpreters") operates analogously to a clinical diagnosis performed by a doctor, investigating deep structural causes and dynamic variables leading to pedagogical failure or success.

The authors also argue, per the note, that the action of certain inhibitors is not necessarily obvious or linear — e.g., "student resistance to new curricula" does not mechanically affect the documental/technical quality of the curriculum, but constitutes a long-term impediment that sabotages reforms, blocks engagement, and compromises multiple sectors simultaneously (such as assessment and school climate).

Finally, the note records the authors framing structured inhibitor identification as a "prophylactic" procedure, allowing decision-makers to address hidden problems before they culminate in a complete collapse of the educational program.

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note lists three contributions attributed to the authors:
1. Formulation of a new concept: introducing and consolidating "curriculum viability" in the pedagogical literature as an indispensable measure of ongoing well-being that goes beyond conventional bureaucratic quality.
2. Development of the Viability Framework: an applicable instrument uniting WFME-type standards and inhibiting factors under 8 integrated dimensions, enabling complex institutional diagnostics.
3. Integration with curriculum mapping: the framework is presented as an advanced analytical support that works in symbiosis with traditional curriculum mapping, helping managers understand not only where operational discrepancies exist between planned, delivered, and learned curriculum, but the real causes behind those gaps.

## 10. Limitations

The note records two limitations explicitly attributed to the authors:
1. The scarcity of literature robustly and empirically addressing inhibiting factors jointly with standards prevented the creation of systemic cause-and-effect connections for each individual component of the proposed framework.
2. Restricting the scoping review search exclusively to English-language studies may have excluded crucial empirical findings produced in other linguistic and geographic contexts.

The note also records a future-research recommendation: the authors advocate for developing and validating new psychometric tools, questionnaires, and quantitative inventories to measure not only the degree of technical-standard compliance but also the degree of curriculum exposure to hidden inhibiting factors, generating preventive institutional diagnostics.

## 11. Implications

Based on the note, the study's stated implications are primarily practical/diagnostic: the Framework for Assessing Curriculum Viability is presented as a tool for institutional diagnosis that could shift evaluator practice from a compliance-checklist ("reviewer") posture to an investigative, diagnostic ("interpreter") posture. The note frames this as enabling preventive action — addressing "veiled problems" before they cause program collapse — and as complementary to existing curriculum-mapping practices. The note does not record implications for policy, accreditation-body practice change, or empirical validation studies beyond the stated recommendation for future psychometric tool development.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

- The note records that only 13 articles survived quality appraisal from an initial 1233 identified, and that only 2 of those 13 discussed inhibitors jointly with standards. This is a narrow empirical base from which the authors nonetheless derive a 37-standard/19-inhibitor framework across 8 domains; the note itself records the authors acknowledging that this scarcity prevented establishing systemic cause-and-effect connections, which raises a tension between the scope of the proposed framework and the thinness of the inhibitor-specific evidence underlying it.
- The framework's inhibitor list appears to be substantially synthesized/interpreted by the reviewing authors' thematic analysis (open/axial/selective coding) rather than drawn from a large body of directly comparable primary studies reporting inhibitors; the note does not clarify how many of the 13 articles actually contributed to the 19 inhibitors versus the 37 standards, beyond the "2 studies discussed both" finding. This leaves ambiguous how inhibitors were extracted from articles that (per the note) did not explicitly address inhibitors.
- The English-language-only restriction, acknowledged by the authors per the note, creates a linguistic/geographic bias that is in tension with the framework's implicit claim to generalizability across curricular contexts (the note references WFME, LCME, GMC — bodies with different geographic jurisdictions — without clarifying how representativeness across these systems was ensured).
- The "reviewer vs. interpreter" and clinical-diagnosis analogy is a rhetorically strong framing recorded in the note ("interpreters are like doctors of the curriculum"), but the note does not indicate that the study empirically tested whether an "interpreter" approach produces better outcomes than a "reviewer" approach — this appears to be a conceptual/normative argument rather than an empirically demonstrated one, based on what is recorded.
- The claim that formal standard compliance can coexist with severe hidden dysfunction is presented as a foundational premise rather than as a finding independently verified within this scoping review's own primary data (the review synthesizes secondary literature); the note does not record original empirical evidence generated by these authors demonstrating this coexistence, only the argument built from cited literature.

## 17. Missing information

- The note does not specify the disciplinary/level scope of the 13 included primary studies (e.g., whether they concern undergraduate medical education specifically, postgraduate, or mixed health-professions contexts) beyond the general "educational research" inclusion criterion.
- The note does not list the 13 articles or provide detail on which articles contributed which of the 37 standards or 19 inhibitors, beyond naming Bendermacher et al. (2017) and Olson et al. (2013) as the two addressing both standards and inhibitors.
- The note does not describe the specific Qualsyst scoring items or how the 50% cutoff was operationalized beyond stating it as a threshold.
- The note does not indicate whether the proposed Framework for Assessing Curriculum Viability was piloted, validated, or applied to any real curriculum within this study; it appears to be a conceptual/synthesis output rather than a tested instrument, but the note does not explicitly confirm or deny field application.
- The note does not provide information on funding sources, institutional affiliations in detail, or potential conflicts of interest.
- The note does not specify how many total citations/pages or which specific evidentiary weight (e.g., number of times each inhibitor was reported across the 13 articles) underlies each of the 19 inhibitors.
