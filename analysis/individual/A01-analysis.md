# A01 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

The note describes A01 as a case study of curriculum review and reform in higher education, applied specifically to a Merchandising and Fashion Design (MFD) program. According to the note, the motivating problem is twofold. First, institutions face continuous pressure to keep curricula current and relevant, driven by accreditation requirements and by the rapid pace of market change. Second, the note reports that curricular review is commonly described in the literature as an exhausting, complex, and emotionally draining task for faculty, one that frequently generates resistance and interpersonal conflict rooted in differing views and power dynamics among faculty. The note further records a specific gap the authors claim to address: most proposed curricular reform models are strictly linear, and this linearity is said to ignore the fact that, in institutional practice, the process is inherently messy, dynamic, and iterative.

This framing positions the study's problem simultaneously at two levels: a practical/managerial level (how to keep a specific program's curriculum current) and a conceptual/methodological level (a perceived mismatch between linear reform models in the literature and the lived, non-linear character of institutional curricular work).

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note states the study's objective as presenting and detailing a two-year, systematic, non-linear, deductive, participative, and iterative curricular review process, with the aim of demonstrating both its feasibility and its behavioral benefits through a case study.

The note explicitly indicates that the article does not present formal hypotheses to be statistically tested. Instead, it reports that the process was guided by practical questions posed to the faculty during meetings. Paraphrased, these questions concerned: whether the program was offering students the most up-to-date curriculum and what would best serve students; what differentiated the program from other programs; whether the most current industry techniques were being taught; how up-to-date information was being delivered in the classroom; and how individual faculty members were keeping their own teaching materials current. The note presents these five questions as direct quotations (in Portuguese) attributed to the faculty meetings; because the note's rendering of these questions is in Portuguese rather than the article's presumed original English, they are paraphrased here rather than reproduced as quotations, to avoid implying a translation was inserted inside quotation marks.

## 3. Context investigated

The note specifies a single institutional context: the Merchandising and Fashion Design (MFD) department of a public, metropolitan, medium-to-large university located in the Midwestern United States, with 24,390 students enrolled as of fall 2018. The reform process described spans two years and involves the department's full faculty body, together with external stakeholder groups (recent graduates/seniors and alumni) and comparison data from peer, competitive, and aspirant institutions. This is a single-case, single-discipline, single-institution context as recorded in the note.

## 4. Methodological approach

The note characterizes the study as a descriptive case study using a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative survey data with qualitative analysis of perceptions and discussions.

Participants and data, as recorded in the note: the entire departmental faculty participated actively in plenary meetings and a self-assessment process; a structured opinion survey was completed by 50 respondents (18 graduating seniors and 32 alumni), reflecting a 49% response rate out of 103 invited participants; curricular data from peer institutions were collected via internet research.

Data collection is described as occurring in two years. Year 1 (industry focus and internal diagnosis) involved documentary research on external curricula found online, faculty travel to interview alumni in person across various U.S. cities about technological trends and emerging careers, mixed questionnaires administered to students and alumni/employers, and an internal Continuous Improvement Process (CIP) self-assessment questionnaire completed individually by each faculty member regarding their course syllabi. Year 2 (benchmarking and peer review) involved systematic collection of comparison data (program names, required credit hours, course syllabi) from peer institutions, and intensive debate meetings structured around open-ended question scripts addressing individual courses and the curriculum as a whole.

Analytical procedures reported in the note include direct comparative (benchmarking) analysis between the department's curricular structure and reference programs; thematic content analysis and simple descriptive statistics applied to student/alumni survey data; and structured reflective sessions governed by pre-established conduct norms (mutual listening, respect for ideas, no interruptions) used to manage disagreement and build consensus among faculty.

Analytically, this is a practitioner-driven, largely qualitative and process-descriptive design, with a modest quantitative component (descriptive statistics on a 50-respondent survey) embedded within it. The note does not describe any inferential statistical testing, control condition, or independent evaluator external to the department undertaking the reform.

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note lists several distinct conceptual anchors rather than a single unified theoretical framework:

- The tripartite model of Garner and Buckley (1988), described as the guiding structure, holding that a strong curriculum must focus on three core constituencies: the educators who deliver it, the students who experience it, and the employers who hire graduates.
- Continuous environmental scanning, attributed to Laughlin and Kean (1995), used to identify technological, demographic, and market trends in the fashion field.
- A Continuous Improvement Process (CIP), described as inspired by Deming's (2000) continuous-quality model.
- Guidelines and competencies from the International Textile and Apparel Association (ITAA), used as general goal parameters for fashion and apparel education.
- A body of theory on behavioral barriers to change, addressing resistance among senior faculty (comfort with prepared courses) and junior faculty (time constraints tied to promotion and tenure processes), as well as departmental political/power dynamics.
- Peer review concepts attributed to Osborne and Purkey (1995), concerning the importance of mutual evaluation among faculty.

As recorded in the note, this is an eclectic combination of applied management/quality concepts (CIP, environmental scanning, benchmarking) and organizational-behavior concepts (resistance to change, peer review), organized loosely around the Garner and Buckley tripartite model. The note does not indicate that these foundations were integrated into a single explanatory theory; rather, they appear to function as practical scaffolding for designing the review process.

## 6. Object being investigated

Based on the note, the object of investigation is the curricular review process itself — its design, sequencing, and stakeholder dynamics — rather than a specific measurable educational outcome (such as student learning gains or employment rates). The unit of analysis is the two-year deductive, participative, iterative process undertaken by one department's faculty. Secondary objects captured within this process include: stakeholder perceptions (students, alumni) gathered via survey; faculty self-perceptions and interpersonal dynamics captured through self-assessment questionnaires and reflective meetings; and comparative curricular structures at peer institutions, used as an external benchmark.

## 7. Main findings

The note reports the following findings:

- The survey of students and alumni indicated strong demand for greater incorporation of technology into courses, addition of logistics and supply-chain content, and implementation of a textiles laboratory.
- Benchmarking confirmed that the absence of a logistics course was a real curricular gap relative to competing programs; as a direct result, a logistics/supply-chain course was integrated into the program's core curriculum.
- The capstone course was redesigned into an experiential real-market simulation called "Senior Market," in which design students develop full collections evaluated by a three-person panel of industry experts, while merchandising students handle market positioning and presentation to an external public audience.
- The process identified an imminent need to acquire new industrial machinery and software to keep courses aligned with current market technologies.
- Despite difficult debates marked by resistance to change and departmental power disputes, adherence to listening norms and the participative nature of the process are reported to have re-established more honest relationships and increased mutual understanding among faculty from different specialties (merchandising and design) — described in the note as an unexpected behavioral benefit.

The note does not report that the technology-integration or textiles-laboratory demands identified by students and alumni were themselves acted upon or resolved; only the logistics course addition and the capstone redesign are explicitly described as concrete curricular outcomes.

## 8. Main arguments

The note records the authors as arguing that successful curricular reform must abandon the rigidity of purely linear models and instead embrace an iterative, cyclic, and adaptive process that mirrors how reform actually unfolds inside institutions. A second argument is that facilitators and academic managers must understand the intrinsic motivations and behavioral barriers that generate resistance among junior and senior faculty in order to navigate difficult conversations constructively. A third argument is methodological: alternating between a macro perspective (the program as a whole) and a micro perspective (detailed analysis of each individual course) is presented as what ensures the faculty build a coherent and genuinely relevant curriculum. Finally, the authors are recorded as treating student success and employability as the ultimate validation metric for the reform project — though the note does not report that such outcome data were actually collected within this study.

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note attributes two types of contribution to the authors. A theoretical/methodological contribution: presentation of a non-linear framework, described in the note as innovative and replicable, built on two complementary phases and integrating the perspectives of multiple stakeholder groups (faculty, alumni, market, academia). A practical/organizational contribution: evidence that adopting transparent behavioral norms and peer review reduces faculty defensiveness, resulting in re-established, healthier professional relationships aligned around departmental goals.

## 10. Limitations

The note explicitly states that the text does not detail deep methodological limitations regarding the sample or the model. It does record that the authors acknowledge practical and logistical challenges: faculty fatigue in the face of an overwhelming volume of data, political/generational barriers to change, and severe budgetary and internal resource constraints.

Beyond what the note explicitly attributes to the authors' own limitations discussion, several additional limitations can be derived from the methodological details reported elsewhere in the note. First, the design is a single case within a single department at a single institution, which — given the case-study format and the absence of any comparison case reported in the note — constrains the basis on which the framework's "replicability" claim (see Section 9) can be assessed from the note alone. Second, the survey underlying the stakeholder-demand findings had a 49% response rate from 103 invited participants (50 respondents), a modest and self-selected sample for representing the full population of alumni and near-graduates; the note does not report any non-response bias analysis. Third, the note does not describe any external or independent evaluation of the reported relational/behavioral improvements ("more honest relationships," "increased mutual understanding") — these appear to be assessed by the same faculty group that both underwent and, through some of its members, authored the study, which raises a question about the independence of that assessment (this is a derived observation about the design as reported, not a limitation the note attributes to the authors themselves). Fourth, although the note records "student success and employability" as the stated final validation metric for the reform, the note does not report that outcome data of this kind were collected in this study; the authors' own suggested future work (see below) includes investigating long-term efficacy and post-reform learning assessment, which suggests this outcome-validation step was not yet performed within the reported study.

## 11. Implications

The note explicitly records the authors' suggested directions for future research: quantifying the satisfaction of different constituents (faculty, students, employers) over the course of applying the proposed framework; investigating the long-term effectiveness of institutionalizing the curricular changes adopted; and exploring post-reform assessment of learning.

Beyond these explicit future-work directions, some broader implications can be derived from the case as described, while remaining tentative given the single-case design. The emphasis on structured conduct norms (mutual listening, no interruptions) as a apparent mechanism for managing faculty resistance suggests that facilitation design, and not only curricular content decisions, may be treated by the authors as a substantive lever in reform processes — though the note does not report any isolated test of this mechanism apart from the overall process. Similarly, the pairing of external benchmarking with internal self-assessment (CIP) suggests, as a design implication, that combining externally referenced standards with internally generated reflection may be a way the authors frame legitimacy for curricular changes to skeptical faculty; the note does not, however, report a controlled comparison isolating the effect of benchmarking data from the effect of the participative process itself.

## 12. Concepts relevant across the corpus

- Tripartite stakeholder framing of curriculum design (educators, students, employers) as an organizing structure for gathering and weighing input.
- Environmental scanning as a mechanism for tracking market, technological, and demographic change relevant to curriculum content.
- Continuous Improvement Process (CIP) as an organizational quality-management concept applied to academic curricula.
- Behavioral-barriers framing of resistance to change, differentiated by career stage (junior vs. senior faculty) and linked to institutional incentive structures (promotion, tenure) and departmental power dynamics.
- Peer review as a consensus-building and mutual-evaluation mechanism among faculty.
- Benchmarking against peer, competitive, and aspirant institutions as a method for identifying curricular gaps.
- The distinction between linear/staged reform models and iterative/cyclic/messy reform models as competing conceptualizations of institutional change processes.
- Alternating macro (whole-program) and micro (individual-course) levels of analysis as a structuring device for curricular review.

- Resistance to change among faculty, differentiated by generational/career-stage position.
- Participatory governance and multi-stakeholder consultation in academic decision-making.
- Behavioral/relational outcomes of organizational change processes in academic departments (as distinct from structural/curricular outcomes).
- Benchmarking as a legitimation and gap-identification device in curriculum design.
- Structured conduct norms as a conflict-management mechanism in collegial governance.
- Accreditation and market-relevance pressure as external drivers of curricular change.
- Case study as an evidentiary basis for proposing generalizable process frameworks.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

Several tensions and assumptions can be identified within what the note reports, without appeal to information outside it.

First, there is a labeling tension: the process is described as "deductive" while also being described as heavily participative and iterative, incorporating extensive bottom-up stakeholder input (student/alumni surveys, faculty self-assessment, open-ended debate). A deductive process would typically proceed from established theoretical premises (e.g., the Garner and Buckley tripartite model) applied downward to curricular decisions; the process as described in the note, however, appears to combine that top-down structuring with substantial inductive, ground-level data gathering and negotiation. Whether the "deductive" label accurately characterizes the process as operationally described, or is better understood as a hybrid, is not resolved within the note.

Second, there is a tension between the study's claim to offer a "replicable" framework and its evidentiary basis: a single department, single institution, single two-year cycle, described through a descriptive case study without any reported comparison case or independent replication. The note does not report evidence, within this study, that the framework was successfully applied elsewhere; the replicability claim therefore rests on the authors' own characterization rather than on demonstrated cross-context application, as far as the note records.

Third, the positive relational/behavioral findings ("more honest relationships," "increased mutual understanding") are reported without a described independent measurement instrument or external evaluator; given that the faculty who underwent the process include or overlap with those reporting on its results, this raises a question — not resolved by the note — about the extent to which these findings reflect a structured evaluation versus a retrospective, participant-generated account of the process's success.

Fourth, the study designates student success and employability as the "final validation metric" for the curricular reform, yet the note does not report that such outcome data were collected or analyzed in this study; the authors' own suggested future work (long-term efficacy of institutionalized changes, post-reform learning assessment) implicitly acknowledges that this validation has not yet occurred. This creates a gap between the evaluative standard the authors articulate and the evidence the note reports as having actually been produced.

Fifth, the 49% response rate from 103 invited alumni/near-graduates, yielding 50 respondents, is treated in the findings as indicative of stakeholder demand (e.g., for more technology, logistics content, a textiles lab); the note does not report any assessment of whether the responding subgroup differed systematically from non-respondents, which is an assumption worth flagging when weighing how representative the identified "gaps" are of the broader student/alumni population.

## 17. Missing information

The note does not report: the number of faculty members involved in the review process; demographic or role-based composition of the survey respondents beyond the senior/alumni split; validation procedures (if any) for the survey instrument or the internal CIP self-assessment questionnaire; the specific statistical methods beyond "simple descriptive statistics"; how consensus among faculty was operationally determined or measured during the structured reflective sessions; whether the identified technology-integration and textiles-laboratory demands (as distinct from the logistics course and capstone redesign) were acted upon; any quantitative or qualitative data on student learning outcomes or graduate employability following the reform; the specific instruments or criteria used by the three-person industry panel to evaluate "Senior Market" student collections; and any account of dissenting faculty perspectives that were not resolved through the consensus process. The note also does not specify page-level or section-level detail on how the "deductive" characterization of the process is theoretically justified by the authors.
