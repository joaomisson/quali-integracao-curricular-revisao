# A04 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

The note reports that the study examines a four-year collaborative curriculum revision process jointly conducted by faculty and administrators at a U.S. higher-education institution of theological education (Ashland Theological Seminary, ATS). According to the note, the motivating problem is twofold: first, despite curriculum being considered fundamental to institutional well-being and effectiveness, "little attention has been given to the evolution of curriculum and its review and transformation in the institutions" (as quoted in the note's Trecho 1); second, the existing literature on curriculum governance is described as concentrating almost exclusively on the faculty's role, leaving a gap regarding shared governance between faculty and administrators in comprehensive curriculum reform processes. The note also states that radical curricular reforms are rare and demanding because they require significant time and broad active participation, which is described as colliding with the traditional culture of higher education — characterized by faculty individualism, extreme autonomy, and disciplinary fragmentation.

The note frames the study's relevance as filling this gap by empirically documenting a real four-year case of radical change, offering practical insight into how interdepartmental collaboration can be organized and how human and structural resistance can be addressed.

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note records the primary objective as investigating and deriving meaning from the experience of the phenomenon of comprehensive curriculum review at Ashland Theological Seminary, with a secondary objective of deepening understanding of the collaboration process between faculty and administration during the reform. Two research questions are explicitly listed in the note, framed from the perspective of a higher-education administrator:
1. How did the curriculum review team experience the comprehensive curriculum review process?
2. How did faculty and administration collaborate during this review process?

The note explicitly states that no formal hypotheses were formulated, consistent with what it describes as an exploratory, inductive qualitative study whose scope was to generate theory from field data.

## 3. Context investigated

The note specifies the empirical context as Ashland Theological Seminary (ATS), described as the graduate division of Ashland University in Ohio, USA, and characterized in the note as the largest seminary in the state. The object of study is a single four-year curriculum reform process within this institution.

## 4. Methodological approach

The note describes the study as a single qualitative case study guided by a Grounded Theory methodological perspective. Participants were 10 members of the curriculum review team (5 faculty and 5 administrators) who served throughout the four years; the note adds that the interviewee group included one individual who later became president of the institution.

Data collection, per the note, combined three sources: (1) in-depth semi-structured individual interviews, recorded and transcribed, averaging 1.5 hours each; (2) document analysis of emails, meeting minutes, and curriculum design materials produced during the reform; and (3) reflective records in the form of personal self-reflection journals kept by the principal researcher.

Analytic procedures are described as occurring simultaneously with data collection and coding, using the constant comparative method with theoretical sampling, structured into three coding stages: open coding (identifying, labeling, and categorizing initial concepts directly from emails and transcripts), axial coding (connecting categories and subcategories, analyzing their dimensions and properties, generating visual representations of themes), and selective coding (integrating categories around core concepts, narrowing the theory to five major variables/assertions).

The note records an explicit bias-control procedure: because one of the authors served as facilitator and chair of the reform, he recorded his own responses before the interviews and waited six months after the curriculum reform's conclusion before beginning interviews, in order to establish emotional distance.

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note lists five conceptual anchors used by the authors:
- Postmodern Curriculum characteristics (Doll, 1993; Slattery, 2006), understanding curriculum development as a non-linear, creative, fluid process centered on human relationships among participants ("curriculum-in-action"), grounded in communal cooperation, holistic vision, and interdisciplinarity.
- Complexity and Curriculum Reform (Burgess, 2004), distinguishing minor, isolated disciplinary changes from comprehensive curriculum reform, which requires understanding how parts interconnect and tolerating ambiguity and the emergence of self-organized, unplanned solutions.
- Reculturing vs. Restructuring (Fullan, 2001), positing that genuine educational reform requires changing the culture of how people act and think in the organization (reculturing), rather than merely altering course grids or syllabi (restructuring).
- Shared Governance (Mortimer & Sathre, 2007), positing curriculum as a corporate responsibility of the institution as a whole, requiring flexibility and active partnership among faculty, administrators, and boards.
- Communities of Practice (Bauman, 2005) and Team Learning Approach (Bensimon & Neumann, 1993), conceptualizing the review group as a community prioritizing collaborative learning and thinking-together over a purely task-technical focus.

## 6. Object being investigated

The object investigated, as recorded in the note, is the lived experience and collaborative dynamics of a ten-person mixed faculty-administrator team during a four-year comprehensive curriculum revision at a single theological seminary. The unit of analysis is the review team's process and its members' interpretations of that process, rather than the curriculum content itself or measurable student-learning outcomes.

## 7. Main findings

The note reports that data analysis consolidated into five main findings:
1. Shared Vision: collective construction of a guiding philosophical vision (centered on desired student learning outcomes) served as an indispensable foundation preceding any practical discussion of course syllabi.
2. Shared Responsibility: the perception that curriculum is a common corporate duty helped dissolve traditional boundaries between "faculty" and "administrative" domains, improving organizational empathy and securing administrative financial support for implementation.
3. Collaboration: the reform promoted intense inter- and intradepartmental exchange; some sectors integrated deeply because the process forced transparent dialogue about what each instructor actually taught.
4. Cultural Challenges: paradoxically, the same human dynamics that favored change also created impasses — faculty struggled to relinquish strict autonomy and disciplinary "territory wars"; there were questions about leadership of the process being held by an administrator; initial role clarity was lacking for half the team; and there was an absence of financial resources or teaching-load reduction at the project's outset.
5. Sense of Community and Connection: a strong sense of community and personal loyalty built among team members acted as the principal motivational fuel sustaining engagement through periods of high emotional tension and conflict.

## 8. Main arguments

According to the note, the authors argue that structured collaboration between different institutional groups enables organizational learning and makes deep change possible. They propose a grounded theory in which the team operated under a "community of practice" logic, adopting a posture of "mutual learning" rather than a purely task-oriented bureaucratic approach, with participants' motivation stemming from a desire to learn together and improve the seminary. The note records the authors' concluding argument that changing curricula is not an act of technical or structural engineering but an effort of reculturing, and that academic leaders must anticipate structural, communicative, and resource-related barriers in order to plan processes that respect the time needed for relationships to mature.

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note lists three categories of contribution attributed to the authors:
- Theoretical: an expansion of the concept of shared governance applied to comprehensive curricular change, demonstrating that reform need not be an exclusively faculty-driven process but can be a joint, co-managed responsibility with administration.
- Methodological: a detailed application of qualitative triangulation (interviews, document analysis, and reflective journals) combined with rigorous Grounded Theory coding stages, plus a practical model for reflexive bias control for administrators researching their own workplace.
- Practical: a structured list of tailored action recommendations for presidents/administrators, participating faculty, committee coordinators, and technical team members intending to conduct similar reforms.

## 10. Limitations

The note explicitly records three acknowledged limitations:
- Field-research bias: the principal researcher was himself the academic vice-president and chair of the reform committee, which could have distorted perceptions of the reform's success and of collaboration, though the note states this was mitigated by the emotional-distancing safeguards and the practice of recording responses in advance.
- Resource indefinition: the lack of planning for adequate institutional compensation (teaching-load reduction or direct financial compensation) overburdened faculty and made the effort dependent on their intrinsic motivation.
- Role uncertainty: half of the interviewees reported facing initial lack of clarity about their exact functions on the team and their representational role.

The note also records three suggested directions for future research: investigations focused exclusively on the cultural dynamics of barriers to educational collaboration; detailed study of the relationship between organizational learning and organizational change, particularly the role of belonging and community in committee productivity; and isolated evaluation of the role and influence of academic department heads in reform processes, given that they acted as both facilitators and, at times, obstacles to change.

## 11. Implications

The note's discussion and conclusions section frames curricular change as requiring reculturing rather than technical restructuring, implying that institutional leaders planning similar reforms should anticipate structural, communicational, and resource barriers and allow sufficient time for relational maturation among participants. The practical recommendations described in Section 8 of the note (targeted at presidents, faculty, committee coordinators, and technical staff) suggest that the study is oriented toward informing institutional practice for future comprehensive reforms, not only toward theory-building.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

- Single-case generalizability: the study is based on a single institution (a theological seminary) and a single reform process with 10 participants. The note does not indicate any claim of statistical generalizability, but the theoretical claims advanced (e.g., that curricular reform is inherently a matter of "reculturing" requiring shared governance) are stated in fairly general terms; the evidentiary base described in the note supports only an in-depth account of this one case.
- Insider-researcher role: the principal researcher was simultaneously the chair/facilitator of the reform being studied and an interviewer of the very team he led. Although the note describes mitigation steps (six-month wait, pre-recorded responses), this arrangement raises a structural tension between generating "grounded" theory from participants' voices and the researcher's own stake in narrating the reform as a success. The note itself acknowledges this as a limitation but does not report independent verification (e.g., external auditor, member-checking beyond what is described) of whether the mitigation fully addressed the risk of distorted interpretation.
- Success-oriented framing: the five findings as reported emphasize predominantly positive outcomes (shared vision, shared responsibility, collaboration, community/connection), with "Cultural Challenges" appearing as the sole finding centered on difficulty. Given the insider-researcher issue above, it is worth questioning whether the balance of positive-to-negative findings reflects the empirical reality of the four-year process or partly reflects the vantage point of a researcher invested in the reform's legitimacy. The note does not provide information to adjudicate this.
- Assumption that shared governance necessarily "dissolves" faculty-administrative boundaries: the note reports this as an authors' finding/interpretation ("Achado 2"), but does not describe what would count as evidence of durable boundary dissolution versus temporary situational cooperation limited to the reform period. Whether the reported empathy and boundary-dissolution persisted beyond the four-year project is not addressed in the note.
- Resource/compensation gap treated as a limitation rather than as a potential confound: the note frames the absence of financial resources or workload reduction as a limitation, yet the reform is still reported as succeeding via "intrinsic motivation" and communal bonding. This raises an unexamined tension in the note between celebrating community-driven success and flagging that the process was not institutionally resourced — a dynamic that could be read as sustainability risk rather than solely as a footnote limitation.
