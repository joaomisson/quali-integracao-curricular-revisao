# A13 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

According to the note, the study addresses large-scale, whole-of-program curriculum reform in higher education, specifically in accounting degree programs, examined through the leadership lens of a Program Director (an intermediate academic leadership role). The note frames the research problem as a chronic tension between the need to modernize higher education curricula and the structural barriers imposed by traditional university culture.

The note explicitly states that accounting curricula are historically criticized for being excessively technical, outdated relative to market digitalization, and isolated from related disciplines, while reform efforts face systemic resistance to change, slow bureaucratic processes, internal political disputes, and workload-allocation systems that reward pure research over pedagogical innovation. The note also records a claimed literature gap: that the scientific literature rarely details the practical process of integral curriculum reforms led by program directors under conditions of uncertainty and severe financial constraint.

The note further situates the study's relevance in financial pressures from reduced public funding, dependence on international student revenue, and the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, concurrent with new global and national professional accreditation guidelines. (EXPLICIT, per the note's "Tema e problema de pesquisa" section.)

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note records the objective as filling the literature gap by empirically detailing the process of reforming a full accounting program curriculum from the reflective perspective of a Program Director.

Two research questions are recorded verbatim in translated/paraphrased form in the note:
1. How do intermediate academic leaders conduct deep curriculum reforms to raise teaching quality under strong uncertainty and resource constraints in constantly changing environments?
2. How do these leaders navigate traditional university culture — individualism, risk aversion, internal political games, and hierarchical bureaucratic flows — to improve teaching practices?

The note explicitly states that no formal research hypotheses were formulated, attributing this to the qualitative, exploratory, and cooperative nature of the action research method applied. (EXPLICIT.)

## 3. Context investigated

The note specifies the setting as a single Australian university (referred to pseudonymously as "Australian University" / AU) undergoing corporate restructuring, mass layoffs, and severe budget cuts due to the pandemic crisis. The study period is recorded as October 2019 to May 2021 (18 months). The disciplinary context is accounting education within a business school. (EXPLICIT.)

## 4. Methodological approach

The note describes the methodology as collaborative action research combined with qualitative reflective practice, based on critical self-study narrated by the project's leader (the author herself, in her role as Program Director).

Participants and data, as recorded in the note:
- A 4-person steering committee (including two senior academics).
- A team of 17 accounting discipline faculty who systematically reviewed 11 accounting programs comprising 51 compulsory subjects.
- Subsequently, 13 discipline specialists who cooperated in redesigning 25 core compulsory subjects.
- Primary and secondary data sources: subject outlines, course structures, student surveys, institutional retention data, student focus-group interviews, employer interviews, national teaching guidelines, and the author's personal reflective diary maintained during the project.

The note describes the analytic procedure as reflective thematic analysis along two axes: (a) alignment of subjects with market/employability demands, and (b) internal consistency of pedagogical outlines, evaluated through the lens of Constructive Alignment (coupling of learning outcomes, assessments, and teaching practices). (EXPLICIT.)

The methodology is fundamentally autoethnographic/practitioner-based: the primary evidentiary instrument is the author's own reflective diary and her dual role as both change agent and researcher. This is a first-person, insider account of a single reform process rather than an externally observed or multi-case study. ## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note records four integrated theoretical/conceptual pillars:
- Reflective Practice and Transformative Learning, attributed to Jack Mezirow and Edward Taylor, holding that constant critical reflection allows educators to reinterpret experience and transform problematic scenarios into more effective teaching actions.
- Constructive Alignment (CA), attributed to John Biggs and Catherine Tang, concerning systematic harmony between intended learning outcomes (ILOs), teaching activities, and assessment methods.
- Action Research (AR) and Action Learning (AL), oriented toward solving real problems through continuous spirals of planning, action, observation, and joint reflection, drawing on David Kember, Ortrun Zuber-Skerritt, Harvey & Jones, and McGill & Beaty.
- Situational factors and adaptive leadership, drawing on L. Dee Fink's integrated course design models and contemporary adaptive leadership approaches for crisis periods.

(EXPLICIT, per the note's "Fundamentação" section.)

## 6. Object being investigated

The object of investigation, as recorded in the note, is the process of leading a whole-of-program curriculum reform — encompassing curriculum review, redesign, and bureaucratic approval — as experienced and narrated by the intermediate academic leader who drove it. The object is thus simultaneously the curriculum reform process itself and the leadership practice/reflective experience of conducting it. ## 7. Main findings

The note organizes findings into three phases:

**Phase 1 (Curriculum Review):** The note reports that the existence of 11 separate programs generated content redundancy and strong student confusion regarding pathway choice; a serious gap was mapped in the integration of digital technologies (e.g., data analytics and business intelligence tools) and critical professional competencies (critical thinking, ethical judgment, interpersonal communication skills); assessment systems were found to be misaligned and excessively limited, relying largely on multiple-choice tests (measuring low cognitive levels), take-home essays, and traditional closed written exams; prior curricular decisions were reportedly made "behind closed doors" for purely political leadership reasons, without grounding in concrete data.

**Phase 2 (Curriculum Redesign):** The note records the rationalization of 11 former courses into just 2 flagship programs, modernized, highly flexible, and containing interdisciplinary and work-integrated learning options; structured pedagogical redesign of 25 compulsory subjects; introduction of new business analytics subjects co-designed with IT specialists, data analysts, and market partners; creation by the author of a plain-language "Reference Guide" to enable subject specialists to define ILOs and assessments without technical jargon; diversification of assessment methods to include practical assessments, interactive simulations, and presentations alongside traditional written tests.

**Phase 3 (Bureaucratic Approval):** The note records submission of a total of 103 curriculum review documents, successfully approved by the institution's Academic Board, substantially exceeding the historical average of 9 submissions for the same period at the institution; this reportedly required approximately 200 hours of direct bureaucratic writing work by the author, on top of her regulated teaching duties. The note also records a consolidated organizational-culture change: destabilization/simplification ("desburocratização") of the internal peer-review rite within the business school, attributed to the author's feedback and advocacy.

(All EXPLICIT, per the note's "Principais resultados" section.)

## 8. Main arguments

The note records the author as arguing that:
- Real-world action research processes in unstable environments are profoundly complex, dynamic, overlapping, and non-linear, in contrast to the simplified cyclical schemas formally presented in the literature.
- The COVID-19 crisis, although traumatic and productive of work overload and faculty layoffs, functioned as a catalyst that broke habitual individual academic resistance, accelerating agile pedagogical innovations such as online teaching and flexible modern assessments.
- Traditional higher-education governance and bureaucratic approval structures are excessively slow, inflexible, and exhausting, functioning as an active disincentive for faculty to initiate teaching reforms because of the exhausting administrative workload involved.
- University leadership should support, protect, and value the Scholarship of Learning and Teaching and pedagogical design innovations in promotion decisions and workload allocation, which the author argues remain systematically obscured by favoritism toward conventional scientific research.

(EXPLICIT, per the note's "Discussão e conclusões" section — these are recorded as the author's claims/conclusions, not independently verified findings.)

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note records three categories of claimed contribution:
- Theoretical: empirically showing the effectiveness of systematically combining Biggs's Constructive Alignment model with Mezirow's Transformative Learning framework in the development of business education programs.
- Methodological: presenting a practical guide/model for collecting and triangulating internal and market data to restructure entire undergraduate programs, offering replicable guidelines for leaders in other disciplines seeking to use action research.
- Practical: consolidation of obsolete and misaligned courses into integrated, digital-data-analysis-focused degrees, combined with organizational gains from rationalizing and simplifying peer-review norms at the university.

## 10. Limitations

The note explicitly records the following limitations as stated by the author:
- Reliance on the researcher's own subjective reflection and self-study supported by her reflective diary, raising challenges common to qualitative, self-analytical case studies.
- The study context is restricted to one Australian university with specific vulnerabilities (high financial dependence on international students in business disciplines), limiting immediate generalization of the political obstacles found to institutions with public or mixed funding systems in other countries.
- The substantial bureaucratic stress led to a temporary sacrifice of the author's own disciplinary research.

The note also records that although the article lacks a dedicated future-work subsection, the author signals the relevance of longitudinal empirical follow-up studies to track whether renewed, technically/digitally focused curricula actually affect accounting graduates' labor-market insertion, and signals urgency for research on models to support, sustain, and retain university middle leaders, addressing their overload. (EXPLICIT.)

## 11. Implications

The note's discussion suggests several implications, framed as the author's arguments rather than independently established conclusions: that crisis conditions (e.g., pandemic disruption) can be leveraged as accelerants for institutional pedagogical change; that bureaucratic governance structures function as disincentives to teaching innovation and may need active simplification; and that institutional reward systems (promotion, workload allocation) should be restructured to value teaching-and-learning scholarship on par with conventional research. ## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

- The study's evidentiary base rests substantially on the author's own reflective diary and self-narrated experience as both the change agent and the researcher. This creates a structural risk of self-serving interpretation: the note records outcomes framed largely as successes (e.g., program rationalization, high documentation approval volume, peer-review simplification) without an apparent external or independent evaluative check recorded in the note.
- The claim that COVID-19 acted as a catalyst that "broke habitual resistances" is presented in the note as the author's interpretation; the note does not record independent evidence (e.g., faculty surveys measuring resistance before/after) substantiating this causal claim beyond the author's narrative.
- The note records that prior curricular decisions were made "behind closed doors" for political reasons — an assessment made by the same author who subsequently led the replacement process, raising a potential conflict of interest or self-legitimizing framing that the note does not address.
- The 103 approved documents versus a historical average of 9 is presented as a positive outcome (efficiency/success), but the note does not record any qualitative assessment of the review quality or rigor of that approval process, given the volume disparity — a potential tension between throughput and depth of institutional scrutiny.
- The generalizability of findings is explicitly limited by the note to one institution with specific financial vulnerabilities (international student dependence), yet the author's stated methodological contribution claims to offer "replicable guidelines for leaders of other disciplines" — a tension between the acknowledged contextual specificity and the broader methodological ambition claimed.
- The note does not clarify how "success" is defined or measured (e.g., student outcomes, employer satisfaction, graduate employability) beyond process-level indicators (documents approved, subjects redesigned); the promised future longitudinal study on graduate labor-market insertion suggests this outcome-level validation is explicitly absent from the current study.
