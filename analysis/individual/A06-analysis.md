# A06 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

According to the note, the study (Kandiko Howson & Kingsbury, 2023, "Curriculum change as transformational learning," *Teaching in Higher Education*) addresses systemic curriculum change in higher education, specifically how central strategic policy guidelines are translated, interpreted, and enacted by different departmental cultures and communities. The note frames the research problem as follows: despite growing regulatory pressures forcing universities to collect data and adopt large-scale reforms, the dynamics of how such centralized strategic policies are actually enacted in the everyday contexts of departments is poorly understood ("pouco compreendida"). The note states that evaluating large-scale curricular reforms is notoriously complex due to long maturation periods and the large number of variables involved, and that centralized policies risk remaining a "retórica formal em folhetos de marketing" (formal rhetoric in marketing brochures) without altering the cultural and teaching practices of the disciplines. This is a paraphrase of the note's own problem statement; only the quoted Portuguese expressions above ("pouco compreendida" and "retórica formal em folhetos de marketing") reproduce wording that appears verbatim in the note.

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note reports the study's objective as evaluating the degree of engagement and alignment of academic departments with the guidelines and the actual intent of a comprehensive institutional curricular reform policy, and capturing the impact of this change on disciplinary and institutional culture at the early stage of the reform process. The research question, as recorded in the note, investigates the extent to which departments at a research-intensive university transitioned from traditional curricula (based purely on disciplinary content transmission and high-stakes exams) toward active incorporation of active-learning approaches and broader institutional objectives into their own language, pedagogy, and practices.

## 3. Context investigated

The note specifies the empirical context as a highly decentralized ("highly devolved"), medium-sized, research-intensive institution in the United Kingdom. The study was conducted in the second year of a systemic curricular reform program supported by a nine-year investment plan. Fifteen undergraduate departments distributed across three distinct Faculties were independently evaluated based on their documentation. The note characterizes the analytic focus as the "planned/intended curriculum" ("currículo planejado/pretendido") derived from official documents at a specific time cut, rather than the curriculum as enacted in classrooms.

## 4. Methodological approach

The note describes the study as qualitative evaluative research adopting a linguistic ethnography approach and critical discourse analysis, structured as a longitudinal case study focused on planned/intended curriculum documentation. Three types of policy texts ("objetos de política") were cross-analyzed: (1) the Learning and Teaching Strategy, a centralizing document structured around four pillars — Assessment Reform, Active Learning, Diversity and Inclusion, and Digital Technologies; (2) Curriculum Redesign Forms, internal departmental documents justifying curricular changes and detailing their relation to the strategic pillars; and (3) Programme Specifications, contractually binding official documents directed at students and quality assurance, described in the note as functioning as a "pacto negociado" (negotiated pact).

The note reports that researchers developed an evaluation rubric assessing the four strategic pillars along three dimensions — Language (use of strategy terminology), Intent (incorporation of the spirit of the reforms), and Application (declared practical implementation) — applied to 12 indicators in the Redesign Forms and 16 indicators across four sections of the Programme Specifications, totaling 28 indicators. Two independent analyses rated each section on a categorical 1–5 scale (Absent, Vague, Implicit, Present, Explicit).

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note identifies several theoretical anchors:
- Transformational Learning Theory, drawing on Mezirow, Habermas, and Freire, used as the lens for conceptualizing corporate/institutional curriculum change as a "dilema desorientador" (disorienting dilemma), distinguishing deep structural transformations in "hábitos mentais" (habits of mind) from superficial formal adaptations ("pontos de vista").
- Policy Enactment, based on Ball et al., which analyzes policies not as instructions mechanically applied but as processes actively interpreted and recreated within institutional cultures by diverse actors.
- Curriculum conceptions drawing on Biggs' constructive alignment, Barnett and Coate's know-act-be framework, and Bernstein's framing of valid knowledge.
- Dynamics of curricular change balancing top-down centralized policy command and bottom-up local disciplinary engagement, described in the note through the terms "introjeção" (introjection) disciplinar and "projeção" (projection) of external demands.

## 6. Object being investigated

The object of investigation, as recorded in the note, is the documented (written, planned) curricular response of 15 undergraduate departments to an institution-wide strategic Learning and Teaching reform policy — specifically the textual traces of that response across strategy documents, departmental redesign forms, and programme specifications. The note is explicit that the object is the planned/intended curriculum as represented in official documents, not the curriculum as taught or experienced.

## 7. Main findings

The note reports the following principal findings:
- A "gap" between language and application: engagement with the technical language of reform was significantly higher than engagement with its intent or practical application. On average, there was a full categorical decline in rubric rating (e.g., from "Presente" to "Implícito") when moving from the linguistic level to concrete application across the four pillars.
- Unequal engagement across pillars: Assessment Reform and Active Learning pillars achieved high receptivity at the language level ("Presente" or "Explícita" in all departments), but practical execution was uneven — only about one-fifth of departments explicitly applied the guidelines, while others retained traditional structures based on high-stakes exams as the dominant method (with exams representing 70%–90% of final grades). Diversity and Inclusion and Digital Technologies pillars registered low adherence: Diversity and Inclusion terminology was absent in half of curricular proposals and rarely translated into classroom applications; the digital area was almost entirely omitted, historically treated as the responsibility of central technical support units rather than a departmental pedagogical responsibility.
- A three-cluster typology of departmental response emerged: Active departments (~1/3), which fused disciplinary identity and content with the strategic pillars and created specific assessment plans (e.g., Medicine, integrating social and health diversity into learning outcomes); Engaged departments (~1/3), which showed receptiveness through isolated, punctual innovations (e.g., Team-Based Learning in some modules) without deeply restructuring general teaching conceptions; and Passive departments (~1/3), which showed strictly bureaucratic compliance focused on regulatory adjustments (credits, module rearrangement), using reform terminology cosmetically ("mimetismo linguístico") for approval and funding access while maintaining an exclusive focus on traditional disciplinary content.

## 8. Main arguments

The note records the authors as arguing that linguistic incorporation of institutional terminology functions as a necessary but insufficient threshold ("Linguagem como Limiar") for mindset change, and that linguistic conformity even in passive departments may represent the beginning of a minimal contractual agreement enabling later reforms. The authors are recorded as arguing that there is a conflict between disciplinary identity and central structure: formulation of learning outcomes focused largely on disciplines' own content with little institutional influence, and that external regulatory forces from professional accreditation bodies (PSRBs) exert substantially more power over local curriculum definitions than central institutional policies. The note reports the authors' conclusion that, at systemic scale, curriculum review operates as a disorienting dilemma stimulating transformational learning, but that real and sustainable pedagogical reform occurs only when institutional objectives are fused into each discipline's own pedagogy and epistemology through the action of disciplinary specialists and translators.

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note lists three self-identified contributions:
- Theoretical: an unprecedented (per the note, "inédito") application of Mezirow's Transformational Learning Theory to institutional governance and higher-education policy, explaining processes of resistance and reception of curricular innovations.
- Methodological: development and validation of a systematic evaluation instrument (a 28-indicator multi-criteria rubric) applicable for other universities to monitor and critically analyze their own curricular change processes.
- Practical: demonstration that reforms do not occur in a purely vertical (top-down) fashion, and identification of the role of "tradutores pedagógicos" (pedagogical translators) — such as educational designers, technologists, and disciplinary pedagogy specialists — in bridging central goals and disciplinary specificities.

## 10. Limitations

The note explicitly records the following limitations as stated by the authors: the linguistic-ethnography design studies exclusively written representations in formal documents, without directly observing the curriculum as enacted in classrooms, informal teaching dynamics, or informal modifications over time; there is a possibility of "political linguistic mimicry" ("mimetismo linguístico político"), where departments use the correct keywords cosmetically to secure promised additional funding or simply to satisfy formal bureaucratic requirements; and the study represents a time-bound, situated snapshot analysis ("análise pontual e situada temporalmente"). The note also records the authors' stated future-research agenda: investigating continued practical execution, the transition into the classroom, and impacts on actual student learning outcomes, as well as evaluating how additional external disruptive factors (e.g., the Covid-19 pandemic or the Black Lives Matter movement) may have impacted or accelerated the diversity and technology guidelines initially mapped.

## 11. Implications

Based on the note, the study implies that institutional curricular reform monitoring should distinguish between rhetorical/linguistic adoption and substantive practical/intentional adoption, since these can diverge systematically and by a full evaluative category. It also implies, per the note's discussion, that professional/regulatory bodies (PSRBs) may be more consequential drivers of local curriculum content than internal institutional strategy, and that institutions seeking substantive reform may need to invest in intermediary roles (pedagogical translators) rather than relying on top-down directives alone. These are the note's own discussion-and-conclusion statements; no further implications beyond what is recorded should be assumed.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

Several assumptions underlying the study's design and claims, as recorded in the note, merit scrutiny. First, the study's evidentiary base is exclusively formal written documentation (strategy documents, redesign forms, programme specifications); the note itself flags this as a limitation, since it cannot observe classroom enactment, informal practices, or informal changes over time. This raises a tension between the study's strong empirical claims about a "gap" between language and application and the fact that "application" itself is measured only as declared/documented application, not observed practice — meaning the measured "application" score may itself be another form of textual representation subject to the same mimicry risk identified for "language."

Second, the rubric-based methodology assumes that a five-point categorical scale (Absent, Vague, Implicit, Present, Explicit), rated by two independent analysts, can reliably capture qualitative differences in "intent" — a notably interpretive and arguably more subjective construct than "language" (presence of terms) or declared "application." The note does not report inter-rater reliability statistics or resolution procedures for rating disagreements, which would be relevant to assessing the rubric's robustness.

Third, the three-way typology (Active/Engaged/Passive) partitions departments into roughly equal thirds; the note does not indicate whether this even distribution is a substantive empirical finding or partly an artifact of how cluster boundaries were defined, since no explicit clustering methodology (e.g., statistical clustering criteria) is described in the note beyond qualitative categorization.

Fourth, the claim that PSRB (professional, statutory, and regulatory body) accreditation exerts more influence over curricula than central institutional policy is a strong comparative claim; the note does not describe how this comparison was operationalized or measured, beyond appearing in the discussion section, so its evidentiary basis relative to the main rubric findings is unclear.

## 17. Missing information

The note does not specify: the exact name or discipline scope of the university studied (anonymized as a research-intensive, highly devolved UK institution); the specific 15 departments beyond the example of Medicine; the inter-rater reliability or agreement statistics between the two independent raters; the precise procedure by which departments were sorted into the Active/Engaged/Passive clusters (e.g., whether based on aggregate rubric scores, qualitative judgment, or a defined threshold); sample excerpts or examples for the Diversity and Inclusion or Digital Technologies pillars comparable to the Medicine example given for Active departments; any statistical significance testing associated with the reported "full category drop" pattern; and details of how the study defines or bounds "the second year" of the nine-year reform program in relation to the document snapshot analyzed. The note also does not report whether the study included any interview, survey, or observational data beyond the three document types, which would be necessary to assess claims about actors' motivations (e.g., attributing "cosmetic" language use to funding-seeking behavior).
