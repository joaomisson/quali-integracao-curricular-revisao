# A02 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

According to the note, the article addresses curriculum mapping and curricular revision in medical education. The note records that curriculum mapping is described as a consolidated tool for identifying where and how frequently competencies, knowledge, and attitudes are taught and assessed within an educational program. The note reports a critical gap identified in the literature the authors draw on: although mapping successfully identifies curricular gaps and redundancies, institutions reportedly face serious difficulties in planning and implementing practical, actionable strategies to fill those gaps. The note states that prior studies frequently recommend revisions or reforms without detailing how, or at which specific points in the curriculum, interventions and assessments should be introduced.

The specific problem investigated, per the note, is how to fill an unexpected gap identified in prognosis/prognostication skills within the undergraduate medical curriculum at the University of Virginia (UVA), using a structured approach.

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note describes the main objective as documenting how the authors identified and filled a curricular gap related to prognostication skill in the UVA School of Medicine undergraduate program, using the Theory of Change (ToC) model to establish a conceptual framework and a transparent process for curricular revision.

The note explicitly states that the study does not formulate formal hypotheses or research questions in the traditional hypothesis-testing format. Instead, it investigates resolving a mismatch between the intended (planned/written) curriculum, the delivered (taught/tested) curriculum, and the experienced (learned) curriculum, in relation to Competency No. 11 ("Formulate a prognosis...").

## 3. Context investigated

The note situates the study within a single institution: the undergraduate medical education program at the University of Virginia School of Medicine. The process was reportedly led by a three-person committee composed of the Director of Curricular Integration and Development, the Associate Dean for Curriculum, and the Senior Advisor for Educational Affairs.

The curricular context analyzed spans three phases, per the note: Phase 1 (pre-clinical, 18 months, approximately 6,800 session-level learning objectives across 13 disciplines); Phase 2 (clinical clerkships, 16 required clerkships, 781 aggregated objectives); and Phase 3 (post-clerkship, 63 courses including 17 advanced clinical electives, 642 objectives). The mapping process is recorded as having begun in 2019, using the institution's custom learning management system, VMed.

## 4. Methodological approach

The note characterizes the study type as a descriptive case report, focused on curricular development and continuous quality improvement (CQI).

The methodology described in the note comprises several steps: (1) mapping session-level objectives to discipline-level objectives (an intermediate layer of 20-25 objectives per discipline) and subsequently to the program's 12 Competency Goals (the "Twelve Competencies of the Contemporary Physician"), all cataloged and analyzed via VMed; (2) a targeted database search for the term "prognosis" and for Competency No. 11, supplemented by searches for synonyms such as "trajectory of illness" and "goals of care" to rigorously map the theme's presence across all phases; (3) application of the Theory of Change model to structure the response to the identified gap, including mobilizing resources (existing curricular time, assessment materials such as written exams, performance evaluations, OSCEs, and academic leadership), designing interventions (restructuring objectives in Internal Medicine clerkship and Phase 3 required disciplines), and tracking short- and medium-term outcomes.

The note does not describe any comparison group, control condition, or externally validated outcome measure beyond the mapping counts and the authors' own account of implementation.

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note identifies four conceptual pillars underlying the work:

1. Curriculum mapping, conceptualized as a method to align the planned curriculum with the program's general educational objectives and to ensure continuous quality improvement and accreditation compliance.
2. Theory of Change (ToC), described as a design and evaluation model focused on complex systems (such as undergraduate medical education), characterized by working backwards from a long-term goal to establish a logical sequence of intermediate outcomes (short- and medium-term), resources, interventions, and underlying assumptions.
3. The intended/delivered/experienced curriculum model, described as a theoretical basis recognizing that curricular misalignments occur when there is a lack of synchrony between instructors' objectives, the lessons and assessments actually delivered, and students' perceptions of their own learning.
4. Bloom's Taxonomy, used specifically to ensure new curricular objectives were written at higher cognitive levels (analysis, evaluation, creation) rather than focusing only on simple recall.

## 6. Object being investigated

The object investigated, as recorded in the note, is the process of curricular gap identification and remediation itself — specifically, the prognostication competency (Competency No. 11) within the UVA undergraduate medical curriculum, treated as a case study in applying the Theory of Change model to curricular revision. The unit of analysis is institutional/programmatic (learning objectives across curriculum phases), not individual learners or a comparative sample of institutions.

## 7. Main findings

The note reports the following findings:

- Diagnostic phase (2018–2019): In Phase 1, competencies related to the science of medicine, exam interpretation, and diagnosis (Competencies 2, 6, 9, and 10) were predominant, while there was an extreme scarcity ("paucity") of objectives dedicated to formulating prognoses (Competency 11).
- In Phase 2, of 781 objectives analyzed across 16 required clerkships, only 7 objectives directly referenced formulating a patient's prognosis.
- In Phase 3, of 642 learning objectives across 63 disciplines, only 1 objective mapped to the prognostication competency.
- The note states that these quantitative data empirically confirmed the existence of a real gap in the planned/written curriculum.
- Following application of the ToC model, the note reports successful filling of the maps across all three curriculum phases, and that student performance assessments in Phases 2 and 3 were modified to formally include evaluation of prognostication ability.
- The note records the authors' interpretation that institutional evaluations by students and faculty had never flagged the topic as deficient (suggesting the "experienced curriculum" was adequate in clinical practice), while the "planned curriculum" (written) seriously failed to document or evidence this training — illustrating that curricular misalignment can be invisible without rigorous mapping.

## 8. Main arguments

The note presents the authors as arguing that the Theory of Change model offers substantial advantages for complex curricular reforms: it forces explicit articulation of hidden assumptions, supports selection of multiple systemic interventions (as opposed to superficial quick "patches"), improves transparent communication with stakeholders, and functions as a roadmap of clear temporal milestones for evaluating progress. The note also records the argument that this process helps mitigate inertia and natural resistance to change in medical schools.

A further argument recorded in the note is that prior curricular gap-analysis literature tends to stop at diagnosis (identifying gaps/redundancies) without proposing actionable solutions, and that this study's contribution is to document a transition from diagnosis to systematic, resolutive intervention.

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note describes three types of contribution:

- Practical: a concrete intervention roadmap for medical schools to structure clinical disciplines around prognostication competencies, with Bloom's-taxonomy-based objectives.
- Methodological: a demonstration of the practical utility of the Theory of Change model applied to curricular development, with the note stating that the study "proves" the reverse, systematic approach is effective for breaking down complex educational problems.
- Theoretical: an expansion of the medical education literature by presenting a documented case of transition from diagnosis (gap mapping) to systematic resolutive intervention, described in the note as overcoming the limitations of traditional curricular reports that identify problems without proposing actionable solutions.

## 10. Limitations

The note records two declared limitations:

1. The diagnostic analysis focused exclusively on the presence or absence of written learning objectives, without auditing the exams and assessments actually administered during the initial period to check whether the topic was assessed "outside" the written objectives. The note adds the authors' mitigating claim that UVA policy requires assessments to be tied to objectives, making it unlikely that frequent formal assessments occurred without a corresponding planned objective.
2. Single institutional scope: the study reports the reform results and experience of only one educational institution (University of Virginia School of Medicine).

The note also records two future-work directions proposed by the authors: integrating prognostication into OSCEs (not yet accomplished) and encouraging educators at other health-sciences schools to test and use the Theory of Change approach for managing curricular reforms and correcting misalignments at different scales and on different topics.

## 11. Implications

Based on the note, the study implies that curriculum mapping alone is insufficient for curricular improvement unless paired with a structured framework for translating identified gaps into actionable interventions. The note frames the Theory of Change model as a candidate general-purpose methodology for this translation step, applicable in principle beyond the specific prognostication case, though the note itself only documents its application within one institution.

The note also implies — through the authors' interpretation of the disconnect between "experienced" and "planned" curricula — that reliance on student/faculty satisfaction evaluations alone may be an unreliable signal of curricular adequacy, since such evaluations did not surface the prognostication gap that systematic mapping later revealed.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

- The note reports the authors' claim that quantitative counts of learning objectives (7/781 in Phase 2; 1/642 in Phase 3) "empirically confirmed" a real gap in the planned curriculum. This treats objective-count scarcity as a direct proxy for educational inadequacy; however, the note's own first declared limitation acknowledges that written objectives may not capture everything actually taught or assessed, which sits in tension with the strength of the word "confirmed."
- The authors' rebuttal to that limitation — that UVA policy requires assessments to be tied to objectives, making unaccounted teaching "unlikely" — is, per the note, a policy-based inference rather than an empirical audit of actual exams and assessments. This is an assumption worth flagging: institutional policy compliance is asserted, not independently verified in the note.
- The note frames student/faculty satisfaction evaluations as never having flagged the prognostication topic as deficient, and interprets this as evidence that the "experienced curriculum" was adequate "in clinical practice." An alternative reading not addressed in the note is that satisfaction/evaluation instruments may simply not have been designed to probe this specific competency, which would be a measurement-instrument limitation rather than evidence of curricular adequacy — the note does not distinguish between these two explanations.
- The methodological and theoretical contributions described in the note (e.g., the claim that the study "proves" the ToC approach's reverse, systematic approach is effective) rest on a single documented case at a single institution. The note's own second declared limitation (single institutional scope) sits in tension with the stronger contribution language ("demonstrates," "proves") used elsewhere in the note.
- The note does not indicate whether the improved learning-objective counts and modified performance assessments (the reported "outcomes") were followed by any measurement of actual student prognostication competency, faculty teaching behavior change, or patient-care outcomes — the reported success is framed at the level of curricular documentation and assessment design changes, not demonstrated learning gains.

## 17. Missing information
