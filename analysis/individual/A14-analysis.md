# A14 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

The note describes a study centered on the social and scientific process of redesigning a single elective higher-education curriculum focused on sustainability competencies — the Transfaculty Cross-Section Program on Sustainable Development (TQNE) at the University of Basel, Switzerland. According to the note, the study's stated problem is that, although numerous case studies describe Higher Education for Sustainable Development (HESD) programs and their outcomes, there is a "blind spot" in the literature regarding the process itself — the social, didactic, and learning process that leads to curricular revision and improvement. The note also reports that current HESD discourse lacks practical procedures for handling challenges specific to elective programs, such as extreme interdisciplinary heterogeneity among students and the limited contact time instructors have with them. (EXPLICIT)

The stated relevance of the problem, per the note, is that universities are continuously challenged to implement curricular quality improvements that translate complex theory into practice under real institutional constraints of time and personnel. (EXPLICIT)

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note reports a general objective: to present and critically evaluate an approach for redesigning HESD study programs, with specific focus on the role of a temporary scientific support team mediating, facilitating, and complementing the activities of academic decision-makers, treating curricular redesign itself as a process of social learning. (EXPLICIT)

The note explicitly states that the study does not present formal quantitative hypotheses or rigidly numbered classical research questions. Instead, it investigates how scientific support can integrate teachers' experience-based practical knowledge with the solid conceptual foundations of HESD research, helping actors reach a realistic compromise between existing conditions and the theoretical ideal scenario. (EXPLICIT)

## 3. Context investigated

The empirical context is a single elective (non-mandatory) transfaculty program on sustainable development at the University of Basel, funded by the "Sustainable Development at Universities" program (2013–2016), per the note. (EXPLICIT) The governance structure separated a redesign team (local decision-makers) from a scientific support team composed of two HESD specialists external to the program. (EXPLICIT)

The note also references a comparative reference context: telephone interviews with directors of 8 similar sustainability elective programs in Switzerland and Germany, used as external benchmarking data during the diagnostic phase. (EXPLICIT) This suggests the study's primary empirical basis remains a single institutional case, with the multi-program interviews functioning as supplementary reference material rather than a comparative case-study design in their own right. (DERIVED — based on the note's framing of the external interviews as one of several diagnostic data streams feeding into a single redesign process, rather than as an independent multi-site outcome comparison.)

## 4. Methodological approach

The note characterizes the study as action research (pesquisa-ação) conducted through the actual redesign of the TQNE program. Data collection is described in four components: (1) retrospective analysis of 8 years of accumulated structured student evaluation questionnaires (5–7 datasets per course, ranging from 6–90 responses, average 34, with open and closed questions synthesized to categorize practical problems); (2) semi-structured interviews (~30 minutes) with 9 local instructors; (3) 1-hour telephone interviews with directors of 8 comparable external programs plus document analysis, processed through computer-assisted Qualitative Content Analysis (MAXQDA) using a two-dimensional matrix crossing practical challenges against curricular design elements; and (4) a formative pilot evaluation of the redesigned curriculum via non-participant observation (with an observation protocol focused on theme and active learning), semi-structured interviews with the 4 main instructors, student questionnaires (72–100% response rate), and a focus group with 7 students who completed the full program within one year. (EXPLICIT)

The methodology combines diagnostic qualitative content analysis with an intervention/action phase and a subsequent formative evaluation, consistent with an action-research design alternating academic inquiry and applied curricular decision-making, as the note's theoretical section also states. (EXPLICIT) The note does not specify inter-rater reliability procedures, sampling rationale for the 9 interviewed instructors relative to the total teaching staff, or statistical treatment of the questionnaire data beyond descriptive synthesis. (UNSUPPORTED)

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note lists multiple conceptual anchors: (a) the HESD literature on sustainability competencies and pedagogical approaches (citing de Haan, Wiek, Rieckmann, Lozano, Sipos, per the note); (b) curriculum as a "contextualized social process," attributed to Cornbleth (1988), defining program-level curriculum as an ongoing negotiation and decision-making process among actors under concrete institutional conditions; (c) a process-oriented program development approach emphasizing participation, structured planning, and mutual acceptance of the curriculum as the primary guide for local actors' practice; (d) action research as the methodological alignment guiding systematic alternation between academic research and practical program development; (e) transdisciplinarity in curricular change, involving direct collaboration between certified HESD specialists and non-certified local instructors with practical course experience; and (f) the "teaching choreographies" theory of Oser and Baeriswyl (2001), used to rethink selection of teaching-learning methods ("micromethods") linked to students' mental processes. (EXPLICIT)

The theoretical apparatus is explicitly pluralistic — the study does not appear to rely on a single dominant theory but assembles curriculum-as-social-process, action research, transdisciplinarity, and teaching-choreography theory into a composite framework. (DERIVED — based on the note listing six distinct conceptual sources without indicating a hierarchical or single primary theory.)

## 6. Object being investigated

The object of investigation is the process of curricular redesign itself — not the curriculum's content or outcomes as static objects, but the social, didactic, and decision-making process by which a specific HESD elective program is diagnosed, redesigned, and piloted, together with the role and instruments of a scientific support team within that process. (EXPLICIT, derived directly from the stated theme: "processo social e científico de redesenho curricular")

## 7. Main findings

The note reports diagnostic findings: students and instructors identified a lack of a unifying framework for topic selection and unclear links between individual courses and global sustainability; instructors were unaware of what colleagues taught in their own classrooms; and classroom heterogeneity created difficulties in calibrating the depth of presentations. (EXPLICIT)

The scientific support team created two structuring devices: a meso-level "Briefing Note" documenting which macro-level decisions needed resolution, alternatives adopted by partner universities, and impacts of each decision against HESD criteria; and a micro-level "Pedagogical Workshop" aimed at raising individual instructors' awareness and capability in applying active/reflective learning "micromethods" adapted to their disciplinary content. (EXPLICIT)

Reported redesign outcomes include: centralizing learning objectives on analytical skills and critical reflection on sustainability topics (prioritizing critical understanding over practical facilitation of complex processes); creation of a unified, cross-cutting annual focus theme (initiated with "Food and Sustainability"); implementation of a joint kick-off event using team teaching to present the interconnected relevance of the theme; and structuring an integrative block course centered on active group dynamics. (EXPLICIT)

Pilot evaluation results indicated the kick-off event successfully unified focus and provided transparency to students, and the annual theme enabled instructors to make cross-references and update scientific content more effectively. However, the note reports that the final integrated block course partially failed its consolidation goal because of difficulty finding an instructor prepared for interdisciplinary teaching who had not been an active part of the social curricular design process. (EXPLICIT)

## 8. Main arguments

The note reports that the authors interpret curricular redesign as occurring permanently in an "area of tension" between the empirically and theoretically desirable ("optimal") curriculum and the rigid reality bounded by institutional structural barriers (instructor time resources, historical path dependencies, financial limits). (EXPLICIT)

The authors reportedly argue that scientific support functions not as a technocratic imposition of inaccessible theoretical models but similarly to a "coach": its primary function is to make this area of conflict explicit through neutral data so that local actors can decide participatorily and consciously, achieving a realistic, broadly supported, and practically executable compromise. (EXPLICIT)

The note records three pillars the authors propose as underlying successful HESD curricular transformation: (1) rigorous connection to instructors' prior local practices and experiences; (2) preservation of multiple didactic frameworks, avoiding dogmatic imposition of a single pedagogical line; and (3) constant opening of environments conducive to dialogue and interdisciplinary consensus-building. (EXPLICIT)

These arguments collectively position curricular reform less as an application of an external "correct" model and more as a negotiated, facilitated social process bounded by institutional realism — a normative-methodological stance rather than a purely empirical finding, since the "coach" framing and the three pillars appear to be interpretive conclusions the authors draw from their single-case action-research intervention rather than independently validated causal claims. (DERIVED — based on the fact that the note presents these as "Discussão e Conclusões" drawn from one case's diagnostic and pilot data, without described comparative or counterfactual testing.)

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note lists three self-described contributions: theoretical (reducing the literature gap by scientifically detailing the dynamic process of didactic restructuring itself, moving from purely theoretical competency debates to theorizing real organizational and pedagogical change dynamics); methodological (designing and demonstrating operational validity of practical knowledge-transposition instruments — the meso-level briefing note and micro-level teaching-learning workshops — within university action research); and practical (providing a structured, feasible, and replicable redesign model respecting resource scarcity of small university programs through "outsourcing" of educational market-research-type tasks). (EXPLICIT)

## 10. Limitations

The note explicitly records the authors' own acknowledgment that, as a single-case action-research study (University of Basel), the immediate empirical findings have restricted generalizability to highly similar contexts. (EXPLICIT) It also reports that lack of time and human resources continued to be cited by local instructors as a limitation on deeper implementation of active methods within individual courses. (EXPLICIT) Additionally, delegating the integrative block course to an instructor who had not participated in the initial workshops proved to be a critical and vulnerable point of execution. (EXPLICIT)

The note states the article does not present a structured, dedicated section on future work, though its analyses reportedly point to the importance of future research on the temporal sustainability of these programs' constructive alignment, the long-term developmental impact on educators exposed to transdisciplinary didactic advising, and ways to mitigate integration barriers for substitute or newly appointed instructors. (EXPLICIT)

Beyond what the authors themselves state, the note does not describe how the qualitative content analysis's coding matrix was validated, whether findings from the 9 interviewed instructors were checked against the full instructor population, or what criteria were used to judge the pilot "successful" versus "partially failed" beyond the specific block-course issue. (UNSUPPORTED)

## 11. Implications

Per the note, the study implies that curricular reform processes benefit from a mediating, non-technocratic support function that surfaces institutional and empirical tensions through neutral data rather than prescribing solutions, and that instruments such as briefing notes and pedagogical workshops can operationalize this mediating role in resource-constrained settings. (EXPLICIT, following from the "Contribuições" and "Discussão e Conclusões" content)

A further implication suggested by the block-course finding is that participatory exposure to the redesign process itself may be a necessary condition for instructors to enact the resulting pedagogical changes coherently — meaning the curricular artifact (e.g., an integrative course) may not be transferable independent of the social process that produced it. (DERIVED — based on the explicit finding that assigning the integrative block to a non-participating instructor was "risky and time consuming" and produced partial failure, as quoted in the note.)

## 12. Concepts relevant across the corpus

- Curriculum conceived as a "contextualized social process" (Cornbleth, 1988) rather than a static document — a distinction between curriculum-as-plan and curriculum-as-ongoing-negotiation.
- The notion of a "blind spot" in HESD literature regarding process (as opposed to outcomes) of curricular change.
- Scientific/academic support in a "coach" role versus a technocratic/top-down expert role — a distinction between imposing models and facilitating locally-owned decisions.
- The "area of tension" (or area of conflict) between the theoretically/empirically "optimal" curriculum and the institutionally constrained "existing" curriculum.
- Meso-level (briefing note) and micro-level (pedagogical workshop/teaching micromethods) instruments as differentiated levels of intervention in curricular change.
- Teaching choreographies theory (Oser and Baeriswyl, 2001) linking micromethod selection to students' mental processes.
- Transdisciplinary collaboration between certified specialists and non-certified practitioners (local instructors) as a mode of knowledge integration.
- The risk of entrusting significant pedagogical change to actors who were not part of the participatory process that generated it.

## 13. Candidate themes/codes

- Process versus outcome focus in HESD/curriculum research.
- Scientific/academic support roles in institutional change (coach vs. technocrat).
- Action research as a method for simultaneous diagnosis and intervention.
- Institutional constraints (time, resources, path dependency) as bounding conditions for reform.
- Participatory design and its link to implementation fidelity.
- Interdisciplinary/transdisciplinary heterogeneity as a pedagogical challenge in elective programs.
- Instruments of knowledge transposition (briefing notes, workshops) as boundary objects between research and practice.
- Unifying thematic framing (annual cross-cutting theme) as a curricular integration device.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

The study frames curricular redesign success partly through its own pilot evaluation, conducted by the same team that designed and implemented the interventions (the scientific support team and redesign team), raising a question about the independence of the evaluation from the intervention it assesses; the note does not indicate any external or independent evaluation mechanism. (DERIVED — based on the note's description of the same action-research team conducting both the redesign and the formative pilot evaluation, without mention of an independent evaluator.)

The claim that the "coach" role of scientific support avoids technocratic imposition rests on the authors' own characterization of their intervention; the note does not report instructor or student perceptions specifically evaluating whether the support team was experienced as non-imposing, non-technocratic, or genuinely neutral in presenting "empirical data." (DERIVED — the note describes the authors' stated philosophy of practice via the quoted premises, but the evaluative interview/questionnaire data reported concerns satisfaction with the kick-off event and thematic focus, not explicit assessment of the support team's role perception.)

The three "pillars" of success (connection to prior practice, preservation of multiple didactic frameworks, openness to dialogue) are presented as general conclusions but are derived from a single institutional case; the note does not indicate whether these pillars were tested against alternative or contrasting cases within the same study. (EXPLICIT limitation already noted by the authors regarding generalizability, per the note.)

The partial failure of the integrative block course, attributed to the non-participating instructor's lack of exposure to the redesign process, could alternatively be explained by other unreported factors (e.g., instructor teaching style, subject matter difficulty, timing); the note attributes the failure specifically to lack of participatory involvement without describing how alternative explanations were ruled out. (DERIVED — based on the note's single causal narrative for the block-course outcome without discussion of competing explanations.)
