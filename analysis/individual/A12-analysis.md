# A12 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

The note frames the study as an investigation into the process of *initiating curriculum revision* in higher education, examined through the lens and practice of educational developers (referred to in the note as "assessores pedagógicos"/"desenvolvedores educacionais"). According to the note, the motivating problem is a gap between abundant theoretical literature on curriculum development and design and a scarcity of practice-oriented literature on how to actually begin a revision process — a gap that affects both academic staff and novice educational developers. The note explicitly states that reviewing an existing curriculum is described as more intimidating and difficult than designing one from scratch, and that educational developers often work in isolation, come from varied disciplinary backgrounds, and lack "role models" or theory-to-practice guides for this specific activity. EXPLICIT (note, item 2).

The relevance argument given in the note is that, in a dynamic higher-education landscape, many academics are required to revise curricula without prior experience and turn to educational developers who may themselves lack empirical reference points for how to approach this initial mediating stage. EXPLICIT (note, item 2).

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note states the main objective as exploring the practices of experienced educational developers from Ireland and the UK at the moment of initiating curriculum revision, and lists two guiding questions: (1) what approaches these professionals adopt, specifically, when initiating curriculum revision; and (2) what factors influence these practices. EXPLICIT (note, item 3).

## 3. Context investigated

The study context, as recorded in the note, is higher education in Ireland and the United Kingdom. Phase 1 interview participants were six experienced educational developers active in Irish higher education, some with prior international experience; Phase 2 questionnaire respondents were drawn from the professional networks SEDA (UK) and EDIN (Ireland), with the 22 returned questionnaires coming from Ireland (10), the UK (10), the Netherlands (1), and South Africa (1). EXPLICIT (note, item 5). The note does not specify institution types (e.g., research-intensive vs. teaching-focused), disciplinary settings of the academic staff being advised, or the historical/policy period in which the fieldwork occurred (only that literature references extend to 2009). UNSUPPORTED.

## 4. Methodological approach

The note describes a sequential mixed-methods design in two phases. Phase 1 (qualitative) involved six purposively ("amostragem seletiva") selected experienced educational developers, using in-depth, unstructured qualitative interviews prioritizing natural dialogue flow; data were recorded, coded with assistance of the Annotape software package, grouped into categories via constant comparison, and credibility-checked with another educational developer. Phase 2 (quantitative/qualitative) involved a semi-structured postal questionnaire (open and closed questions) sent to 74 educational developers in the SEDA and EDIN networks, yielding 22 completed returns (30% response rate); analysis consisted of descriptive frequency analysis of starting-point choices (reported in a Table 1) plus qualitative analysis of open responses. EXPLICIT (note, item 5).

The note's synthesis (item 11) refers to "28 desenvolvedores educacionais experientes" as the combined population studied, which is consistent with summing the six Phase 1 interviewees and the 22 Phase 2 questionnaire respondents. DERIVED (arithmetic consistency between item 5's reported sub-sample sizes and item 11's aggregate figure). However, the note does not clarify whether the six Phase 1 interviewees were a subset of, or entirely separate from, the 22 Phase 2 respondents; if they were the same individuals, treating the total as 28 distinct participants would be inaccurate. UNSUPPORTED (the note does not state whether phase-1 and phase-2 samples overlap).

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note lists several explicit theoretical anchors: (a) a product vs. process contrast in curriculum models — the product model (plans, transparent objectives, means-ends logic, attributed to Tyler and Popham & Baker, and linked to the Bologna Process in Europe) versus the process model (activities, flexibility, creativity, student experience, attributed to Stenhouse and Knight); (b) Stark's (2000) two-phase curriculum-planning "filters" model, involving exploration of staff beliefs/considerations followed by filtering of student and contextual influences; (c) Diamond's (1998) approach to needs assessment; (d) Toohey's (2000) emphasis on debating staff beliefs, values, and pedagogical ideologies before fixing goals and content; (e) Biggs's (2003) constructive alignment, linking learning objectives to teaching and assessment activities; (f) Land's (2004) ecological model of educational developer professional orientations, specifically the "internal consultant" and "interpretative-hermeneutic" orientations, emphasizing situated cognition, attentive listening, and departmental context over managerial postures. EXPLICIT (note, item 4).

## 6. Object being investigated

The primary object of investigation, as recorded in the note, is the practices and approaches of educational developers at the point of initiating curriculum revision — i.e., professional practice rather than curriculum content or outcomes per se. A secondary, closely linked object is the set of contextual and dispositional factors that shape those practices. EXPLICIT (derived directly from the stated objective and research questions in item 3). The note does not indicate that the study examined curricula themselves (their content, structure, or quality) as an object; it is explicitly restricted to the initiation stage of the revision process, not later implementation or evaluation. EXPLICIT (note, item 9, describing this restriction as a limitation).

## 7. Main findings

The note reports the following main results:

- Successful initiation requires intensive preliminary dialogue; developers report needing to listen carefully and question staff about the real motivators for change; academics rarely request help with "the curriculum as a whole" but tend to focus on specific pain points (content overload, assessment). Key questions cited include "Por que você está fazendo mudanças?" and "O que os estudantes precisam saber?" (paraphrased by the note from the original English; the note itself renders these as translated Portuguese, not as English direct quotes at this point). EXPLICIT (note, item 6).
- Four broad initial pathways identified in interviews: (1) focus on graduate profile ("graduateness"); (2) exploration of the program's educational philosophy; (3) presentation of research related to the change driver; (4) introduction of theoretical curriculum models. EXPLICIT (note, item 6).
- Questionnaire data showed a strong majority preference for starting with learning objectives/outcomes (43.47% at program level, 40.90% at module level), with graduate-profile exploration also prominent. Notably, questionnaire respondents showed little interest in starting with research data on change motivators, in contrast to interview participants, among whom "presentation of research" was one of the four identified pathways. EXPLICIT (note, item 6) — this is a tension the note itself flags ("Ao contrário dos entrevistados, os respondentes do questionário demonstraram pouco interesse...").
- All participants agreed that discussion of specific subject content (disciplines/isolated topics) should be the last step in the planning process. EXPLICIT (note, item 6).
- Decision-making by developers is strongly influenced by staff discipline, team energy/motivation, institutional strategy, and available time. EXPLICIT (note, item 6).

## 8. Main arguments

The note's discussion/conclusions (item 7) present several linked arguments: initiating curriculum revision is a situated process benefiting from deep initial listening and should not be rushed, requiring "a long, long dialogue" (direct quotation preserved from note item 10/7). There are no universal recipes; developers must show methodological flexibility and tailor their approach to institutional and stakeholder context. Although most developers practically adopt the product model (oriented toward learning outcomes and constructive alignment), they simultaneously draw on process-model strategies (listening, contextualization, respect for staff's pedagogical philosophy) — the note frames this as a hybrid or dual orientation rather than a pure allegiance to either model. EXPLICIT (note, item 7). The note also argues there is a lack of systemic institutional evaluation to measure the effectiveness of the pedagogical-advising strategies adopted. EXPLICIT (note, item 7).

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note lists three contributions attributed to the study: (1) a practical contribution — an integrative conceptual model (Figure 1) systematizing the stages and factors of curriculum initiation, intended as a strategic guide for novice developers and academic staff; (2) a competency-development contribution — highlighting the centrality of interpersonal communication, listening, and negotiation skills in the educational developer's role; (3) an advocacy contribution — evidencing the urgent need for continuing-education programs and "role-modeling" practices in which senior developers make their actions transparent to support newcomers' formation. EXPLICIT (note, item 8).

## 10. Limitations

The note records the following author-identified limitations: the small size of the Phase 2 questionnaire sample (22 respondents, 30% response rate); the strict restriction of the study to the initiation phase only, excluding implementation of new teaching methodologies, assessment strategies, or long-term curricular impact evaluation; and the absence of data demonstrating measurable effectiveness of the suggested methodological approaches, with success instead assessed only through informal, intuitive means by developers (e.g., "as pessoas retornam" — paraphrased in the note, not given as an English direct quote). EXPLICIT (note, item 9). Suggested future research includes investigating why particular starting approaches (outcomes, philosophy, or graduate profile) are chosen across different disciplinary/organizational contexts, and follow-up studies tracking later stages of curriculum development (implementation and systematic evaluation). EXPLICIT (note, item 9).

## 11. Implications

The note's contributions and future-research sections imply several practical directions: that professional development programs for educational developers should formalize mentoring and "role-modeling" mechanisms rather than leaving practice-knowledge tacit among seniors (EXPLICIT, note item 8); that institutions initiating curriculum revision processes might benefit from structured but flexible starting frameworks (e.g., the Figure 1 model) rather than prescriptive content-first approaches (EXPLICIT, note items 7–8); and that further empirical work is needed to validate whether particular initiation strategies are more effective across disciplinary or organizational contexts, since the note explicitly states no effectiveness data currently exist beyond informal impressions (EXPLICIT, note item 9). Beyond these author-stated directions, broader implications — e.g., for institutional policy design, quality-assurance frameworks, or accreditation processes — are not discussed in the note and would be speculative to attribute to the study. UNSUPPORTED.

## 12. Concepts relevant across the corpus

- The product vs. process distinction in curriculum models (objectives/plans/means-ends logic vs. activities/flexibility/experience-orientation).
- Constructive alignment (Biggs) as a framework linking learning outcomes, teaching activities, and assessment.
- Stark's two-phase curriculum-planning "filters" (staff beliefs/considerations, then student/context influences) as a heuristic for sequencing curriculum design conversations.
- The distinction between "internal consultant" and "interpretative-hermeneutic" professional orientations for educational/curriculum developers (Land's ecological model).
- The notion of curriculum revision initiation as a distinct, under-theorized phase, separable from design, implementation, and evaluation phases.
- "Graduateness"/graduate profile as an entry point for curriculum discussions.
- The idea that content-level (disciplinary/topic) decisions should be sequenced last in curriculum planning discussions.
- Role-modeling and mentoring as mechanisms for transmitting tacit professional practice knowledge among practitioners (here, educational developers).
- Dialogic/listening-based professional practice as a counterweight to managerial or purely instrumental advisory postures.

## 14. Relationships that should be investigated against other studies

- Whether any other studies in the corpus address the specific initiation/entry phase of curriculum design/revision as a distinct analytical stage, or whether they treat curriculum design as a single undifferentiated process.
- Whether other studies in the corpus similarly report a tension between the model or philosophy predominantly *espoused* by practitioners or institutions (e.g., outcomes/product-oriented frameworks) and the relational, situated, process-oriented strategies actually enacted in practice.
- Whether the discrepancy this note identifies between interview-based and questionnaire-based data (e.g., interviewees citing "presentation of research" as a legitimate entry point, versus questionnaire respondents showing low interest in that same starting point) recurs in other studies employing mixed-methods designs with two distinct participant pools, and how such studies interpret or reconcile such discrepancies.
- Whether other studies in the corpus discuss the professional isolation of curriculum/educational developers, or the lack of "role models"/mentoring structures, as a barrier to effective practice.
- Whether other studies engage with Land's ecological model of educational developer orientations, Biggs's constructive alignment, or Stark's curriculum-planning filters, and if so, whether they extend, qualify, or contest the framing given here.
- Whether other studies provide effectiveness or outcome data for curriculum-revision advisory practices that could address the evaluative gap this note explicitly flags as unresolved.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

- The note explicitly reports a divergence between what interviewees identified as a legitimate initiation pathway (presenting research on change motivators) and what questionnaire respondents actually preferred in practice (low interest in that same starting point). This internal inconsistency is acknowledged by the note itself but is not resolved or further interrogated — it is unclear whether this reflects a genuine practice divergence between the two participant pools, a methodological artifact of unstructured interviews versus closed/open questionnaire items, or a difference in what "starting point" means across the two data-collection instruments. DERIVED (identified by comparing the item-6 interview findings against the item-6 questionnaire findings within the note).
- The study's central claim that most developers "in practice" adopt the product model while relying on process-model strategies (item 7) rests on the same mixed-methods data described in item 5; the note does not clarify how this dual-orientation conclusion was derived analytically (e.g., whether it is a qualitative interpretation of interview transcripts, a synthesis across both phases, or an inference from the frequency data alone). DERIVED (the claim appears in the discussion section without an explicit link back to a specific analytic procedure in the methodology section).
- The claim of "successful" initiation is explicitly conceded by the note to rest on informal, intuitive indicators of success (e.g., people returning) rather than any measured outcome — this means the study's practical recommendations (e.g., the Figure 1 model) are grounded in practitioner self-report and consensus rather than demonstrated effectiveness. EXPLICIT (note, item 9), with the interpretive point about the recommendations' evidentiary grounding being DERIVED.
- The sample sizes are small (6 interviewees; 22 questionnaire respondents from 74 invited, 30% response rate) and geographically concentrated in Ireland and the UK (with only two respondents from elsewhere), which the note itself lists as a limitation but which also raises an unaddressed question about how far "no universal recipes" and "context-dependent flexibility" claims can be generalized beyond the specific institutional/national settings sampled. EXPLICIT for the reported sample composition (note, item 5); DERIVED for the generalizability concern, which follows from applying the note's own stated small-sample limitation to its broader claims about flexibility and context-dependence.
- It is unclear from the note whether the 6 Phase 1 interviewees are included among, overlap with, or are wholly distinct from the 22 Phase 2 respondents, which affects how the "28 developers" figure in the synthesis (item 11) should be interpreted. UNSUPPORTED (not clarified in the note).
