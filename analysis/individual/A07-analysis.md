# A07 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

The note identifies the subject investigated as curriculum mapping ("mapeamento curricular"), described as a method of spatial representation and management of the different components of an academic curriculum and the relations between them.

The note reports that the problem motivating the work is a communication gap in curriculum design: although medical education has focused intensely on curriculum-development strategies (student-centered, problem-based, and community-based learning), communication about the curriculum itself has been neglected. According to the note, teachers and students frequently do not know which topics are covered, where they are taught, what resources are available for each learning outcome, or how assessments connect to the overall program.

The note frames the relevance of this problem in terms of increasing curricular complexity: the shift toward more centralized curricula (as opposed to isolated department-based curricula) and the coexistence of mandatory and optional components have made teaching programs highly complex. Curriculum mapping is presented in the note as the tool that addresses this complexity, optimizing time for students and teachers and helping ensure that planned learning actually occurs.

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note states the objective of the work as presenting the concept of curriculum mapping in detail, illustrating how it makes the curriculum transparent and integrated, proposing a multidimensional structure based on 10 observation "windows," and describing nine practical steps for its development and successful implementation in an institution.

The note explicitly characterizes this as a conceptual and practical-guidelines article (an educational guide) that does not propose traditional empirical hypotheses. Instead, according to the note, it seeks to answer operational questions posed by multiple actors in the school community, such as: "Como professores e estudantes sabem o que é abordado no currículo e onde isso ocorre?", "Como a avaliação se relaciona com o programa de ensino?", and "Quais recursos são necessários para sustentar cada parte do programa?" — these being the note's own Portuguese-language renderings of the guiding questions, not quotations attributed to the original English-language article.

## 3. Context investigated

The note specifies the context as professional education, with a predominant focus on medical education. It reports that the author draws on case studies and accounts of practical implementation experiences at real medical schools: the University of Birmingham (based on Filemaker Pro databases and modified MeSH terms), Oslo (digital integration of electronic resources), and Tufts (an internet-based curricular database).

## 4. Methodological approach

The note classifies the study type as a theoretical review article, conceptual modeling, and proposition of institutional guidelines (an AMEE Guide). This is not framed in the note as an empirical study with a conventional data-collection and data-analysis design.

The note describes the analytical/developmental procedure as systematizing the creation of curriculum maps by unfolding them into 10 interconnected data dimensions and providing a structured plan of 9 consecutive steps to guide developers, from needs assessment to long-term system maintenance.

DERIVED: Because the note characterizes the work as a conceptual/guideline article rather than an empirical study, the "results" reported (the 10 windows, the 9 steps) should be understood as the proposed content of the guide itself rather than findings derived from a data-analysis procedure. This distinction affects how the "Main findings" section below should be read.

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note lists several prior models and theories the author draws upon:

- Curriculum Mapping (English, 1978, 1979, 1984): the seminal concept of mapping the curriculum to present the "taught" curriculum as opposed to the "declared" (fictional) or "tested" curriculum.
- Concept Mapping (Novak & Gowin, 1984): used as a starting point for structuring curricular goals.
- Matrix representation systems (Du Bois & Kiewra, 1989; Kiewra, 1997): two-dimensional modeling of knowledge (hierarchical, sequential, and matrix structures) to facilitate spatial visualization of interrelations among ideas, contrasted with standard linear texts.
- Outcome-Based Education and the Three-Circle Model (Harden et al., 1999b): a framework composed of 12 categories of learning outcomes divided among what the physician is able to do, how they approach their practice, and the physician as a professional.
- Task-Based Learning (Harden et al., 2000): identifying specific professional tasks as the organizing focus of learning.

The note also reports, in the limitations section, that the author relies conceptually on Ausubel's framework of advance organizers ("organizadores prévios de Ausubel") to support the rationale for mapping, in the absence of direct empirical evidence.

## 6. Object being investigated

The object under examination, as reported in the note, is curriculum mapping itself as a conceptual and practical tool/method — its structure (10 windows), its terminology (expertise mapping), and its implementation process (9 steps) — rather than an empirical phenomenon studied through observation or measurement of learners or outcomes.

## 7. Main findings

The note reports the following as the article's principal outputs:

- The 10 Windows of the curriculum map, allowing the curriculum to be viewed from 10 distinct perspectives: (1) expected learning outcomes; (2) curricular content or areas of expertise; (3) student assessment; (4) learning opportunities; (5) learning location; (6) learning resources; (7) timetable; (8) staff; (9) curriculum management; (10) students.
- Expertise Mapping: a term the author proposes to replace traditional "concept mapping," adapting it to a competency-based education model to explicitly include skills and attitudes alongside the cognitive domain.
- A 9-step process for constructing the map: (1) assess needs; (2) define the scope of the task; (3) establish the links; (4) populate the windows; (5) decide the format; (6) consider past, present, and future; (7) define access levels; (8) familiarize staff and students; (9) evaluate and update the map.

DERIVED: Because the note frames this as a conceptual/guideline article, these "findings" constitute the proposed content of the framework rather than results demonstrated through empirical testing. The note itself later states that direct empirical evidence for the framework's effectiveness is scarce (see Section 10), reinforcing that these outputs are best understood as a proposed model rather than validated findings.

## 8. Main arguments

According to the note, the authors argue that the key to effective integrated teaching lies in the coordinated exchange of information among faculty about what is being taught, ensuring that activities reflect the institution's core objectives.

The note reports that the curriculum map is interpreted not merely as a static schedule or syllabus but as the method for operationalizing outcome-based education, functioning as part of a Learning Management System (LMS).

The note also records conditions the authors argue are necessary for successful implementation: unrestricted institutional support (such as workload credit for engaged faculty), clear academic leadership with dedicated time, and a system presenting a simple, user-friendly interface despite its underlying structural complexity.

A recurring argument, reflected in the note's selected excerpts, is that the essence of a curriculum map lies not in its nodes but in the links between them: "...the essence of a curriculum map is not the nodes but the links or relations between the nodes."

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note distinguishes two types of contribution:

Theoretical: proposition of the concept of "expertise mapping" (joining clusters, areas, and units of expertise/learning, which the note describes as analogous to "reusable learning objects"), and the expansion of the traditional curriculum map to incorporate management data and individual student portfolios.

Practical/methodological: provision of an operational 9-step model, accompanied by a specification form ("pro forma") for organizing the distribution of responsibilities in collecting and updating information. The note reports that the map helps identify unplanned redundancies ("visitar duas vezes a mesma área") and gaps in learning.

## 10. Limitations

The note reports that the author himself acknowledges that practical experience with curriculum mapping in education is still limited. From a scientific standpoint, the note states there is little direct empirical evidence demonstrating conclusively the effectiveness of maps in directly improving student learning, with the argument relying primarily on the conceptual framework of Ausubel's advance organizers rather than direct empirical validation.

The note also reports a further limitation acknowledged by the author: integrating scientific evidence bases on educational effectiveness (BEME) into each curricular node is described as a herculean task that exceeds the capacity of an individual institution.

DERIVED: The explicit acknowledgment of scarce direct empirical evidence, combined with the prescriptive and confident tone of the 9-step guide and the "glue" metaphor, suggests a gap between the conceptual/rhetorical strength of the argument and its empirical grounding. This is a tension worth noting analytically (see Section 16) rather than a limitation stated by the author in those terms.

## 11. Implications

The note reports the following suggestions for future work made in the source: creating and testing evaluation methodologies sensitive enough to measure the impact of mapping on student learning; exploring the curriculum map as a tool for geographic and academic contextualization for empirical research on educational interventions; and integrating evidence of teaching effectiveness directly into the map's nodes.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

The note reports that the article's motivating premise assumes that improved communication and transparency about the curriculum (via mapping) causally improves educational outcomes ("otimizando o tempo de alunos e professores e garantindo que o aprendizado planejado de fato ocorra"), yet the same note also explicitly records the author's acknowledgment that there is little direct empirical evidence demonstrating the map's effectiveness in improving student learning. This is a tension between the confident, prescriptive framing of the tool (the "glue" metaphor, the definitive 9-step methodology) and the admittedly weak empirical base supporting its efficacy.

DERIVED: The reliance on Ausubel's advance organizer theory as the primary conceptual justification, rather than direct empirical testing of curriculum mapping itself, suggests the argument for efficacy is analogical/theoretical rather than demonstrated. This is an interpretation drawn from the note's own juxtaposition of the efficacy claims with the stated limitation regarding empirical evidence, not an independent assessment of the original article.

DERIVED: The case examples from Birmingham, Oslo, and Tufts are described in the note as implementation experiences/case studies rather than as controlled or comparative evaluations. The note does not indicate that these institutional examples measured learning outcomes systematically; therefore, they appear to function as illustrations of feasibility rather than as evidence of effectiveness. This distinction is not stated explicitly by the note as a limitation but follows from the absence of any reported outcome-evaluation methodology for these cases.

The claim that curriculum mapping is "the" method to operationalize outcome-based education (as reported in the note) presents mapping as uniquely suited to this role, without the note indicating that alternative operationalization methods were considered or compared.
