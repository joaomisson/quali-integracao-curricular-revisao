# A11 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

According to the note, the study addresses curricular reform and curricular coherence in undergraduate programs within U.S. higher education. The problem framed by the note is "curricular drift" (curricular drift): over four decades, degree programs originally designed as rational, interdependent sequences of learning experiences have expanded, contracted, and morphed into aggregates of isolated courses whose only unifying feature is reaching the 120-credit threshold or satisfying disciplinary accreditation requirements. The note states that this produces pedagogical incoherence, hinders development of broad competencies demanded by the labor market (e.g., critical thinking, communication), and generates frustration among both students (who cannot perceive the coherence of their academic trajectory) and faculty (who feel a loss of control over their curricula). The note frames the study's relevance around a "moral imperative" facing institutions given changes in the student population served (including minority and first-generation students), and the need to ensure efficient academic pathways enabling timely graduation, minimal debt, and preparation for 21st-century demands.

This is an EXPLICIT characterization, as it closely follows the note's own "Tema e problema de pesquisa" section.

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note reports the study's objective as examining and improving curricular coherence in undergraduate programs at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas (UNLV), through a multidisciplinary team of faculty and administrators who developed a curricular review framework and a consultative support model to assist academic departments in restructuring their curricula.

The note lists two guiding research questions:
1. How do curricular changes designed and approved by departments actually affect the structural complexity of programs?
2. What are faculty reactions, confidence levels, and concerns regarding the implementation process of curricular reforms in their departments?

EXPLICIT, drawn directly from the "Objetivo e perguntas de pesquisa" section of the note.

## 3. Context investigated

The note specifies the empirical setting as UNLV, described as a university designated by the U.S. Department of Education as a minority-serving and Hispanic-serving institution, with more than 31,000 enrolled students. The intervention tracked nine undergraduate programs across seven academic departments in five different colleges, between fall 2016 and spring 2019. For the faculty-reaction component, data were collected from 18 faculty members across six departments who responded to post-reform questionnaires.

EXPLICIT, based on the "Metodologia" section of the note.

## 4. Methodological approach

The note characterizes the study as an institutional curricular-intervention case study combining qualitative and quantitative methods. Data collection procedures reported include:

1. A "Curricular Coherence Framework" — a non-prescriptive reflective guide organized into three axes: Design (e.g., reinforcement of key competencies throughout the program), Implementation (e.g., pass rates, excessive need for waivers), and Evaluation (e.g., pedagogical use of institutional assessment data).
2. Support and compensation — customized data (failure rates, flow reports), administrative assistance to speed proposal approval, and a $1,500 financial compensation for departmental activities.
3. A reaction survey (adapted Stages of Concern Questionnaire) using a 5-point Likert scale measuring perceived usefulness, personal confidence, and level of concern.
4. A custom evaluation rubric — a 3-point qualitative classification measuring progress of structured changes across eight goal categories.
5. Complexity analysis — four-year degree plan data for each program loaded into the University of New Mexico's Curricular Analytics platform to compute structural complexity scores pre- and post-reform.

This is EXPLICIT, taken from the "Metodologia" section. The note does not specify sampling procedures for the 18-faculty survey (e.g., response rate, invitation method), nor details of qualitative coding procedures if any were used — these remain UNSUPPORTED by the note as given.

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note reports that the authors integrate several distinct theoretical strands:

- Models of change in higher education (Trowler et al., 2003): five approaches to organizational change differentiated by drivers and power flow — technical-rational, resource allocation, diffusionist, Kai Zen/bricolage, and complexity.
- Communities of Practice (Wenger, 1998 — the note spells it "Wegner"): used to frame the academic department as the primary locus where teaching practices, assessment criteria, and a sense of intellectual ownership over the curriculum develop.
- Curricular Tensions (Kandiko & Blackmore, 2010): focused on intrinsic dilemmas of curriculum redesign, specifically breadth versus depth and structure versus choice.
- Historical Curricular Processes (Bernstein, 1996): addresses curricular evolution through processes of introjection (internal disciplinary demands) and projection (external pressures and regulations).
- Curricular Complexity (Heileman et al., 2017): a mathematical model quantifying structural complexity of course sequences via two per-course factors — blocking factor (number of subsequent courses whose access depends on that course) and delay factor (length of the longest prerequisite chain the course belongs to).

EXPLICIT, drawn from the "Fundamentação" section. Note: the note itself renders the Wenger citation as "Wegner, 1998" — this spelling is reproduced as it appears in the note, which may reflect a transcription artifact in the literature note rather than the original article.

## 6. Object being investigated

The object under investigation is a curricular reform intervention — its design, implementation process, and consequences — across nine undergraduate degree programs at a single university (UNLV), examined along two axes: (a) measurable structural complexity of course sequences (via the Curricular Analytics quantitative model) and (b) faculty perceptions, confidence, and concerns regarding the reform process (via survey instrument).

DERIVED: this framing synthesizes the dual quantitative/qualitative object described piecemeal across the "Objetivo," "Metodologia," and "Principais resultados" sections of the note, making explicit that the study's object is simultaneously a structural/curricular artifact and a human/institutional process.

## 7. Main findings

The note reports the following results:

- Faculty reaction: high perceived usefulness (mean 4.23 ± 0.75), moderate confidence in implementation (3.5 ± 0.55), and very low levels of concern (1.27 ± 0.46). The most frequently cited obstacles were difficulty reaching internal departmental consensus and bureaucratic barriers in the university/state approval system.
- Curricular actions taken: departments focused almost unanimously on eliminating bureaucratic structural barriers (e.g., unnecessary or obsolete prerequisites) — attempted by 8 programs, completed by 100% of them (maximum progress score of 3.0). Removal of "boutique" courses (not directly serving overall program goals) also succeeded highly (3 programs attempted, 100% completed). By contrast, no department attempted to introduce "signature work" opportunities or methods to make learning outcomes transparent to students.
- Complexity impact: no average reduction in structural complexity scores was observed pre- versus post-revision, and total required credits remained unchanged. Four programs reduced their complexity scores, four increased, and one stayed the same. In STEM programs, average complexity rose from 316 to 322, driven by the voluntary decision to split unified 4-credit courses (e.g., General Chemistry) into separate 3-credit theory and 1-credit corequisite lab components — a change that increases complexity under the mathematical algorithm (by creating new requirement links) but is described in the note as practically beneficial because it prevents a student who fails only one part from having to retake the entire combined course.

EXPLICIT, drawn directly from the "Principais resultados" section, including the specific numeric values reported in the note.

## 8. Main arguments

The note attributes to the authors several interpretive arguments beyond the raw findings:

- Eliminating peripheral structural obstacles (prerequisites) constitutes "low hanging fruit" — easy for departments to accept and execute — whereas deep pedagogical changes (competency transparency, signature work) require intensive negotiation of time and effort plus sustained external resources and instructional designers.
- The authors conclude that blindly reducing complexity should not be established as a final goal of educational reform; an excessively unstructured curriculum shifts the entire burden of coherence onto the student, potentially resulting in fragmented course choices or in hidden, systematically uncommunicated requirements.
- Metric mapping tools like Curricular Analytics proved useful for locating technical publication errors in degree plans (e.g., courses recommended for the same semester that had a prerequisite relationship between them) and for revealing that formal catalog requirements were routinely waived by advisors and faculty in actual practice.
- A change-management model based on listening and decentralized empowerment of departments produced favorable and durable experiences for faculty.

EXPLICIT, drawn from the "Discussão e conclusões" section of the note.

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note lists two categories of contribution attributed to the authors:

- Practical contribution: a functional consultative approach that reduces departmental resistance to central-administration interference in faculty curricular-design autonomy.
- Theoretical/methodological contribution: empirical demonstration that managing complex structural change in higher education is enhanced by hybridizing technical-rational, diffusionist, financial-incentive, and complexity-based approaches; the article also contributes to demystifying the "low complexity" metric as an absolute synonym for learning quality.

EXPLICIT, drawn from the "Contribuições" section.

## 10. Limitations

The note reports the authors' acknowledged limitations:

- The faculty-reaction survey had a small sample of respondents (18 respondents from 6 departments), introducing a possible self-selection bias — the note speculates (attributed to the authors, per the note's own phrasing "provavelmente") that more engaged and confident faculty were more likely to complete the instrument.
- Participants tended to select only low-effort, low-time-impact curricular changes.

EXPLICIT, drawn from the "Limitações Reconhecidas" section. Note that the note itself flags the self-selection explanation as a probabilistic inference ("provavelmente") rather than a confirmed mechanism, and this hedge is preserved here.

The note does not report limitations concerning the generalizability of findings beyond the single-institution case, absence of a comparison/control group, or possible confounds in the complexity metric itself (e.g., whether Heileman et al.'s algorithm captures pedagogically meaningful complexity) — these remain UNSUPPORTED by the note.

## 11. Implications

The note records the following forward-looking suggestions attributed to the authors:

1. Creating a tiered financial-incentive structure (larger incentives for programs adopting deep revisions of learning outcomes or signature work).
2. Adopting a multi-stage intervention, where an initial phase addresses course-sequencing (simpler) and subsequent phases progressively advance toward complex discussions of systemic departmental assessment and consolidation of professional competencies.
3. Conducting longitudinal analyses to track the real empirical impacts of these curricular changes on student success and indicators such as retention, time-to-degree, and reduction of exceptional course waivers.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

- The study's central complexity metric (Heileman et al.'s blocking/delay factor model) is treated as a proxy for structural coherence, yet the note itself documents a case (STEM lab/theory course splitting) where an increase in the algorithmic complexity score corresponds to what the authors regard as a pedagogically beneficial change. This suggests the metric's construct validity as an indicator of "problematic" complexity is questionable, and the authors appear to acknowledge this tension explicitly (their caution against treating complexity reduction as an intrinsic goal) — but the note does not report any alternative or supplementary metric being developed or validated to address this gap. UNSUPPORTED beyond the note's own critique.
- There is an evident tension between the self-reported faculty data (high perceived usefulness, low concern) and the behavioral outcome data (near-total avoidance of deep pedagogical reforms such as signature work or outcome transparency). The note presents this as a documented discrepancy rather than resolving it; it is not clear from the note whether the authors treat this gap as evidence that the survey instrument failed to capture resistance to deep change, or as evidence that "low concern" is compatible with conservative behavioral choices for other reasons (e.g., rational avoidance of high-effort tasks under resource constraints). This interpretive gap is DERIVED as a tension worth flagging, since the note reports both facts adjacently in the "Principais resultados" and "Trechos importantes" sections without an explicit resolving mechanism attributed to the authors, beyond noting time/resource costs as the presumed cause.
- The self-selection bias limitation (survey respondents were "provavelmente" the more engaged/confident faculty) is presented as a probable but unverified explanation in the note; it is not clear whether the authors had any independent data (e.g., comparison of respondent vs. non-respondent departments) to substantiate this, or whether it is a plausibility-based caveat. This remains UNSUPPORTED as a verified finding, only EXPLICIT as an authors' stated caveat.
- The note frames the reform effort as originating from the provost's office (top-down, technical-rational in structure) while simultaneously emphasizing participatory, department-empowering framing ("escuta e...empoderamento descentralizado"). The note itself flags this hybridity via the quoted passage acknowledging the top-down structural origin alongside efforts to mitigate perceptions of unilateral mandates. Whether this represents a genuine reconciliation of centralized authority with decentralized agency, or primarily a rhetorical/political strategy to secure faculty buy-in for an administratively driven initiative, is not resolved in the note and is worth treating as an open interpretive tension rather than a settled finding.
