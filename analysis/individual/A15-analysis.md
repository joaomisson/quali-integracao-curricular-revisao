# A15 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

The literature note reports that the article investigates curriculum change processes for embedding Education for Sustainable Development (ESD) in higher education institutions (HEIs), examining how sustainability is integrated institutionally and transversally into curricula. The note frames the research problem as a gap: while numerous individual case studies describe successful sustainability integration in isolated courses or programs, the note states that "pouco se sabe sobre os processos de implementação sistemáticos que levam à verdadeira institucionalização abrangente dos currículos de sustentabilidade" (paraphrased by this analyst as: little is known about the systematic implementation processes that lead to genuinely comprehensive curricular institutionalization). According to the note, curriculum change processes are complex and vary enormously across institutions, which hinders comparison, generalization, and mutual learning among HEIs, and the literature reportedly lacks theorization of how multiple drivers and barriers interact in these processes. The note records the relevance rationale as resting on HEIs' role in preparing future decision-makers for a sustainable society transition.

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note explicitly records the objective as conducting a systematic synthesis (meta-analysis) of 131 international case studies to identify shared patterns (clusters) of sustainability curriculum implementation processes in HEIs, analyzing the interaction of drivers and barriers, the level of change achieved, and the type of integration. Two research questions are explicitly listed: (1) how does sustainability curriculum change occur in higher education institutions; and (2) which interrelated factors lead to which level of implementation.

## 3. Context investigated

The note explicitly describes the empirical material as 131 international case studies of sustainability curriculum implementation in HEIs, drawn from an initial pool of 230 cases identified in peer-reviewed journals, book chapters, and institutional websites. Of these, 133 were selected based on level of detail, and 2 were later discarded for insufficient data, yielding the final sample of 131 cases. The note does not provide a systematic breakdown of the geographic distribution of the full sample, though it explicitly names India and Vietnam as contexts underlying specific cluster subgroups (cluster 4b and cluster 6a respectively), and explicitly records geographic imbalance as an acknowledged limitation.

## 4. Methodological approach

The note explicitly identifies the study as a meta-study using the "case survey method," described as a meta-analytic technique that systematically synthesizes qualitative case studies and converts them into quantitative data through a coding scheme. The coding scheme comprised 111 variables, categorized predominantly as barriers (absence/weakness), medium level (ambiguous impact), drivers (high strength), or absence of information. Five trained coders performed the coding, with inter-rater agreement reported at 94% on a 10% test subsample. Analytic procedures explicitly recorded include Ward cluster analysis (executed in R, version 3.6.2) to group HEIs by similarity across coded variables, and indicator species analysis to identify which variables were statistically significant characteristics of each resulting cluster.

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note records that the authors synthesize concepts and theories from several research fields: general curriculum change theories (e.g., Cuban 1999; Fullan 2007), organizational change and innovation (e.g., Kotter 1996; De La Harpe and Thomas 2009; Verhulst and Lambrechts 2015), transformational change (e.g., Eckel and Kezar 2003; Kapitulčinová et al. 2018), transition network methodology (e.g., Pardellas Santiago et al. 2017; Stephens et al. 2008), social dynamics and cultural change focused on learning organizations and adaptive versus progressive change (e.g., Hoover and Harder 2015; Gaugh and Scott 2001; Avery and Nordén 2017), and Meadows' leverage points for system intervention (e.g., Lidgren et al. 2006; Meadows 1999).

From this literature, the note reports that the authors structure the analysis around five essential interrelated elements: (1) type of ESD implementation — vertical (explicit focus), horizontal (implicit/integrated), or combined (Lambrechts et al. 2013), and disciplinary, interdisciplinary, or transdisciplinary approaches (Barth 2013); (2) depth of curricular change — measured via the depth-and-diffusion scale (Eckel et al. 1999) and the four levels of Sterling and Thomas (2006): denial (no change), bolt-on (education about sustainability added to existing courses), build-in (education for sustainability in new interdisciplinary structures), and redesign (sustainable education integrated into the institution's vision and core); (3) stages and dynamics of curriculum change, from initiation to adoption (Lattuca and Stark 2009) and collaboration phases (Krizek et al. 2012); (4) impetus for change — internal versus external (Lattuca and Stark 2009), normative versus goal-oriented (Fumasoli and Lepori 2011), and intrinsic motivation/reflection (Hoover and Harder 2015); and (5) drivers and barriers — stakeholders, organizational tools (strategic plans, participation), and institutional culture (Velazquez et al. 2005; Barth 2015; Lozano and Barreiro-Gen 2019).

## 6. Object being investigated

The object under investigation, as recorded in the note, is the aggregate pattern of curriculum change processes documented across 131 published case studies of HEIs implementing ESD. This is a second-order object: the study does not conduct primary fieldwork on institutions but treats already-published case narratives as its raw data, recoded into standardized variables for statistical pattern detection. The unit of analysis is therefore the individual HEI case as represented in existing published literature, not the institution studied directly by the authors.

## 7. Main findings

The note explicitly reports that cluster analysis revealed six distinct patterns of sustainability curriculum implementation processes:

1. **Collaborative paradigm change** (N=19): reaches the deepest level, redesign; characterized by strong collaboration and support from internal and external stakeholders, formal participation processes, a widely accepted vision statement, systemic integration of sustainability across teaching, research, campus management, and outreach, distributed leadership, dedicated resources, and faculty training.
2. **Bottom-up, evolving institutional change** (N=26): also reaches redesign (with some build-in tendencies); initiated bottom-up by students and faculty through small projects, expanding via informal communication arenas, with formal top-management support and dedicated resources emerging later to consolidate strategy.
3. **Top-down, mandated institutional change** (N=30): results mostly in build-in level; initiated and planned by leadership due to external incentives (governmental requirements or competitive advantage), but with little formal community participation, absence of a unified vision, low sense of ownership, and resulting faculty resistance.
4. **Externally driven initiatives** (N=22): results in bolt-on or build-in level; characterized by weak internal support/planning but strong external propulsion; divided into two subgroups — (a) programs supported by international research networks or Regional Centres of Expertise (RCEs) led locally by sustainability champions; (b) cases in India motivated by governmental and Supreme Court mandates, where NGOs and RCEs externally support material development due to lack of local interdisciplinary faculty competence.
5. **Isolated initiatives** (N=26): leads to build-in or bolt-on level; occurs when few sustainability champions lead fragmented initiatives marked by low cross-faculty collaboration, lack of internal coordination, and a highly competitive university environment; external activity occurs via international projects but with little campus operational sustainability activity.
6. **Limited institutional change** (N=8): reaches bolt-on or build-in level; involves bottom-up activities facing severe barriers and lack of support, absence of a strategic plan, weak leadership, lack of resources, and low interdisciplinary faculty competence; divided into two subgroups — (a) Vietnam cases suffering from cultural-understanding barriers and traditional didactics, and (b) institutions with a long history of implementation attempts severely limited by weak coordination and inadequate communication.

The note also reports a two-phase process model: Phase 1 (Initiation), in which change can begin with any stakeholder or institutional area (teaching, research, management, outreach), and Phase 2 (Sustaining), in which communication, collaboration, and leadership tools are crucial to reach a critical mass of supporters. Five key influences for sustaining broad implementation are explicitly listed: impetus, communication, sense of ownership, collaboration/coalitions, and coordination. The note explicitly records that patterns are not linear, obligatory life-cycle stages — HEIs can move between patterns, and trajectories depend on institution-specific context.

## 8. Main arguments

The central argument recorded in the note is that institutionalization of sustainability curricula is not achieved through a single linear pathway but through several qualitatively distinct patterns shaped by the interaction of impetus, communication, ownership, collaboration, and coordination. A specific sub-argument, supported by a direct quotation in the note, is that informal collaboration and communication among "champions" can partially compensate for a lack of formal top-leadership support: "Strong informal collaboration and communication can compensate for a lack of formalized leadership support from the top." A second sub-argument, also directly quoted, holds that a unified and participatory vision statement fosters ownership and durability of change: "Maintaining a unified vision statement and active participation of all stakeholders fosters a sense of ownership in ESD implementation and ensures that it will be long-lasting." A third argument, explicitly quoted, positions the six patterns as alternative processes rather than obligatory sequential stages: "[...] the patterns can be seen as different processes that are used to achieve the institutionalization of sustainability curricula rather than as different stages through which the HEI must transition." Underlying the whole study is an implicit methodological argument that the case survey method, combined with cluster analysis, can produce statistically robust, generalizable patterns out of an otherwise heterogeneous body of qualitative case-study literature.

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note explicitly records three types of contribution claimed by the authors. Theoretically, the article is said to integrate curriculum-change and organizational-change literatures with ESD practice, proposing an empirically derived taxonomy of six patterns to help understand the complexity of change. Methodologically, the note describes the study as pioneering in applying the case survey method combined with cluster analysis in the context of sustainability in higher education, demonstrating how qualitative data from hundreds of dispersed case studies can be transformed into structured, highly reliable quantitative data for statistical analysis. Practically, the note records that the study provides a self-reflection scheme ("esquema de autorreflexão") for university managers, allowing them to identify which pattern their institution currently fits and extract practical lessons to evolve toward a full paradigm redesign.

## 10. Limitations

The note explicitly records five limitations acknowledged by the authors: (1) geographic imbalance — most studied cases concentrate in specific countries/continents, not representing a globally uniform view; (2) heterogeneous secondary data — the analyzed case studies had widely varying foci, theoretical perspectives, and methodologies in their original publications; (3) success bias — because many case studies are self-reported, there is a strong tendency to publish success stories, omitting structural failures, severe barriers, and internal dynamics; (4) data gaps — missing information was statistically treated as irrelevant by the authors, which may mask real influences, and it was reportedly difficult to map long-term (decades-long) change processes based only on the analyzed texts; (5) small subgroups — clusters 4 and 6 depended heavily on multi-case studies, requiring broader sampling to validate the proposed subdivisions.

## 11. Implications

The note explicitly lists six suggested directions for future research: improving the quality of individual or comparative case studies by explicitly documenting strategies used to address barriers; investigating with greater precision the real impact of international policy milestones (such as the UN Decade for ESD) on HEI change; collecting data from the perspective of multiple stakeholders to understand their underlying assumptions about ESD; more clearly delineating the temporal phases of the processes and when each influence is most decisive; investigating double-loop learning and collaboration dynamics under financial constraints; and using new data types (in-depth interviews, monitoring data, and institutional sustainability assessment reports) to fill informational gaps. On the practical side, the note records the pattern typology as intended for use as a diagnostic/self-reflection tool by university managers.

- Top-down versus bottom-up initiation of curricular change.
- Interaction/interplay of drivers and barriers as a joint explanatory mechanism.
- External mandates/policy pressure versus internal motivation as change triggers.
- Coordination structures (units, committees, shared responsibilities) as enablers of synergy and non-redundancy.
- Collaboration and coalition-building, internal and external, as compensatory mechanisms in competitive or under-resourced environments.
- Self-reported case-study bias as a methodological concern in sustainability-in-higher-education research.
- Context-specific/geographic subgroup dynamics (e.g., India's government/Supreme Court-mandated cases; Vietnam's cultural/didactic barriers).

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

The coding scheme reduces qualitative case narratives to 111 categorical variables (driver/barrier/medium/absent). This is an assumption worth challenging: converting rich institutional narratives into standardized categorical codes risks losing contextual nuance and interpretive detail present in the original case studies, even though the note reports a high inter-rater reliability (94%) for the coding process itself — reliability of coding is not the same as validity of the underlying reduction.

The note explicitly records that missing information was "statisticamente consideradas irrelevantes" (statistically treated as irrelevant) by the authors, which the authors themselves acknowledge may mask real influences. This is simultaneously an authors'-acknowledged limitation and a methodological assumption worth flagging: treating absence of reported information as equivalent to absence of the underlying factor is a substantive interpretive choice, not a neutral default.

There is a latent tension between the explicit claim that the six patterns are not obligatory linear stages (quote 4) and the substantive framing of "redesign" as the deepest/most complete level and "denial"/"bolt-on" as comparatively superficial. Even if patterns are not temporally sequential, the depth-of-change scale itself is hierarchical, which creates ambiguity about whether the typology is purely descriptive/classificatory or implicitly normative/developmental (i.e., whether redesign is presented as a more desirable outcome that HEIs should aspire to).

Because the underlying sample is built substantially from published, often self-reported cases, and the authors acknowledge a "success bias" limitation, claims about what factors "work" to sustain implementation may be inflated relative to failure modes, which are structurally underrepresented in the source material the meta-analysis draws upon.

The claim recorded in the note that internal stakeholders "são mais poderosos para viabilizar transformações" (are more powerful in enabling transformation) is presented as a finding, but the note does not specify the measurement or coding basis for this comparative claim about relative "power," leaving the evidentiary grounding for this specific claim underspecified within the note itself.
