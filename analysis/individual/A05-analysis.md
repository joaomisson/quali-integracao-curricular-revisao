# A05 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

The literature note reports that the article investigates the process of developing, implementing, and evolving an interdisciplinary undergraduate curriculum focused on Sustainability Science. According to the note, the study is motivated by three explicitly stated gaps/problems: (1) a scarcity of dedicated programs — fewer than 3% of roughly 2,900 four-year U.S. higher-education institutions were found to offer programs containing "sustainable" or "sustentabilidade" (sustainability) in their degree titles; (2) a pattern of superficial or "cosmetic" reformulations, in which institutions rearranged existing courses from other departments rather than designing a single new, centrally focused course, risking trivialization of the field into a cliché; and (3) a widespread perception among academics that designing a dedicated sustainability-science bachelor's program from scratch was an excessively difficult undertaking.

The note also records a stated relevance rationale: Sustainability Science had matured into an autonomous discipline, and the study's setting, New Jersey, faced rigorous state greenhouse-gas mitigation targets and severe pollution problems that the authors frame as urgently requiring a prepared scientific workforce.

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note reports the main objective as presenting and detailing a 10-step process used by Kean University to create a B.S. in Sustainability Science, intended to function as a practical guide encouraging and assisting other higher-education institutions in designing similar programs.

The note explicitly states that the article does not set out to test traditional scientific hypotheses. Instead, the curriculum's restructuring and sequential logic were guided by four foundational questions about the relationship between humanity and the biosphere, reproduced in the note as: (1) what unique characteristics of Earth allowed life to develop and evolve; (2) what humans are doing to compromise those characteristics; (3) why humans behave in ways that compromise Earth's life-supporting characteristics; and (4) what corrective actions are necessary to achieve sustainability.

## 3. Context investigated

The note identifies the empirical context as Kean University, a public higher-education institution in New Jersey, USA, specifically the College of Natural, Applied and Health Sciences (departments of Biology, Chemistry-Physics, Computer Science, and Earth Sciences). The temporal scope, per the note, spans from early program development (opinion surveys conducted in spring and fall 2008) through governmental approval (September 2009), hiring of a program director, curricular revision, founding of the Center for Sustainability Studies (January 2011), and enrollment tracking through a projected fall 2012 cohort.

## 4. Methodological approach

The note characterizes the article as a descriptive case study of curricular and organizational development, situated at a single institution. Data sources reported in the note include: a formulation group (an interdisciplinary faculty committee plus the college dean); a first student opinion survey (spring 2008, convenience sample, 366 students, electronic Qualtrics questionnaire administered at a campus computer terminal during Earth Week); a second student opinion survey (fall 2008, 50 students, mostly from biology and biotechnology, administered by the FOCUS student group to gauge direct interest and career intentions); and an external evaluation in which an independent consultant reviewed the Program Approval Document (PAD). The note also reports ongoing monitoring procedures: direct evaluation through analysis of capstone-course projects (to verify attainment of program goals) and indirect evaluation through a Graduating Student Survey.

DERIVED: As described in the note, this is a single-institution, largely narrative/descriptive case study relying on non-probabilistic convenience samples rather than a comparative, experimental, or statistically representative design. This is an interpretation of the methodology as reported, not a statement the note itself makes about the design's rigor.

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note lists five explicit theoretical/conceptual anchors: (1) the emergence of Sustainability Science as a mature, transdisciplinary research field, attributed to Clark & Dickson (2003), Kates et al. (2001), and Clark (2007); (2) the moral responsibility of universities to lead awareness-building and practical capacity for a viable future, grounded in The Essex Report (1995), Francisco & Steinfeld (2010), and van Dam-Mieras et al. (2008); (3) the stages-of-Environmental-Education model of Hungerford, Peyton, and Wilke (1980) — dividing learning into ecological foundations, conceptual awareness of issues, investigation/evaluation, and action skills — which the note states inspired the curriculum's four guiding questions; (4) the concept of maladaptive human behavior from Maloney and Ward (1973), used to analyze why humans act contrary to their own life-support system; and (5) a critique of curricular trivialization aligned with Blewitt (2004), concerning the risks of using "sustainability" as a label without structural innovation.

## 6. Object being investigated

The primary object investigated, as reported in the note, is the design, approval, implementation, and revision process of a specific undergraduate degree program (curriculum) as an organizational and pedagogical artifact — not a theoretical construct being tested empirically. A secondary object is student demand and receptivity toward a sustainability-focused degree, assessed through the two opinion surveys and later through enrollment and qualitative feedback data.

## 7. Main findings

The note reports the trajectory as synthesized into 10 (with an 11th results item covering post-launch evidence) fundamental steps: (1) definition of program objectives (workforce demand, inquiry-based learning, potential combined MBA option); (2) regional mapping showing no specialized competing sustainability programs in the NY/NJ region at the time; (3) vocational analysis showing high demand for environmental careers (citing the Kaplan career guide); (4) student demand evidence — 99% of Survey 1 respondents supported institutional promotion of sustainability, and 77% of biology students in Survey 2 expressed interest in pursuing sustainability-related careers; (5) creation of an original curriculum with 72 science/mathematics credits and 11 new courses; (6) governmental approval, including unanimous approval by the New Jersey Council of Presidents in September 2009; (7) hiring of a program director (the article's first author), who identified the need to improve the program in response to criticism that it resembled a modified biology degree; (8) a full scope-and-sequence revision making sustainability the priority focus of each individual course (rather than a topic addressed within traditional courses), including new courses such as "Mathematical Principles for Sustainability" and "Calculus Applied to Sustainability," mandatory integrative seminars across all four years, and division into two student concentrations (Earth Systems and Human Systems); (9) founding of the Center for Sustainability Studies (CSS) in January 2011 as an independent academic home facilitating interdisciplinary faculty affiliation from other colleges (e.g., design, arts); (10) institution of continuous outcome-assessment mechanisms integrated into the curriculum; and (11) reported evidence of program success — continuous enrollment growth from 13 students in the inaugural 2010 cohort to 25 (2011), 31 (early 2012), and a projected 40 (fall 2012), alongside strongly positive student feedback on the systemic approach and integrated redesign of science coursework.

## 8. Main arguments

The note reports three central arguments attributed to the authors: (1) sustainability should function as the priority integrating lens for teaching, such that when calculus and science courses are taught within practical sustainability contexts, students better understand and accept the mathematical and theoretical challenges of the coursework; (2) the traditional fragmented educational model — historically rejected by theorists such as Nathaniel Shaler in 1905 — fails to give students a sense of the unity and interconnection of nature; and (3) genuinely innovative and robust training requires breaking down "academic silos" and promoting permanent transdisciplinary forums.

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note records three categories of contribution claimed by the authors: a methodological/practical contribution offering a structured, applied 10-step roadmap intended as a reference for other universities seeking to navigate the bureaucratic and pedagogical challenges of creating their own program; a pedagogical contribution consisting of the concept of teaching rigid traditional scientific content (calculus, chemistry, etc.) within a sustainability context to optimize student engagement; and an institutional-partnerships contribution, namely the creation of the New Jersey Sustainability Science Education Partnership (NJSSEP), enabling automatic transfer agreements for students from two-year technical/technologist programs and outreach connections to high schools.

## 10. Limitations

The note explicitly lists two substantive limitations acknowledged in the article: the initial curriculum too closely resembled a lightly modified Biology or Environmental Studies program, with insufficient practical emphasis on the human and conceptual dimensions specific to sustainability, which necessitated a deep revision led by the newly hired director; and an early lack of intellectual cohesion among cohorts, which led to the creation of mandatory seminars across all years.

The note also explicitly states that the study does not list formal methodological limitations regarding the evaluation of the student questionnaires.

DERIVED: The absence of an authors'-acknowledged methodological limitation regarding the surveys is notable given that, per the note, both surveys relied on convenience/self-selected samples (366 students recruited at a single Earth Week event; 50 students recruited through a sustainability-oriented student group, mostly from biology/biotechnology). This suggests a potential gap between the limitations the article itself foregrounds (curricular design shortcomings) and limitations an external reader might identify in the evidentiary basis for demand claims (sampling bias, absence of response rates, lack of a comparison group). This is an interpretation drawn from the descriptive details in the note, not a limitation the note attributes to the authors themselves.

## 11. Implications

The note reports the following implications: the 10-step process is presented as a replicable practical guide for other institutions; future work is suggested in the form of new sustainability-oriented courses in Art, Music, Society, and Green Chemistry; expansion of regional and state-level collaborations with secondary schools and two-year colleges; and promotion of international conferences and workshops through the Center for Sustainability Studies to engage global academics on transdisciplinarity in the field.

- The distinction between substantive curricular innovation and "cosmetic" or superficial reconfiguration of existing courses under a sustainability label (risk of trivialization/cliché).
- "Academic silos" as a named institutional barrier to transdisciplinary education, and the proposal of dedicated interdisciplinary centers (e.g., a Center for Sustainability Studies) as a structural remedy.
- The concept of maladaptive human behavior (Maloney & Ward, 1973) as an explanatory lens for why humans act contrary to their own life-support systems.
- A staged model of environmental-education learning progression (ecological foundations → conceptual awareness → investigation/evaluation → action skills), used here to structure a curriculum's guiding questions.
- Institutional partnership mechanisms (e.g., automatic transfer agreements between two-year and four-year programs) as a pipeline-building strategy for sustainability-related higher education.
- Continuous, curriculum-embedded outcome assessment (e.g., capstone-based direct evaluation plus graduating-student surveys) as a program-monitoring approach.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

- The article generalizes a 10-step process derived from a single institution operating within a specific state political and regulatory context (New Jersey's greenhouse-gas mitigation goals) into a prescriptive guide for other institutions. The note does not report any discussion of how institutional, regulatory, or resource differences across contexts might limit transferability of the process.
- The central pedagogical argument — that teaching quantitative/scientific content through a sustainability lens improves student comprehension and acceptance of difficulty — is presented in the note as a conclusion, but the reported evidentiary basis appears to be enrollment growth and qualitative student praise rather than a controlled or outcome-based comparison (e.g., no reported data on learning outcomes, grades, or retention before versus after the scope-and-sequence revision described in step 8).
- Both opinion surveys reported in the note used non-probabilistic, convenience/self-selected samples (an Earth Week campus terminal sample and a sample recruited via a sustainability-oriented student group already predisposed toward the topic), which plausibly inflates supportive figures such as the 99% endorsement rate. The note explicitly states that the article does not list formal methodological limitations for these surveys, which is itself a notable gap given the demand-justification role these figures play in the argument.
- The claim of "program success" (step/finding 11) rests on continued enrollment growth over a short window (2010–projected 2012) and positive qualitative feedback; the note does not report longer-term outcome measures (graduation rates, employment outcomes, learning-outcome assessment results) that would more directly substantiate claims of programmatic success.
- The critique of "cosmetic" sustainability programs (recombining existing courses without new course creation) is a strong normative framing that positions the authors' from-scratch approach as inherently superior; the note does not report any direct comparative evaluation between the authors' program and the "cosmetic" alternatives being criticized.

## 17. Missing information

- The note does not report response rates (i.e., the eligible population size relative to the 366 and 50 respondents), demographic composition beyond departmental affiliation, or the exact survey question wording used to elicit the 99% and 77% figures.
- The note does not report details of the capstone-course assessment rubric or criteria used to verify attainment of program learning goals, nor findings from the Graduating Student Survey.
- The note does not report learning-outcome, retention, graduation, or post-graduation employment data for students in the revised program.
- The note does not report the specific criteria or findings of the external consultant's review of the Program Approval Document beyond noting that such a review occurred.
- The note does not report funding sources, author institutional roles beyond "first author became director," or any conflict-of-interest information.
- The note does not report how "sustainability" was operationally defined for survey respondents, which bears on how the demand figures (e.g., 99% support) should be interpreted.
- The note does not report comparative data from other institutions' sustainability programs beyond the general claim that no specialized regional competitors existed at the time of development.
