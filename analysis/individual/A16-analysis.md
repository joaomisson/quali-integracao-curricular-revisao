# A16 — Analytical study profile

## 1. Scope and research problem

The note describes A16 as addressing the development and structuring of undergraduate medical curricula based on theoretical models. The note reports that the study frames its problem around a tension between the need for scientifically grounded, standardized curriculum development and the practical confusion medical educators face when trying to apply the many available theoretical models, given the differences between the stages/steps of each model.

The note reports a specific figure attributed to unspecified prior research: "nearly 40% of medical schools have a mismatch between curriculum content and teaching objectives due to inappropriate selection of generic models." The note also records that the authors identify a gap in existing literature reviews, which — according to the note — focus predominantly on evaluation models and satisfaction indicators, lacking a systematic and comprehensive compilation of the specific theoretical models that guide curriculum development itself (as opposed to its evaluation).

The relevance argument recorded in the note is resource-oriented: inadequate model selection is said to waste human, material, and financial resources, while scientific rigor and standardization in curricular design are presented as directly affecting teaching-learning effectiveness and student knowledge absorption.

## 2. Research objectives/questions

The note records the main objective as integrating a comprehensive curriculum development framework, built from the steps and components of theoretical curriculum development models described in the literature on undergraduate medical education, while also synthesizing the practical problems and points requiring attention during that process.

The research question recorded in the note is: which steps and elements should be followed in a comprehensive and systematic way in curriculum development in medical education.

## 3. Context investigated

The note indicates that the reviewed body of empirical literature spans studies published between 2013 and 2024, drawn from multiple countries, explicitly listed in the note as China, Iran, India, the USA, Brazil, Canada, Ireland, and Australia. This is the context of the 18 empirical studies synthesized by the review, not a single primary-research setting; the review itself is a secondary/integrative study operating over this multi-country literature rather than investigating one institutional or national context directly.

## 4. Methodological approach

The note describes the study as an integrative literature review following the five-stage methodological guidelines of Whittemore and Knafl (2005): problem identification, literature search, data evaluation, data analysis, and presentation of results.

Search and selection process, as recorded in the note:
- Eight electronic databases consulted: PubMed, Embase, The Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CINAHL, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP, searched from database inception through December 31, 2024.
- Eligibility criteria: studies focused on establishing curricula for undergraduate medical students that explicitly used a theoretical model in their design and provided specific process details; restricted to English- or Chinese-language publications.
- Screening yielded 1,672 initial records, reduced to 1,322 after deduplication, 115 selected for full-text review, and 18 empirical articles finally included in the synthesis. Screening disagreements were resolved by consensus or a third reviewer.
- Profile of included studies: 3 qualitative, 6 quantitative, and 9 mixed-methods studies.
- Quality appraisal: performed independently by two researchers using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT, 2018 version); no eligible article was excluded on quality grounds.
- Data analysis: content analysis technique, evaluating logical connections and relationships between the steps of the different models in order to integrate them into a unified framework.

The note does not specify the operational procedure by which "logical connections" between model steps were identified and merged into the four-step framework, beyond naming the technique as content analysis.

## 5. Theoretical/conceptual foundations

The note records the premise that curriculum development is a process of curricular reconstruction influenced by social and academic context (the note quotes the descriptor "social e acadêmico" from the Portuguese-language fichamento, referring to context framed in the original as social and academic).

Ten principal theoretical models are mapped, as listed in the note: Kern's six-step method, the ADDIE model, Fink's integrated course design model, the DACUM (Develop A Curriculum) model, the Taylor model, the Participatory Curriculum Development (PCD) model, a seven-step model, Steketee's four-dimensional framework, the Siragusa model, and the Talbot and Verrinder model.

The note records that these models are segmented into two groups: models centered on student competencies (DACUM, PCD, Siragusa) and models centered on learning objectives (ADDIE, Fink, Taylor, Kern, seven-step, Talbot & Verrinder, Steketee). The note does not indicate whether this two-group typology is the authors' own analytical construction or a classification already established in prior literature.

## 6. Object being investigated

The object investigated, as recorded in the note, is at a meta-level: it is the published theoretical models of curriculum development and the empirical studies that applied them to undergraduate medical curricula, rather than a single curriculum, student population, or institution. The primary analytic units are the 18 empirical articles that explicitly used a named theoretical model and reported development-process details, and the 10 theoretical models these articles instantiate.

## 7. Main findings

The note records the following as main results:

1. An "Integrated Development Framework" (described in the note as Fig. 2) unifying the steps of the 10 analyzed models into a four-step structure:
   - Step 1: Situational analysis of the curriculum — mapping social context, health policy, school structure, and student needs/characteristics; the note records Fink's model as highlighted as the most robust for this phase.
   - Step 2: Definition of curricular objectives — establishing clear goals focused on knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values.
   - Step 3: Design of curricular content — planning of pedagogical content, teaching strategies/methods, and development of educational resources (e.g., handouts, books).
   - Step 4: Curriculum implementation and evaluation — course execution (with attention to motivation and interaction) and structuring of formative (ongoing) and summative (final) assessments.

2. Curricular efficacy: of the 18 articles, 13 tested the proposed curricula in practice. The note records that all of these implementations reported significant quantitative efficacy, with substantial increases in theoretical knowledge and practical skill scores post-course.

3. Deficiencies and dissatisfaction factors, drawn from qualitative student feedback in the empirical studies: inadequate scheduling (courses too short or confusingly timed, harming comprehensive/systematic learning); insufficient offline practical activities (particularly in online/hybrid teaching, where lack of in-person simulation and real patient contact hindered translating theory into practical skill); and inappropriate or inadequate textbook selection (absence of standardized textbooks impeding review, and student preference for concise handouts over dense research articles early in learning).

## 8. Main arguments

The note records several argumentative claims attributed to the authors:

- Model selection should align directly with the context and objectives of the course/discipline — the note gives examples: Kern as highly specific to general medical curricula, Fink as ideal for integrating theoretical and affective learning in general higher education, and DACUM as focused on professional-competency analysis in technical courses.
- The proposed four-step framework is argued to simplify and accelerate curriculum development relative to traditional models — described in the note as containing "intricate procedures" and "protracted development cycles" — by eliminating redundancies while preserving systematicity. The note also records the claim that the framework fills a gap left by theories that focused only on process sequencing without detailing the internal content of each step ("link").
- Curriculum developers should attend strictly to class time, in-person practical activities, and textbook quality to achieve proposed implementation outcomes.
- Quantitative data is argued to highlight problems, while qualitative analysis is argued to be needed to ascertain their causes, jointly supporting ongoing curricular improvement — recorded in the note as a direct quotation: "Quantitative data can highlight issues, while qualitative analysis can ascertain the causes, thereby enhancing the curriculum and promoting its ongoing development."

## 9. Authors' contributions

The note records three categories of contribution attributed to the authors:

- Theoretical: the first systematic compilation of curriculum design models in medical education, presenting an integrated four-step framework said to resolve the dispersion of prior theories.
- Practical: a practical, evidence-based reference for educators to guide course-design decisions, plus an empirical warning about common errors compromising student satisfaction.
- Methodological: a detailed methodological example of integrating qualitative and quantitative data within integrative reviews in health education, using the MMAT framework.

## 10. Limitations

The note records two limitations attributed to the authors themselves:

- Exclusive inclusion of English- and Chinese-language publications, which may have excluded relevant curricular evidence published in other languages.
- Exclusive focus on studies that explicitly mentioned an applied theoretical model, which may have excluded curriculum-development approaches or models not yet formally reported in the medical education literature.

The note records that the authors indicate a need for future field research to empirically validate the proposed four-step integrated framework in academic medical practice — implying, though not stated as an explicit limitation label, that the framework itself has not yet been empirically tested as a unified whole within this study.

## 11. Implications

The note frames the four-step framework as intended to guide future pedagogical curricular reforms in a simplified and high-quality way, grounded in the synthesized scientific evidence. The note also records the implication that developers should specifically attend to scheduling/time adequacy, in-person practical activities, and textbook/material quality as implementation-stage priorities, since these were identified as sources of student dissatisfaction across the reviewed studies.

## 12. Concepts relevant across the corpus

- Distinction between curriculum models centered on student competencies versus models centered on learning objectives.
- Situational analysis as a foundational first step in curriculum design (mapping social/political/institutional context and student characteristics/needs).
- Formative versus summative assessment as complementary evaluation components within curriculum implementation.
- Mixed-methods curricular evaluation logic: quantitative data as a signal of problems, qualitative data as an explanatory mechanism for causes.
- Scheduling/time adequacy as an implementation-stage barrier to curricular effectiveness.
- Adequacy of hands-on/in-person practical activities as a factor in translating theoretical knowledge into practical skill, particularly relevant in online/hybrid teaching modalities.
- Adequacy and format of instructional materials (standardized textbooks versus concise handouts versus dense research articles) as a factor in learner satisfaction.
- Integrative review methodology (Whittemore and Knafl, 2005) as a synthesis approach for heterogeneous qualitative/quantitative/mixed-methods literature.
- Use of the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) for quality appraisal across study designs.
- Model-context fit as a selection criterion for choosing among multiple available theoretical frameworks.

## 16. Tensions or assumptions worth challenging

- The note records a statistic ("nearly 40% of medical schools have a mismatch between curriculum content and teaching objectives") attributed only to unspecified "research data" ("Some research data show that..."). The note does not identify the source study, sample, or methodology behind this figure, so its evidentiary weight cannot be assessed from the note alone; it functions rhetorically to establish problem severity without traceable support within the note.
- The claim that all 13 implementations tested in practice reported significant quantitative efficacy raises a possible selection/publication-bias concern: the eligibility criteria required studies that explicitly used a theoretical model and reported process details, which may itself select for published success stories (studies with null or negative results, or those not explicitly framed around a named model, would have been excluded by design). The note does not address this possibility.
- The four-step integrated framework is presented as simplifying and accelerating curriculum development while preserving systematicity, but the note indicates this framework has not yet been empirically validated as a unified whole (future field validation is called for). The claimed advantages (simplification, redundancy elimination) are thus argumentative/synthetic claims about the review's own output rather than findings independently tested within the study.
- The note does not clarify how the four-step framework relates to or resolves the prior two-group typology (competency-centered versus objective-centered models) — whether the new framework favors one orientation, merges both without tension, or leaves the distinction analytically unresolved.
- The restriction to English- and Chinese-language literature, combined with the requirement for explicit theoretical-model usage, may bias the resulting "comprehensive" framework toward curricular traditions and institutional contexts that are more likely to publish in these languages and more likely to formally theorize their design process, potentially underrepresenting informal, iterative, or regionally specific curriculum-design practices.
- The note does not specify how "logical connections" between the ten source models' steps were operationalized during content analysis to produce the four-step framework, leaving the internal synthesis procedure itself under-described relative to the search/screening procedure, which is reported in much greater methodological detail.

## 17. Missing information

- The source, sample, and methodology behind the "nearly 40%" curriculum-objective mismatch statistic are not specified in the note.
- The specific quantitative measures used to establish "significant quantitative efficacy" (e.g., effect sizes, statistical tests, comparison groups, pre/post design details) are not described beyond "substantial increase" in scores.
- The mapping between each of the 18 included empirical studies and which of the 10 theoretical models each one used is not detailed in the note.
- Sample sizes, institutional settings, or student population characteristics of the 18 included empirical studies are not provided.
- The specific operational procedure of the content-analysis synthesis (how model steps were compared, merged, or excluded to build the four-step framework) is not detailed.
- Funding sources or conflict-of-interest disclosures for the review are not mentioned in the note.
- Whether the two-group typology (competency-centered vs. objective-centered) is original to this review or drawn from prior literature is not stated.
