# Evidence Matrix — Corpus A01–A16

**Stage 2, Step 1.** Standardized per-study extraction from the 16 completed
individual analyses under `analysis/individual/`.

## What this document is

This is an **intermediate representation**, not an interpretation. Every entry is a
re-mapping of one `Axx-analysis.md` file into a common 22-field schema so that the
studies can be compared in later steps. It deliberately contains **no cross-study
conclusions, no themes, no clusters, and no synthesis**. Nothing here was read from
`literature/`, `dissertation/`, `analysis/dissertation/`, or `analysis/critique/`.

Each entry is traceable to exactly one source: entry `Axx` derives solely from
`analysis/individual/Axx-analysis.md`. Studies were extracted in isolation from one
another; no entry references any other study.

## Evidence conventions

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *(unmarked prose)* | EXPLICIT — stated directly in the source analysis. |
| `[DERIVED]` | A reasonable interpretation drawn from what the source states, with its reasoning attached. Not asserted by the source. |
| `NOT REPORTED` | The information would be relevant but the source does not supply it. Deliberately not inferred. |
| `NOT APPLICABLE` | The field is meaningless for this study type (e.g. institutional context for a literature review). |

Field 22 ("Note-layer caveats") is the per-study **UNSUPPORTED ledger**: what the
underlying literature note failed to preserve about that study. It is the standing
record of what cannot legitimately be claimed about a study in later steps.

Several source notes rendered quotations in Portuguese even where the original
article is English-language. Those passages are paraphrased in English here, and each
affected entry says so in field 22.

## A01 — Two-year non-linear participative curricular review in a Merchandising/Fashion Design department

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study addresses curricular review and reform in higher education applied to a Merchandising and Fashion Design (MFD) program. The motivating problem operates at two levels. Practical/managerial: institutions face continuous pressure to keep curricula current and relevant, driven by accreditation requirements and rapid market change. Conceptual/methodological: curricular review is commonly described in the literature as exhausting, complex, and emotionally draining for faculty, generating resistance and interpersonal conflict rooted in differing views and power dynamics; the authors identify a gap in that most proposed curricular reform models are strictly linear, which ignores that institutional practice is messy, dynamic, and iterative.

**2. Object of investigation** — The curricular review process itself — its design, sequencing, and stakeholder dynamics — rather than a specific measurable educational outcome such as student learning gains or employment rates. Secondary objects captured within this process: stakeholder perceptions (students, alumni) gathered via survey; faculty self-perceptions and interpersonal dynamics captured through self-assessment questionnaires and reflective meetings; and comparative curricular structures at peer institutions used as an external benchmark.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — Single institutional context: the Merchandising and Fashion Design (MFD) department of a public, metropolitan, medium-to-large university in the Midwestern United States, with 24,390 students enrolled as of fall 2018. The reform spans two years and involves the department's full faculty body, plus external stakeholder groups (recent graduates/seniors and alumni) and comparison data from peer, competitive, and aspirant institutions. Single-case, single-discipline, single-institution.

**4. Study or article type** — Descriptive case study using a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative survey data with qualitative analysis of perceptions and discussions. No formal, statistically testable hypotheses are presented; the article instead reports practical guiding questions posed to faculty during meetings. [DERIVED] The design is practitioner-driven and largely qualitative/process-descriptive, with a modest quantitative component (descriptive statistics on a 50-respondent survey) embedded within it — reasoning: no inferential statistical testing, control condition, or independent evaluator external to the department is described.

**5. Methodological approach** — Participants and data: the entire departmental faculty participated actively in plenary meetings and a self-assessment process; a structured opinion survey was completed by 50 respondents (18 graduating seniors and 32 alumni), a 49% response rate out of 103 invited; curricular data from peer institutions were collected via internet research. Data collection occurred across two years. Year 1 (industry focus and internal diagnosis): documentary research on external curricula found online; faculty travel to interview alumni in person across various U.S. cities about technological trends and emerging careers; mixed questionnaires administered to students and alumni/employers; an internal Continuous Improvement Process (CIP) self-assessment questionnaire completed individually by each faculty member regarding their own course syllabi. Year 2 (benchmarking and peer review): systematic collection of comparison data (program names, required credit hours, course syllabi) from peer institutions; intensive debate meetings structured around open-ended question scripts addressing individual courses and the curriculum as a whole. Analytical procedures: direct comparative (benchmarking) analysis between the department's curricular structure and reference programs; thematic content analysis and simple descriptive statistics applied to student/alumni survey data; structured reflective sessions governed by pre-established conduct norms (mutual listening, respect for ideas, no interruptions) used to manage disagreement and build consensus.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Several distinct conceptual anchors rather than a single unified framework: the tripartite model of Garner and Buckley (1988) as the guiding structure, holding that a strong curriculum must focus on three core constituencies — educators who deliver it, students who experience it, and employers who hire graduates; continuous environmental scanning, attributed to Laughlin and Kean (1995), to identify technological, demographic, and market trends in fashion; a Continuous Improvement Process (CIP) inspired by Deming's (2000) continuous-quality model; guidelines and competencies from the International Textile and Apparel Association (ITAA) as general goal parameters; a body of theory on behavioral barriers to change, addressing resistance among senior faculty (comfort with prepared courses) and junior faculty (time constraints tied to promotion and tenure), and departmental political/power dynamics; peer review concepts attributed to Osborne and Purkey (1995), on the importance of mutual evaluation among faculty. [DERIVED] This is an eclectic combination of applied management/quality concepts and organizational-behavior concepts, loosely organized around the Garner and Buckley model rather than integrated into a single explanatory theory — reasoning: no integrating theoretical statement is described; the foundations function as practical scaffolding.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — The two-year deductive, participative, iterative process undertaken by one department's faculty (single case, single discipline, single institution). Secondary units: stakeholder perceptions via the 50-respondent survey; faculty self-assessment via CIP questionnaires per faculty member per course syllabus; comparative curricular structures at peer institutions.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — A two-year, systematic, non-linear, deductive, participative, and iterative curricular review process, structured as two complementary phases (Year 1: industry focus/internal diagnosis; Year 2: benchmarking/peer review) and guided by practical questions posed to faculty during meetings, paraphrased as: whether the program offered the most up-to-date curriculum and served students best; what differentiated the program from others; whether the most current industry techniques were taught; how up-to-date information was delivered in the classroom; and how individual faculty kept their own materials current. Concrete process outputs: addition of a logistics/supply-chain course to the core curriculum (direct result of benchmarking); redesign of the capstone course into an experiential real-market simulation called "Senior Market," in which design students develop full collections evaluated by a three-person industry-expert panel while merchandising students handle market positioning and presentation to an external public audience; identification of an imminent need for new industrial machinery and software.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — Full departmental faculty (plenary meetings, self-assessment); 50 survey respondents (18 graduating seniors, 32 alumni) of 103 invited (49% response rate); peer, competitive, and aspirant institutions (compared via internet research on curricula); a three-person panel of industry experts evaluating "Senior Market" student collections; an external public audience for merchandising presentations; academic managers/facilitators who must navigate faculty resistance.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The Garner and Buckley (1988) tripartite model (educators, students, employers) as the organizing structure for weighing input; the five practical guiding questions (paraphrased in field 8) used in faculty meetings; alternating between a macro perspective (the program as a whole) and a micro perspective (detailed analysis of each individual course), presented by the authors as what ensures a coherent and genuinely relevant curriculum.

**11. Contextual conditions** — Public, metropolitan, medium-to-large university in the Midwestern United States; 24,390 students enrolled as of fall 2018; single MFD department; accreditation requirements and rapid market/technological change in the fashion field as external pressures driving the review.

**12. Barriers** — Resistance to change among senior faculty (comfort with prepared courses) and junior faculty (time constraints tied to promotion and tenure processes); departmental political/power dynamics; faculty fatigue in the face of an overwhelming volume of data; severe budgetary and internal resource constraints; difficult debates marked by resistance to change and departmental power disputes.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — Structured conduct norms (mutual listening, respect for ideas, no interruptions) governing reflective sessions to manage disagreement and build consensus; the participative, multi-stakeholder nature of the process; peer review (Osborne & Purkey, 1995) as a consensus-building mechanism; benchmarking against peer/competitive/aspirant institutions as an external legitimation device; the internal CIP self-assessment; alternating macro/micro analysis as a structuring device.

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — Year 1 mechanisms: documentary research on external curricula online; faculty travel to interview alumni in person across U.S. cities on technological trends and emerging careers; mixed questionnaires to students and alumni/employers; individual CIP self-assessment questionnaires per faculty member on course syllabi. Year 2 mechanisms: systematic peer-institution data collection (program names, required credit hours, course syllabi); intensive debate meetings structured around open-ended question scripts on individual courses and the curriculum as a whole. Resulting concrete changes: logistics/supply-chain course integrated into the core curriculum; capstone course redesigned into "Senior Market."

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — NOT REPORTED as an achieved or evaluated outcome within this study. The note records only the authors' suggested future work: investigating the long-term effectiveness of institutionalizing the curricular changes adopted, and exploring post-reform assessment of learning — [DERIVED] this suggests that institutionalization/sustainability had not yet been evaluated within the reported study, since it is framed as a direction for future research rather than a finding.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - The survey of students and alumni indicated strong demand for greater incorporation of technology into courses, addition of logistics and supply-chain content, and implementation of a textiles laboratory.
  - Benchmarking confirmed that the absence of a logistics course was a real curricular gap relative to competing programs; as a direct result, a logistics/supply-chain course was integrated into the program's core curriculum.
  - The capstone course was redesigned into "Senior Market," an experiential real-market simulation in which design students develop full collections evaluated by a three-person industry-expert panel, while merchandising students handle market positioning and presentation to an external public audience.
  - The process identified an imminent need to acquire new industrial machinery and software to keep courses aligned with current market technologies.
  - Despite difficult debates marked by resistance to change and departmental power disputes, adherence to listening norms and the participative nature of the process are reported to have re-established more honest relationships and increased mutual understanding among faculty from different specialties (merchandising and design) — described as an unexpected behavioral benefit.
  - The technology-integration and textiles-laboratory demands identified by students and alumni are not reported as having been acted upon or resolved; only the logistics course addition and the capstone redesign are explicitly described as concrete curricular outcomes.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - Successful curricular reform must abandon the rigidity of purely linear models and instead embrace an iterative, cyclic, and adaptive process that mirrors how reform actually unfolds inside institutions.
  - Facilitators and academic managers must understand the intrinsic motivations and behavioral barriers that generate resistance among junior and senior faculty in order to navigate difficult conversations constructively.
  - Alternating between a macro perspective (the program as a whole) and a micro perspective (detailed analysis of each individual course) is what ensures faculty build a coherent and genuinely relevant curriculum.
  - Student success and employability are treated as the ultimate validation metric for the reform project — though such outcome data are not reported as having been collected within this study.

**18. Contribution** — Theoretical/methodological: presentation of a non-linear framework, described as innovative and replicable, built on two complementary phases and integrating the perspectives of multiple stakeholder groups (faculty, alumni, market, academia). Practical/organizational: evidence that adopting transparent behavioral norms and peer review reduces faculty defensiveness, resulting in re-established, healthier professional relationships aligned around departmental goals.

**19. Limitations** — The authors' own limitations discussion, as reported, does not detail deep methodological limitations regarding the sample or the model; it records practical and logistical challenges: faculty fatigue in the face of an overwhelming volume of data, political/generational barriers to change, and severe budgetary and internal resource constraints. [DERIVED] Additional limitations beyond what the authors themselves discuss (reasoning developed further under field 21): single-case/single-institution design constraining the "replicability" claim; a modest, self-selected 49% survey response rate (50 of 103 invited) with no reported non-response bias check; no described external/independent evaluation of the reported relational/behavioral improvements; and no reported outcome data (student success/employability) despite this being named the study's final validation metric.

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — Tripartite stakeholder framing of curriculum design (educators, students, employers); environmental scanning; Continuous Improvement Process (CIP) applied to academic curricula; behavioral-barriers framing of resistance to change, differentiated by career stage; peer review as consensus-building/mutual-evaluation mechanism; benchmarking against peer/competitive/aspirant institutions as gap-identification method; linear vs. iterative/cyclic/messy reform-process conceptualizations; alternating macro/micro levels of analysis; curricular reform as ongoing process rather than discrete event; participatory governance and multi-stakeholder consultation; behavioral/relational outcomes of change processes (distinct from structural/curricular outcomes); structured conduct norms as conflict-management mechanism; accreditation and market-relevance pressure as external drivers; case study as evidentiary basis for generalizable process frameworks.

**21. Tensions or assumptions** — Labeling tension: the process is described as "deductive" while also heavily participative and iterative, incorporating extensive bottom-up stakeholder input (student/alumni surveys, faculty self-assessment, open-ended debate); a deductive process would typically proceed from established theoretical premises (e.g., the Garner and Buckley tripartite model) applied downward, whereas the process as described appears to combine top-down structuring with substantial inductive, ground-level data gathering and negotiation — whether "deductive" accurately characterizes the process, or it is better understood as a hybrid, is not resolved in the note. Tension between the claim to offer a "replicable" framework and its evidentiary basis: a single department, single institution, single two-year cycle, described through a descriptive case study without any reported comparison case or independent replication; the replicability claim rests on the authors' own characterization rather than demonstrated cross-context application. The positive relational/behavioral findings are reported without a described independent measurement instrument or external evaluator; given that the faculty who underwent the process include or overlap with those reporting on its results, this raises an unresolved question about whether these findings reflect structured evaluation versus a retrospective, participant-generated account. The study designates student success and employability as the "final validation metric," yet no such outcome data are reported as collected or analyzed, creating a gap between the articulated evaluative standard and the evidence actually produced. The 49% response rate (50 of 103 invited) is treated in the findings as indicative of stakeholder demand, without any reported assessment of whether the responding subgroup differed systematically from non-respondents — an assumption worth flagging when weighing how representative the identified "gaps" are of the broader population.

**22. Note-layer caveats specific to this study** — The note does not report: the number of faculty members involved in the review process; demographic or role-based composition of survey respondents beyond the senior/alumni split; validation procedures (if any) for the survey instrument or the internal CIP self-assessment questionnaire; the specific statistical methods beyond "simple descriptive statistics"; how consensus among faculty was operationally determined or measured during structured reflective sessions; whether the identified technology-integration and textiles-laboratory demands (as distinct from the logistics course and capstone redesign) were acted upon; any quantitative or qualitative data on student learning outcomes or graduate employability following the reform; the specific instruments or criteria used by the three-person industry panel to evaluate "Senior Market" student collections; and any account of dissenting faculty perspectives not resolved through the consensus process. The note also does not specify page- or section-level detail on how the "deductive" characterization is theoretically justified by the authors. Additionally, the five practical guiding questions posed to faculty (see field 8) are rendered in the source note as direct quotations in Portuguese rather than in the article's presumed original English; they have been paraphrased into English here rather than reproduced as quotations, to avoid implying an inserted translation.


## A02 — Filling a prognostication curricular gap at UVA School of Medicine using Theory of Change

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The article addresses curriculum mapping and curricular revision in medical education. Curriculum mapping is described as a consolidated tool for identifying where and how frequently competencies, knowledge, and attitudes are taught and assessed within a program. A critical gap in the literature the authors draw on: although mapping successfully identifies curricular gaps and redundancies, institutions reportedly face serious difficulties planning and implementing practical, actionable strategies to fill those gaps; prior studies frequently recommend revisions without detailing how, or at which specific points in the curriculum, interventions and assessments should be introduced. The specific problem investigated is how to fill an unexpected gap identified in prognosis/prognostication skills within the undergraduate medical curriculum at the University of Virginia (UVA), using a structured approach.

**2. Object of investigation** — The process of curricular gap identification and remediation itself — specifically the prognostication competency (Competency No. 11) within the UVA undergraduate medical curriculum, treated as a case study in applying the Theory of Change model to curricular revision. The unit of analysis is institutional/programmatic (learning objectives across curriculum phases), not individual learners or a comparative sample of institutions.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — Single institution: the undergraduate medical education program at the University of Virginia School of Medicine. The process was led by a three-person committee: the Director of Curricular Integration and Development, the Associate Dean for Curriculum, and the Senior Advisor for Educational Affairs. The curricular context spans three phases: Phase 1 (pre-clinical, 18 months, approximately 6,800 session-level learning objectives across 13 disciplines); Phase 2 (clinical clerkships, 16 required clerkships, 781 aggregated objectives); Phase 3 (post-clerkship, 63 courses including 17 advanced clinical electives, 642 objectives). The mapping process began in 2019, using the institution's custom learning management system, VMed.

**4. Study or article type** — Descriptive case report focused on curricular development and continuous quality improvement (CQI). No formal hypotheses or research questions in the traditional hypothesis-testing format are formulated; instead the study investigates resolving a mismatch between the intended (planned/written), delivered (taught/tested), and experienced (learned) curriculum in relation to Competency No. 11 ("Formulate a prognosis..."). No comparison group, control condition, or externally validated outcome measure beyond the mapping counts and the authors' own account of implementation is described.

**5. Methodological approach** — (1) Mapping session-level objectives to discipline-level objectives (an intermediate layer of 20-25 objectives per discipline) and subsequently to the program's 12 Competency Goals ("Twelve Competencies of the Contemporary Physician"), all cataloged and analyzed via VMed. (2) A targeted database search for the term "prognosis" and for Competency No. 11, supplemented by searches for synonyms such as "trajectory of illness" and "goals of care," to rigorously map the theme's presence across all phases. (3) Application of the Theory of Change model to structure the response to the identified gap: mobilizing resources (existing curricular time, assessment materials such as written exams, performance evaluations, OSCEs, and academic leadership), designing interventions (restructuring objectives in the Internal Medicine clerkship and Phase 3 required disciplines), and tracking short- and medium-term outcomes.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Four conceptual pillars: (1) curriculum mapping, conceptualized as a method to align the planned curriculum with the program's general educational objectives and to ensure continuous quality improvement and accreditation compliance; (2) Theory of Change (ToC), a design and evaluation model for complex systems (such as undergraduate medical education), characterized by working backwards from a long-term goal to establish a logical sequence of intermediate outcomes (short- and medium-term), resources, interventions, and underlying assumptions; (3) the intended/delivered/experienced curriculum model, recognizing that curricular misalignments occur when there is a lack of synchrony between instructors' objectives, the lessons and assessments actually delivered, and students' perceptions of their own learning; (4) Bloom's Taxonomy, used specifically to ensure new curricular objectives were written at higher cognitive levels (analysis, evaluation, creation) rather than focusing only on simple recall.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — Institutional/programmatic: learning objectives mapped across the three curriculum phases at a single institution (UVA), not individual learners and not a comparative sample of institutions.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — A diagnostic-to-remediation process for a single named competency gap. Diagnostic phase (2018–2019): systematic mapping and targeted keyword search located the prognostication competency's presence (or absence) across all three curriculum phases. Remediation phase: application of the Theory of Change model — working backward from the long-term goal, mobilizing existing resources, designing interventions restructuring objectives in the Internal Medicine clerkship and Phase 3 required disciplines, and tracking short/medium-term outcomes including modified performance assessments in Phases 2 and 3.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — The three-person leading committee (Director of Curricular Integration and Development, Associate Dean for Curriculum, Senior Advisor for Educational Affairs); institutional academic leadership as a mobilized resource; students and faculty as sources of satisfaction/evaluation data (referenced but not shown to have flagged the gap); implicitly, clerkship and Phase 3 course directors whose objectives were restructured.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — Presence/absence and frequency of objectives mapped to Competency No. 11 across the three phases; alignment of new objectives with higher Bloom's-taxonomy cognitive levels (analysis, evaluation, creation) rather than recall; logical sequencing under ToC (long-term goal → intermediate outcomes → resources/interventions → underlying assumptions); tying assessments (written exams, performance evaluations, OSCEs) to restructured objectives.

**11. Contextual conditions** — Single, well-resourced academic medical center (UVA) with a custom institutional learning management system (VMed) enabling systematic objective-level mapping; an established competency framework ("Twelve Competencies of the Contemporary Physician") already in place prior to this intervention; institutional policy requiring assessments to be tied to objectives.

**12. Barriers** — Inertia and natural resistance to change in medical schools, addressed by the ToC process (per the authors' argument in field 17); the note's first declared limitation implies a barrier internal to the diagnostic method itself — reliance on written objectives without auditing actually-administered exams/assessments, risking under- or over-counting the true gap.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — The Theory of Change model's structuring effect: forcing explicit articulation of hidden assumptions, supporting selection of multiple systemic interventions instead of superficial "patches," improving transparent communication with stakeholders, and functioning as a roadmap of clear temporal milestones; institutional policy tying assessments to objectives, invoked by the authors as making unaccounted-for teaching "unlikely"; the existing VMed mapping infrastructure enabling rigorous, searchable cataloging of objectives.

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — Restructuring learning objectives in the Internal Medicine clerkship (Phase 2) and in Phase 3 required disciplines to explicitly incorporate prognostication; rewriting objectives to target higher Bloom's-taxonomy levels; modifying student performance assessments in Phases 2 and 3 to formally include evaluation of prognostication ability; mobilizing existing curricular time and existing assessment materials (written exams, performance evaluations, OSCEs) and academic leadership as ToC resources.

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — NOT REPORTED as a measured outcome. The note records only two forward-looking items, not accomplished within the study: integrating prognostication into OSCEs (explicitly stated as not yet accomplished), and encouraging educators at other health-sciences schools to test and use the ToC approach at different scales and on different topics. [DERIVED] No tracking of whether the restructured objectives or modified assessments persisted beyond the initial implementation cycle is reported, so durability/sustainability of the change is not established by the note.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - Diagnostic phase (2018–2019): in Phase 1, competencies related to the science of medicine, exam interpretation, and diagnosis (Competencies 2, 6, 9, and 10) were predominant, while there was an extreme scarcity ("paucity") of objectives dedicated to formulating prognoses (Competency 11).
  - In Phase 2, of 781 objectives analyzed across 16 required clerkships, only 7 objectives directly referenced formulating a patient's prognosis.
  - In Phase 3, of 642 learning objectives across 63 disciplines, only 1 objective mapped to the prognostication competency.
  - These quantitative data are reported by the authors as having empirically confirmed the existence of a real gap in the planned/written curriculum.
  - Following application of the ToC model, the maps were successfully filled across all three curriculum phases, and student performance assessments in Phases 2 and 3 were modified to formally include evaluation of prognostication ability.
  - The authors interpret that institutional evaluations by students and faculty had never flagged the topic as deficient (suggesting the "experienced curriculum" was adequate in clinical practice), while the "planned curriculum" (written) seriously failed to document or evidence this training — illustrating that curricular misalignment can be invisible without rigorous mapping.

**17. Principal arguments** —

## A03 — Multi-criteria decision-making model for sustainability-oriented accounting curriculum planning

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study addresses the Program Course Planning Problem (PCPP) at the operational level, integrating Education for Sustainable Development (ESD) principles into accounting education. The motivating problem is a scarcity of operational studies and quantitative methodologies for structuring curricula; course planning is traditionally conducted manually and intuitively, without standardized processes or explicit consideration of the sustainability competencies demanded by the labor market. A criticism attributed to the field: accounting programs have neglected soft skills and topics such as corporate responsibility, social justice, and environmental protection. The topic's relevance is framed around the claim that course planning determines the quality of academic programs, institutional reputation, and the efficiency of limited educational resource use, while preparing future decision-makers to address complex global challenges — presented as justification for treating curriculum planning as a decision problem worth formal modeling rather than an administrative afterthought.

**2. Object of investigation** — The curriculum-planning process itself — specifically, the joint problem of (a) selecting which courses (mandatory and elective) should compose an accounting program based on their contribution to sustainability competencies, and (b) scheduling those selected courses across semesters in a balanced way. The unit of analysis is a single institutional curriculum (one accounting program at one Chinese university), evaluated through the judgments of a small expert panel rather than through student learning outcomes or longitudinal program performance.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — Undergraduate accounting program at an unnamed Chinese university. The applied case involved a panel of five experts: one accounting department head, three accounting professors, and one professional from a local company, all reported to have more than ten years of experience. The scheduling stage concerned the last two years (four semesters) of the program's curriculum.

**4. Study or article type** — Quantitative empirical case study proposing and applying a multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) support model; no formal statistical hypotheses were formulated, only practical research questions: (1) how to quantify the relevance of different academic courses for building specific sustainability competencies; (2) how to balance credit loads and difficulty levels of selected courses across a semester curriculum.

**5. Methodological approach** — Structured in three sequential phases. Phase I (Competency Prioritization via BWM): the five-expert panel performed pairwise comparisons to determine the relative importance of the eight UNESCO sustainability competencies within accounting education. Phase II (Course Selection and Ranking via FFR): experts rated, on a 1–10 scale, the degree of relation between 53 candidate courses (compiled from the existing curriculum and from other leading universities' programs) and the eight competencies; the Fuzzy Filter Ranking (FFR) algorithm produced a "sustainability mapping index" per course, and top-ranked courses were selected until reaching the credit threshold per module — 19 of 27 candidate mandatory courses and 16 of 26 candidate elective courses. Phase III (Course Scheduling via MCGP): the 35 selected courses were entered into a Multi-Choice Goal Programming (MCGP) mathematical model, executed in LINGO 18.0, to distribute courses across four semesters while respecting prerequisites, credit limits, and elective constraints. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted, testing ten different competency-weight scenarios.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Three conceptual pillars combining strategic management, sustainable education, and operations research: He-Xie Management Theory (HXMT), attributed to Youmin Xi (1989), drawing on Eastern and Western philosophical concepts to address complex organizational problems under uncertainty, divided into the He Principle (an evolutionary mechanism of human coordination and stakeholder engagement) and the Xie Principle (a control mechanism focused on rational process design and mathematical optimization); the UNESCO Sustainability Competences Framework, comprising eight domains — Systems thinking (C1), Anticipatory (C2), Normative (C3), Strategic (C4), Collaboration (C5), Critical thinking (C6), Self-awareness (C7), Integrated problem-solving (C8); and three MCDM methods — Best Worst Method (BWM, Rezaei 2015), described as requiring fewer pairwise comparisons and offering greater consistency than AHP; Fuzzy Filter Ranking (FFR, Chang and Ku 2021), applied to handle the imprecise nature of human judgment in ranking and selecting courses; and Multi-Choice Goal Programming (MCGP, Chang 2008), used for scheduling to allow multiple aspiration levels and avoid underestimating complex decisions.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — A single institutional curriculum: one accounting program at one Chinese university, with course selection and scheduling decisions evaluated through the judgments of a five-member expert panel. NOT APPLICABLE: individual-learner-level or multi-institution comparative analysis — neither is part of the design.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — A three-phase quantitative planning process (not a review of an existing implemented curriculum but a prospective design/selection process): competency weighting (BWM) → course-to-competency mapping and ranking (FFR), selecting 35 of 53 candidate courses → semester scheduling of the selected courses (MCGP) subject to prerequisite, credit, and elective constraints, followed by a ten-scenario sensitivity analysis on competency weights to test selection stability.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — The five-member expert panel (one accounting department head, three accounting professors, one industry professional with a local company), described in the note as the operational instantiation of the He Principle's "stakeholder engagement." **10. **11. Contextual conditions** — Single, unnamed Chinese university's undergraduate accounting program; scheduling scope restricted to the last two years (four semesters) of the curriculum; a pre-existing pool of 53 candidate courses drawn from the current curriculum and from programs at other "leading" universities.

**12. Barriers** — [DERIVED] The note does not report barriers in the organizational-resistance sense; the closest analogues are methodological constraints treated by the authors as limitations rather than barriers encountered during the process: group aggregation bias in expert-judgment elicitation, a single-level (non-branched) competency criteria structure, and restriction of the scheduling model to semester-level (not daily/room-level) allocation.

## A04 — Faculty-administrator collaboration during a four-year comprehensive curriculum reform at a theological seminary

**2. **3. Educational / institutional context** — Ashland Theological Seminary (ATS), described as the graduate division of Ashland University in Ohio, USA, and characterized as the largest seminary in the state.
