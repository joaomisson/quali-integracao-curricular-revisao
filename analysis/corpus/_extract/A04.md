## A04 — Faculty-administrator collaboration during a four-year comprehensive curriculum reform at a theological seminary

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study examines a four-year collaborative curriculum revision process jointly conducted by faculty and administrators at Ashland Theological Seminary (ATS), a U.S. higher-education institution of theological education. The motivating problem is twofold: first, despite curriculum being considered fundamental to institutional well-being and effectiveness, "little attention has been given to the evolution of curriculum and its review and transformation in the institutions" (quoted in the note); second, existing literature on curriculum governance is described as concentrating almost exclusively on the faculty's role, leaving a gap regarding shared governance between faculty and administrators in comprehensive curriculum reform processes. Radical curricular reforms are described as rare and demanding because they require significant time and broad active participation, colliding with the traditional culture of higher education — characterized by faculty individualism, extreme autonomy, and disciplinary fragmentation. The study's relevance is framed as filling this gap by empirically documenting a real four-year case of radical change, offering practical insight into how interdepartmental collaboration can be organized and how human and structural resistance can be addressed.

**2. Object of investigation** — The lived experience and collaborative dynamics of a ten-person mixed faculty-administrator team during a four-year comprehensive curriculum revision at a single theological seminary. The unit of analysis is the review team's process and its members' interpretations of that process, rather than the curriculum content itself or measurable student-learning outcomes.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — Ashland Theological Seminary (ATS), described as the graduate division of Ashland University in Ohio, USA, and characterized as the largest seminary in the state. The object of study is a single four-year curriculum reform process within this institution.

**4. Study or article type** — Single qualitative case study guided by a Grounded Theory methodological perspective; exploratory and inductive, with the explicit aim of generating theory from field data. No formal hypotheses were formulated. Two research questions, framed from the perspective of a higher-education administrator: (1) how did the curriculum review team experience the comprehensive curriculum review process; (2) how did faculty and administration collaborate during this review process. The primary objective was investigating and deriving meaning from the experience of the phenomenon of comprehensive curriculum review, with a secondary objective of deepening understanding of the faculty-administration collaboration process.

**5. Methodological approach** — Participants: 10 members of the curriculum review team (5 faculty and 5 administrators) who served throughout the four years, including one individual who later became president of the institution. Data collection combined three sources: (1) in-depth semi-structured individual interviews, recorded and transcribed, averaging 1.5 hours each; (2) document analysis of emails, meeting minutes, and curriculum design materials produced during the reform; (3) reflective records in the form of personal self-reflection journals kept by the principal researcher. Analysis occurred simultaneously with data collection and coding, using the constant comparative method with theoretical sampling, structured into three coding stages: open coding (identifying, labeling, and categorizing initial concepts directly from emails and transcripts), axial coding (connecting categories and subcategories, analyzing their dimensions and properties, generating visual representations of themes), and selective coding (integrating categories around core concepts, narrowing the theory to five major variables/assertions). An explicit bias-control procedure is recorded: because one of the authors served as facilitator and chair of the reform, he recorded his own responses before the interviews and waited six months after the curriculum reform's conclusion before beginning interviews, to establish emotional distance.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Five conceptual anchors: Postmodern Curriculum characteristics (Doll, 1993; Slattery, 2006), understanding curriculum development as a non-linear, creative, fluid process centered on human relationships among participants ("curriculum-in-action"), grounded in communal cooperation, holistic vision, and interdisciplinarity; Complexity and Curriculum Reform (Burgess, 2004), distinguishing minor, isolated disciplinary changes from comprehensive curriculum reform, which requires understanding how parts interconnect and tolerating ambiguity and the emergence of self-organized, unplanned solutions; Reculturing vs. Restructuring (Fullan, 2001), positing that genuine educational reform requires changing the culture of how people act and think in the organization (reculturing), rather than merely altering course grids or syllabi (restructuring); Shared Governance (Mortimer & Sathre, 2007), positing curriculum as a corporate responsibility of the institution as a whole, requiring flexibility and active partnership among faculty, administrators, and boards; Communities of Practice (Bauman, 2005) and Team Learning Approach (Bensimon & Neumann, 1993), conceptualizing the review group as a community prioritizing collaborative learning and thinking-together over a purely task-technical focus.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — The ten-person mixed faculty-administrator review team and its four-year collaborative process at a single institution (single case). The unit is the team's lived experience and interpretive account of the process, not curriculum content or student outcomes.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — A four-year comprehensive curriculum review at ATS, studied via Grounded Theory as it unfolded, with data collection and coding occurring simultaneously with the process itself (interviews conducted after a six-month post-completion wait). The process is not described in terms of formal sequential steps or phases; rather, it is characterized through five consolidated thematic findings (see field 16) describing how the team experienced and enacted the reform: constructing a shared philosophical vision before technical syllabus work; distributing responsibility across faculty/administrative boundaries; engaging in intense inter/intradepartmental collaboration; confronting cultural/territorial resistance; and sustaining engagement through a strong sense of community.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — The 10-member curriculum review team (5 faculty, 5 administrators), one of whom later became institutional president; the principal researcher, who also served as the reform's facilitator/chair (academic vice-president); academic department heads, described as acting as both facilitators and, at times, obstacles to change.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — A collectively constructed shared philosophical vision centered on desired student learning outcomes, treated as an indispensable foundation preceding any practical discussion of course syllabi; shared/corporate responsibility for curriculum as distinct from faculty-exclusive ownership; degree of inter- and intradepartmental transparency about what each instructor actually taught.

**11. Contextual conditions** — Single graduate theological seminary (largest in its state) operating as a division of a larger university; traditional higher-education culture characterized by faculty individualism, extreme autonomy, and disciplinary fragmentation, described as colliding with the demands of radical, time-intensive, broadly participatory reform.

**12. Barriers** — Faculty struggling to relinquish strict autonomy and disciplinary "territory wars"; questions/contestation about the reform's leadership being held by an administrator; initial role/functional clarity lacking for half the team; absence of financial resources or teaching-load reduction at the project's outset; the collision between the reform's collaborative demands and the traditional culture of faculty individualism and disciplinary fragmentation.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — Collective construction of a shared vision preceding technical work; the perception of curriculum as common corporate duty, which dissolved traditional faculty/administrative boundaries and secured administrative financial support for implementation; intense inter- and intradepartmental collaboration forcing transparent dialogue about actual teaching content; a strong sense of community and personal loyalty among team members, described as the principal motivational fuel sustaining engagement through periods of high emotional tension and conflict; a "community of practice" / "mutual learning" posture rather than a purely task-oriented bureaucratic approach.

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — NOT REPORTED in procedural/sequential detail — the note documents the collaborative and relational dynamics of the process (vision-building, shared responsibility, cross-departmental dialogue) as reconstructed through interviews, documents, and journals, rather than a stepwise implementation protocol for the curriculum changes themselves; no description is given of how finalized curricular changes were formally adopted, approved, or rolled out into course delivery.

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — NOT REPORTED as measured outcome data. The note records only the authors' suggested future-research directions relevant to sustainability: investigating the relationship between organizational learning and organizational change, particularly the role of belonging and community in committee productivity, and isolated evaluation of department heads' role and influence in reform processes. **16. Principal findings** —
  - Shared Vision: collective construction of a guiding philosophical vision (centered on desired student learning outcomes) served as an indispensable foundation preceding any practical discussion of course syllabi.
  - Shared Responsibility: the perception that curriculum is a common corporate duty helped dissolve traditional boundaries between "faculty" and "administrative" domains, improving organizational empathy and securing administrative financial support for implementation.
  - Collaboration: the reform promoted intense inter- and intradepartmental exchange; some sectors integrated deeply because the process forced transparent dialogue about what each instructor actually taught.
  - Cultural Challenges: paradoxically, the same human dynamics that favored change also created impasses — faculty struggled to relinquish strict autonomy and disciplinary "territory wars"; there were questions about leadership of the process being held by an administrator; initial role clarity was lacking for half the team; and there was an absence of financial resources or teaching-load reduction at the project's outset.
  - Sense of Community and Connection: a strong sense of community and personal loyalty built among team members acted as the principal motivational fuel sustaining engagement through periods of high emotional tension and conflict.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - Structured collaboration between different institutional groups enables organizational learning and makes deep change possible.
  - The team operated under a "community of practice" logic, adopting a posture of "mutual learning" rather than a purely task-oriented bureaucratic approach, with participants' motivation stemming from a desire to learn together and improve the seminary.
  - Changing curricula is not an act of technical or structural engineering but an effort of reculturing.
  - Academic leaders must anticipate structural, communicative, and resource-related barriers in order to plan processes that respect the time needed for relationships to mature.

**18. Contribution** — Theoretical: an expansion of the concept of shared governance applied to comprehensive curricular change, demonstrating that reform need not be an exclusively faculty-driven process but can be a joint, co-managed responsibility with administration. Methodological: a detailed application of qualitative triangulation (interviews, document analysis, reflective journals) combined with rigorous Grounded Theory coding stages, plus a practical model for reflexive bias control for administrators researching their own workplace. Practical: a structured list of tailored action recommendations for presidents/administrators, participating faculty, committee coordinators, and technical team members intending to conduct similar reforms.

**19. Limitations** — Three author-acknowledged limitations: field-research bias — the principal researcher was himself the academic vice-president and chair of the reform committee, which could have distorted perceptions of the reform's success and of collaboration, mitigated (per the note) by emotional-distancing safeguards and the practice of recording responses in advance; resource indefinition — the lack of planning for adequate institutional compensation (teaching-load reduction or direct financial compensation) overburdened faculty and made the effort dependent on their intrinsic motivation; role uncertainty — half of the interviewees reported facing initial lack of clarity about their exact functions on the team and their representational role. [DERIVED] The dual insider role of the principal researcher (chair of the reform and lead investigator) constitutes a structural risk of interpretive bias that the described mitigation steps (six-month wait, pre-recorded responses) may not fully eliminate, since no independent/external verification mechanism is reported. [DERIVED] The balance of predominantly positive findings (four of five: Shared Vision, Shared Responsibility, Collaboration, Community) relative to the single explicitly challenge-centered finding (Cultural Challenges) may partly reflect the insider-researcher's vantage point rather than solely the empirical balance of participants' experience — this connects the explicit finding structure with the explicit insider-bias limitation but is not stated as such in the note.

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — Faculty–administrator collaboration in institutional change; shared/corporate governance of curriculum; reculturing vs. restructuring; comprehensive vs. incremental curricular reform; community of practice/mutual learning; role ambiguity in change-management teams; resource/compensation gaps in voluntary institutional reform efforts; grounded theory as a method for studying organizational change; insider-researcher positionality and bias mitigation; sense of belonging/community as motivational sustenance in long-duration change processes; disciplinary territoriality and faculty autonomy as resistance forces; the role of a pre-articulated shared vision as a precondition for detailed technical redesign work.

**21. Tensions or assumptions** — Single-case generalizability: the study is based on a single institution and a single reform process with 10 participants; the theoretical claims advanced (e.g., that curricular reform is inherently a matter of "reculturing" requiring shared governance) are stated in fairly general terms, though the evidentiary base supports only an in-depth account of this one case. Insider-researcher role: the principal researcher was simultaneously the chair/facilitator of the reform being studied and an interviewer of the very team he led; although mitigation steps are described (six-month wait, pre-recorded responses), this raises a structural tension between generating "grounded" theory from participants' voices and the researcher's own stake in narrating the reform as a success — no independent verification (e.g., external auditor, member-checking beyond what is described) is reported. Success-oriented framing: the five findings emphasize predominantly positive outcomes, with "Cultural Challenges" the sole finding centered on difficulty; given the insider-researcher issue, it is worth questioning whether this balance reflects empirical reality or partly the vantage point of a researcher invested in the reform's legitimacy — the note does not adjudicate this. Assumption that shared governance necessarily "dissolves" faculty-administrative boundaries: reported as an authors' finding/interpretation, but the note does not describe what would count as evidence of durable boundary dissolution versus temporary situational cooperation limited to the reform period. Resource/compensation gap treated as a limitation rather than a potential confound: the note frames the absence of financial resources or workload reduction as a limitation, yet the reform is still reported as succeeding via "intrinsic motivation" and communal bonding — an unexamined tension between celebrating community-driven success and flagging that the process was not institutionally resourced, which could be read as a sustainability risk rather than solely a footnote limitation.
