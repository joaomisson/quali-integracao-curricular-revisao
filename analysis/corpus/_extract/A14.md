## A14 — Action research on redesigning a sustainability elective program via a scientific "support team" model at the University of Basel

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study is centered on the social and scientific process of redesigning a single elective higher-education curriculum focused on sustainability competencies — the Transfaculty Cross-Section Program on Sustainable Development (TQNE) at the University of Basel, Switzerland. The stated problem is that, although numerous case studies describe Higher Education for Sustainable Development (HESD) programs and their outcomes, there is a "blind spot" in the literature regarding the process itself — the social, didactic, and learning process that leads to curricular revision and improvement. Current HESD discourse lacks practical procedures for handling challenges specific to elective programs, such as extreme interdisciplinary heterogeneity among students and the limited contact time instructors have with them. The stated relevance of the problem is that universities are continuously challenged to implement curricular quality improvements that translate complex theory into practice under real institutional constraints of time and personnel.

**2. Object of investigation** — The process of curricular redesign itself — not the curriculum's content or outcomes as static objects, but the social, didactic, and decision-making process by which a specific HESD elective program is diagnosed, redesigned, and piloted, together with the role and instruments of a scientific support team within that process. This is drawn directly from the study's stated theme, described in the source as the "processo social e científico de redesenho curricular" (social and scientific process of curricular redesign).

**3. Educational / institutional context** — A single elective (non-mandatory) transfaculty program on sustainable development at the University of Basel, funded by the "Sustainable Development at Universities" program (2013–2016). The governance structure separated a redesign team (local decision-makers) from a scientific support team composed of two HESD specialists external to the program. The study also references telephone interviews with directors of 8 similar sustainability elective programs in Switzerland and Germany, used as external benchmarking data during the diagnostic phase. [DERIVED] This suggests the study's primary empirical basis remains a single institutional case, with the multi-program interviews functioning as supplementary reference material rather than a comparative case-study design in their own right — based on the source's framing of the external interviews as one of several diagnostic data streams feeding into a single redesign process, rather than as an independent multi-site outcome comparison.

**4. Study or article type** — Action research (single-case, empirical article).

**5. Methodological approach** — Action research ("pesquisa-ação") conducted through the actual redesign of the TQNE program. Data collection in four components: (1) retrospective analysis of 8 years of accumulated structured student evaluation questionnaires (5–7 datasets per course, ranging from 6–90 responses, average 34, with open and closed questions synthesized to categorize practical problems); (2) semi-structured interviews (~30 minutes) with 9 local instructors; (3) 1-hour telephone interviews with directors of 8 comparable external programs plus document analysis, processed through computer-assisted Qualitative Content Analysis (MAXQDA) using a two-dimensional matrix crossing practical challenges against curricular design elements; and (4) a formative pilot evaluation of the redesigned curriculum via non-participant observation (with an observation protocol focused on theme and active learning), semi-structured interviews with the 4 main instructors, student questionnaires (72–100% response rate), and a focus group with 7 students who completed the full program within one year. The methodology combines diagnostic qualitative content analysis with an intervention/action phase and a subsequent formative evaluation, consistent with an action-research design alternating academic inquiry and applied curricular decision-making. Inter-rater reliability procedures, sampling rationale for the 9 interviewed instructors relative to the total teaching staff, and statistical treatment of the questionnaire data beyond descriptive synthesis are NOT REPORTED.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — The HESD literature on sustainability competencies and pedagogical approaches (citing de Haan, Wiek, Rieckmann, Lozano, Sipos). Curriculum as a "contextualized social process," attributed to Cornbleth (1988), defining program-level curriculum as an ongoing negotiation and decision-making process among actors under concrete institutional conditions. A process-oriented program development approach emphasizing participation, structured planning, and mutual acceptance of the curriculum as the primary guide for local actors' practice. Action research as the methodological alignment guiding systematic alternation between academic research and practical program development. Transdisciplinarity in curricular change, involving direct collaboration between certified HESD specialists and non-certified local instructors with practical course experience. The "teaching choreographies" theory of Oser and Baeriswyl (2001), used to rethink selection of teaching-learning methods ("micromethods") linked to students' mental processes. [DERIVED] The theoretical apparatus is explicitly pluralistic — the study does not appear to rely on a single dominant theory but assembles curriculum-as-social-process, action research, transdisciplinarity, and teaching-choreography theory into a composite framework; this is inferred from the source listing six distinct conceptual sources without indicating a hierarchical or single primary theory.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — A single elective transfaculty program (TQNE) at one university, examined at the level of its redesign process, involving the redesign team, the two-person scientific support team, 9 diagnostic-phase instructors, 4 pilot-phase instructors, and program students (questionnaire respondents and a 7-student focus group).

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — Diagnostic findings: students and instructors identified a lack of a unifying framework for topic selection and unclear links between individual courses and global sustainability; instructors were unaware of what colleagues taught in their own classrooms; classroom heterogeneity created difficulties in calibrating the depth of presentations. The scientific support team created two structuring devices: a meso-level "Briefing Note" documenting which macro-level decisions needed resolution, alternatives adopted by partner universities, and impacts of each decision against HESD criteria; and a micro-level "Pedagogical Workshop" aimed at raising individual instructors' awareness and capability in applying active/reflective learning "micromethods" adapted to their disciplinary content. Reported redesign outcomes: centralizing learning objectives on analytical skills and critical reflection on sustainability topics (prioritizing critical understanding over practical facilitation of complex processes); creation of a unified, cross-cutting annual focus theme (initiated with "Food and Sustainability"); implementation of a joint kick-off event using team teaching to present the interconnected relevance of the theme; and structuring an integrative block course centered on active group dynamics. Pilot evaluation results: the kick-off event successfully unified focus and provided transparency to students, and the annual theme enabled instructors to make cross-references and update scientific content more effectively; however, the final integrated block course partially failed its consolidation goal because of difficulty finding an instructor prepared for interdisciplinary teaching who had not been an active part of the social curricular design process.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — A redesign team of local decision-makers; a two-person scientific support team of HESD specialists external to the program (functioning in a "coach" role); 9 local instructors interviewed in the diagnostic phase; 4 main instructors interviewed and observed in the pilot phase (including one non-participating substitute instructor assigned to the integrative block course); program students (questionnaire respondents at 72–100% response rate, and a 7-student focus group of those who completed the full program within one year); directors of 8 comparable external sustainability elective programs in Switzerland and Germany, consulted as benchmarking informants.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The two-dimensional MAXQDA analysis matrix crossing "practical challenges" against "curricular design elements." HESD criteria used to assess the impacts of macro-level decisions and partner-university alternatives, as documented in the Briefing Note. Active/reflective learning "micromethods" linked to students' mental processes (teaching choreographies theory). The "area of tension" between the theoretically/empirically "optimal" curriculum and the institutionally constrained "existing" curriculum, functioning as an implicit decision framework balancing ambition against feasibility.

**11. Contextual conditions** — Elective (non-mandatory) program status, entailing extreme interdisciplinary heterogeneity among students and limited instructor-student contact time. Institutional structural barriers: instructor time resources, historical path dependencies, and financial limits. External funding through the "Sustainable Development at Universities" program (2013–2016). Comparative external benchmarking data from 8 similar programs in Switzerland and Germany.

**12. Barriers** — Lack of a unifying framework for topic selection and unclear links between individual courses and global sustainability; instructors unaware of colleagues' course content; classroom heterogeneity complicating calibration of presentation depth; lack of time and human resources cited by local instructors as a limitation on deeper implementation of active methods within individual courses; difficulty finding an instructor prepared for interdisciplinary teaching who had also been part of the social curricular design process — delegating the integrative block course to an instructor who had not participated in the initial workshops proved to be a critical and vulnerable point of execution, and the block course partially failed its consolidation goal as a result.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — The scientific support team functioning as a "coach" rather than a technocratic authority: its primary function is to make the area of tension between optimal and existing curricula explicit through neutral data so that local actors can decide participatorily and consciously, achieving a realistic, broadly supported, and practically executable compromise. Three pillars proposed as underlying successful HESD curricular transformation: (1) rigorous connection to instructors' prior local practices and experiences; (2) preservation of multiple didactic frameworks, avoiding dogmatic imposition of a single pedagogical line; and (3) constant opening of environments conducive to dialogue and interdisciplinary consensus-building. The unified annual focus theme and joint kick-off event (team teaching) served as integration devices that provided transparency to students and enabled instructors to cross-reference content.

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — The meso-level "Briefing Note" (documenting macro-level decisions, partner-university alternatives, and HESD-criteria impacts) and the micro-level "Pedagogical Workshop" (building individual instructor capability in active/reflective learning micromethods) as the two core knowledge-transposition instruments. The unified annual cross-cutting theme (e.g., "Food and Sustainability"), the joint kick-off event with team teaching, and the integrative block course centered on active group dynamics as the concrete redesigned curricular structures. The formative pilot evaluation combined non-participant observation (protocol focused on theme and active learning), semi-structured instructor interviews, student questionnaires, and a student focus group.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - Curricular redesign occurs permanently in an "area of tension" between the empirically and theoretically desirable ("optimal") curriculum and the rigid reality bounded by institutional structural barriers (instructor time resources, historical path dependencies, financial limits).
  - Scientific support functions not as a technocratic imposition of inaccessible theoretical models but similarly to a "coach": its primary function is to make this area of conflict explicit through neutral data so that local actors can decide participatorily and consciously, achieving a realistic, broadly supported, and practically executable compromise.
  - Three pillars underlie successful HESD curricular transformation: (1) rigorous connection to instructors' prior local practices and experiences; (2) preservation of multiple didactic frameworks, avoiding dogmatic imposition of a single pedagogical line; and (3) constant opening of environments conducive to dialogue and interdisciplinary consensus-building.
  - [DERIVED] These arguments collectively position curricular reform less as an application of an external "correct" model and more as a negotiated, facilitated social process bounded by institutional realism — a normative-methodological stance rather than a purely empirical finding, since the "coach" framing and the three pillars appear to be interpretive conclusions the authors draw from their single-case action-research intervention rather than independently validated causal claims, based on the fact that these are presented as "Discussão e Conclusões" drawn from one case's diagnostic and pilot data, without described comparative or counterfactual testing.

**18. Contribution** — Theoretical: reducing the literature gap by scientifically detailing the dynamic process of didactic restructuring itself, moving from purely theoretical competency debates to theorizing real organizational and pedagogical change dynamics. Methodological: designing and demonstrating operational validity of practical knowledge-transposition instruments — the meso-level briefing note and micro-level teaching-learning workshops — within university action research. Practical: providing a structured, feasible, and replicable redesign model respecting resource scarcity of small university programs through "outsourcing" of educational market-research-type tasks.
