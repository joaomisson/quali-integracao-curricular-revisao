## A03 — Multi-criteria decision-making model for sustainability-oriented accounting curriculum planning

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study addresses the Program Course Planning Problem (PCPP) at the operational level, integrating Education for Sustainable Development (ESD) principles into accounting education. The motivating problem is a scarcity of operational studies and quantitative methodologies for structuring curricula; course planning is traditionally conducted manually and intuitively, without standardized processes or explicit consideration of the sustainability competencies demanded by the labor market. A criticism attributed to the field: accounting programs have neglected soft skills and topics such as corporate responsibility, social justice, and environmental protection. The topic's relevance is framed around the claim that course planning determines the quality of academic programs, institutional reputation, and the efficiency of limited educational resource use, while preparing future decision-makers to address complex global challenges — presented as justification for treating curriculum planning as a decision problem worth formal modeling rather than an administrative afterthought.

**2. Object of investigation** — The curriculum-planning process itself — specifically, the joint problem of (a) selecting which courses (mandatory and elective) should compose an accounting program based on their contribution to sustainability competencies, and (b) scheduling those selected courses across semesters in a balanced way. The unit of analysis is a single institutional curriculum (one accounting program at one Chinese university), evaluated through the judgments of a small expert panel rather than through student learning outcomes or longitudinal program performance.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — Undergraduate accounting program at an unnamed Chinese university. The applied case involved a panel of five experts: one accounting department head, three accounting professors, and one professional from a local company, all reported to have more than ten years of experience. The scheduling stage concerned the last two years (four semesters) of the program's curriculum.

**4. Study or article type** — Quantitative empirical case study proposing and applying a multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) support model; no formal statistical hypotheses were formulated, only practical research questions: (1) how to quantify the relevance of different academic courses for building specific sustainability competencies; (2) how to balance credit loads and difficulty levels of selected courses across a semester curriculum.

**5. Methodological approach** — Structured in three sequential phases. Phase I (Competency Prioritization via BWM): the five-expert panel performed pairwise comparisons to determine the relative importance of the eight UNESCO sustainability competencies within accounting education. Phase II (Course Selection and Ranking via FFR): experts rated, on a 1–10 scale, the degree of relation between 53 candidate courses (compiled from the existing curriculum and from other leading universities' programs) and the eight competencies; the Fuzzy Filter Ranking (FFR) algorithm produced a "sustainability mapping index" per course, and top-ranked courses were selected until reaching the credit threshold per module — 19 of 27 candidate mandatory courses and 16 of 26 candidate elective courses. Phase III (Course Scheduling via MCGP): the 35 selected courses were entered into a Multi-Choice Goal Programming (MCGP) mathematical model, executed in LINGO 18.0, to distribute courses across four semesters while respecting prerequisites, credit limits, and elective constraints. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted, testing ten different competency-weight scenarios.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Three conceptual pillars combining strategic management, sustainable education, and operations research: He-Xie Management Theory (HXMT), attributed to Youmin Xi (1989), drawing on Eastern and Western philosophical concepts to address complex organizational problems under uncertainty, divided into the He Principle (an evolutionary mechanism of human coordination and stakeholder engagement) and the Xie Principle (a control mechanism focused on rational process design and mathematical optimization); the UNESCO Sustainability Competences Framework, comprising eight domains — Systems thinking (C1), Anticipatory (C2), Normative (C3), Strategic (C4), Collaboration (C5), Critical thinking (C6), Self-awareness (C7), Integrated problem-solving (C8); and three MCDM methods — Best Worst Method (BWM, Rezaei 2015), described as requiring fewer pairwise comparisons and offering greater consistency than AHP; Fuzzy Filter Ranking (FFR, Chang and Ku 2021), applied to handle the imprecise nature of human judgment in ranking and selecting courses; and Multi-Choice Goal Programming (MCGP, Chang 2008), used for scheduling to allow multiple aspiration levels and avoid underestimating complex decisions.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — A single institutional curriculum: one accounting program at one Chinese university, with course selection and scheduling decisions evaluated through the judgments of a five-member expert panel. NOT APPLICABLE: individual-learner-level or multi-institution comparative analysis — neither is part of the design.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — A three-phase quantitative planning process (not a review of an existing implemented curriculum but a prospective design/selection process): competency weighting (BWM) → course-to-competency mapping and ranking (FFR), selecting 35 of 53 candidate courses → semester scheduling of the selected courses (MCGP) subject to prerequisite, credit, and elective constraints, followed by a ten-scenario sensitivity analysis on competency weights to test selection stability.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — The five-member expert panel (one accounting department head, three accounting professors, one industry professional with a local company), described in the note as the operational instantiation of the He Principle's "stakeholder engagement." NOT REPORTED: student, alumni, or broader employer involvement beyond the single industry professional on the panel.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The eight UNESCO sustainability competency domains (C1–C8) as weighted criteria for course relevance; the resulting "sustainability mapping index" per course; credit-load and difficulty-level balance targets per semester (goal-programming targets G1–G4); prerequisite structure and elective-vs-mandatory credit constraints.

**11. Contextual conditions** — Single, unnamed Chinese university's undergraduate accounting program; scheduling scope restricted to the last two years (four semesters) of the curriculum; a pre-existing pool of 53 candidate courses drawn from the current curriculum and from programs at other "leading" universities.

**12. Barriers** — [DERIVED] The note does not report barriers in the organizational-resistance sense; the closest analogues are methodological constraints treated by the authors as limitations rather than barriers encountered during the process: group aggregation bias in expert-judgment elicitation, a single-level (non-branched) competency criteria structure, and restriction of the scheduling model to semester-level (not daily/room-level) allocation.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — The BWM method's claimed efficiency (fewer pairwise comparisons, greater consistency than AHP); the FFR algorithm's capacity to handle imprecise human judgment; the He-Xie Theory's framing of harmonizing human coordination (He) with mathematical optimization (Xie) as enabling coordinated workflow among academic leadership, professors, and external professionals; sensitivity analysis used by the authors to support a claim of model stability/robustness.

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — LINGO 18.0 software execution of the MCGP scheduling model; pairwise comparison instruments for BWM competency weighting; 1–10 rating scales for FFR course-competency relevance judgments; goal-programming deviation minimization (G1–G4) to achieve credit/difficulty balance across four semesters. NOT REPORTED: any mechanism for translating the resulting model output into actual institutional adoption, faculty buy-in, or governance approval — the note describes only the modeling process, not an implementation/rollout process at the institution.

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — NOT REPORTED. The note describes a proposed and computationally validated planning model, not its adoption, rollout, or persistence within the institution; no information is given on whether the resulting course selection/schedule was actually implemented, approved by governance bodies, or sustained over time.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - Competency weights (BWM): Integrated problem-solving (C8) received the highest average weight (0.2423), followed by Critical thinking (C6, weight 0.1877) and Collaboration (C5, weight 0.1679); Anticipatory (C2, weight 0.0557) received the lowest weight.
  - Sustainability-based course selection (FFR): the method produced module-specific course rankings. Example: in the preliminary mandatory module, "Corporate Social Responsibility" ranked first with a mapping index of 0.2279, while "English for Accounting" ranked last (7th) with an index of 0.0224.
  - Balanced semester schedule (MCGP): the 35 selected courses (90 total credits) were distributed with reported high semester balance — mandatory-course credits varying smoothly between 12 and 14 per semester, elective credits between 9 and 10 per semester, average difficulty levels ranging from 2.71 to 4.00 for mandatory courses and 3.00 to 3.25 for electives, and minimal global deviations from ideal credit/difficulty targets (G1 = 2; G2 = 1.2857; G3 = 2; G4 = 0.375).
  - Sensitivity analysis: across ten tested competency-weight scenarios, course selection remained practically identical in most simulated scenarios, characterized in the note as demonstrating model stability.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - The proposed model is a viable, reliable, and flexible tool for academic planning, capable of precisely modeling subjective human preferences without the kind of simplification that would lead to underestimated decisions.
  - The findings show it is possible to migrate accounting education from a purely technicist and manual teaching logic toward a structured approach that addresses contemporary social demands through green competencies and soft skills.
  - Theoretical harmonization through He-Xie Theory allowed a workflow in which coordination among academic leadership, professors, and external professionals (the He Principle) operated in full alignment with mathematical optimization models (the Xie Principle).

**18. Contribution** — Theoretical: an unprecedented bridge between operations research and He-Xie Management Theory within educational management and curriculum development. Methodological: an integrated BWM-FFR-MCGP framework and the introduction of the "sustainability mapping index" as a quantitative concept for empirically measuring the correlation between university courses and UNESCO's global guidelines. Practical: an operational roadmap for academic administrators to formulate balanced, scientifically justified curricula, plus a reliable tool for academic advisors to help students plan individual study paths.

**19. Limitations** — Author-stated: group aggregation bias — BWM used a simple arithmetic mean to aggregate expert judgments, which can be affected by extreme values; the authors suggest testing Bayesian models or Delphi-based approaches. Single-level criteria structure — course evaluations relied on simple competency-level criteria; the authors recommend branching the main criteria into detailed subcriteria. Restricted scheduling scope — the scheduling model was limited to semester-level distribution; the authors encourage extending the approach to daily physical-allocation problems (teachers to classes, classes to rooms, daily timetables). Lack of methodological comparison — the authors suggest applying other popular MCDM methods (ANP, FCE, ELECTRE, TOPSIS, VIKOR) to the same data to compare performance and rankings. [DERIVED] Additional constraints derivable from the design as described: a single case study of one program at one unnamed Chinese university, evaluated by only five experts, with no indication of statistical power, external validation, or triangulation with other stakeholder groups (students, employers beyond the one panelist, or external curriculum bodies); the "sustainability mapping index" and competency weights rest entirely on subjective expert ratings treated as ground truth, without an independent validity criterion; the sensitivity analysis addresses only variation in competency weights, not variation in the course pool, rating scale, or panel size/composition.

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — MCDM-based curriculum design; sustainability competency quantification; expert-judgment weighting (BWM); course-to-competency mapping index (FFR); balanced semester scheduling (MCGP/goal programming); He-Xie duality (human coordination vs. mathematical optimization); soft skills/socioemotional competencies in accounting education; shift from technicist to sustainability-structured pedagogy; model stability via sensitivity analysis; single-case empirical validation of a planning framework; curriculum/course planning reframed as a formal MCDM problem rather than an intuitive administrative task; expert-panel-based weighting as an alternative to broader-consultation approaches.

**21. Tensions or assumptions** — The central design assumption is that the relevance of a course to a sustainability competency can be validly captured through expert ratings on a 1–10 scale and translated into a single "sustainability mapping index"; the note gives no indication of how this instrument's validity or reliability was assessed beyond the model's internal sensitivity analysis, so the mapping index functions as a modeling convenience whose correspondence to actual pedagogical content or learning outcomes is not evidenced. [DERIVED] The claim that the He Principle (human coordination and stakeholder engagement) is genuinely instantiated is worth scrutinizing: "stakeholder engagement" reduces operationally to a five-expert panel providing pairwise comparisons and 1–10 ratings — a comparatively narrow empirical realization of a philosophical theory framed as addressing "complex organizational problems under uncertainty," with no broader deliberation, negotiation, or conflict-resolution process described. [DERIVED] The note's characterization of "balance" (credits and difficulty levels distributed smoothly across semesters, minimal deviation from goal targets) is a mathematical-optimization criterion, not necessarily a pedagogical one; no consideration of pedagogical sequencing, prerequisite learning progression beyond formal constraints, or cognitive-load considerations beyond the numeric "difficulty level" score is reported. The "model stability" claim rests on a sensitivity analysis limited to variation in competency weights across ten scenarios; the course pool, expert panel, and rating scale itself were not varied, so the robustness claim is narrower than the general framing might suggest. The compilation of the 53 candidate courses is described only as drawn "from the current curriculum and from programs of other top universities," without further selection-criteria detail, raising an unaddressed question about selection bias favoring courses already resembling those at "leading" institutions. [DERIVED] The study's validation strategy is internal (mathematical consistency, sensitivity stability) rather than external (comparison with actual student competency outcomes, employer satisfaction, or accreditation review); the authors' concluding claims of viability, reliability, and flexibility should be read as claims about model behavior under tested conditions, not as demonstrated educational effectiveness. [DERIVED] The practical-contribution framing (an operational roadmap for "academic administrators" and a tool for "academic advisors," phrased generically rather than restricted to the studied institution) implies the authors intend the model to be usable beyond this single case, but no evidence bearing on transferability to other institutions, disciplines, or national/regulatory contexts is reported, so this broader applicability remains an inference from the contribution framing rather than an evidenced claim.

**22. Note-layer caveats specific to this study** — The note does not provide: the specific name or further institutional detail of the Chinese university where the case study was conducted; the precise criteria or process used to compile the pool of 53 candidate courses; the rationale for selecting a five-member expert panel (size, recruitment, compensation); any assessment of inter-rater reliability or agreement among the five experts beyond the arithmetic-mean aggregation noted as a limitation; whether students, alumni, or employers beyond the one panelist were consulted at any stage; any data on actual student outcomes, competency development, or employment results following (or independent of) the proposed curriculum; details of peer review, funding, or ethical approval processes; the full mathematical formulation of the "sustainability mapping index" or the MCGP model beyond the reported summary statistics; and any comparison of the BWM-FFR-MCGP framework's rankings against the alternative MCDM methods (ANP, FCE, ELECTRE, TOPSIS, VIKOR) the authors themselves flag as absent and worth pursuing.
