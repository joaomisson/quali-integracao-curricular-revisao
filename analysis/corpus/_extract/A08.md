## A08 — Barriers to practical implementation of a renewed Bachelor of Youth Work curriculum via Action Learning

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study's theme is curriculum renewal in higher education, with a specific analytical focus on the practical implementation process of new teaching proposals, rather than on curriculum design itself. The research problem is framed as an imbalance in the existing literature: abundant scholarship addresses curriculum design and theoretical coherence, but there is reportedly little investigation into the difficulties of implementing curricula faithfully within real university contexts, and into how practical constraints end up distorting or altering the original design. The study is motivated by the observation that designing a new curriculum is a relatively straightforward process, whereas its effective practical implementation is much more challenging. Stated relevance rationale: without understanding the practical barriers affecting curriculum execution, reforms risk remaining "on paper" only, and understanding these obstacles is presented as helping to avoid a widening gap between courses and advances in science, educational theory, and societal/labor-market demands.

**2. Object of investigation** — The process and practical difficulties of implementing a curriculum reform — specifically the gap between "curriculum-as-designed" and "curriculum-as-enacted" (a distinction attributed to Barnett and Coate, quoted directly in the source) — within a single course over a defined multi-year period. [DERIVED] This positions the object of study not as curriculum content or design quality per se, but as an institutional and organizational process: how time allocation, staffing structures, documentation systems, and quality-assurance regimes interact with (and reportedly obstruct) pedagogical intentions — an inference following directly from the note's repeated framing of "barriers" as arising from workload models, staffing precarity, and documentation templates rather than from flaws in the curricular design itself.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — The Bachelor of Youth Work course at a large, young Australian public university (founded 1991), located in a state capital. The study spans a two-year period (2013–2015), covering two complete Action Learning cycles. The teaching team is lean: one full-time permanent academic (project leader), one part-time contracted instructor, four casual/sessional teachers who worked primarily as community field practitioners, and partial involvement from a university instructional designer.

**4. Study or article type** — Qualitative exploratory study based on the Action Learning method, operating in cycles composed of dialogic questioning, planning, action, data observation, reflection, and replanning. Stated objective: to investigate the difficulties and barriers involved in the practical implementation of a renewed higher-education curriculum at one university, and to use the results to propose how institutions could better support such processes. Two explicit research questions: (1) what are the barriers to curricular change; (2) how could university processes and practices be improved to better support curricular change.

**5. Methodological approach** — Data collection combined institutional data (retention, grades, student feedback drawn from university systems) with data collected directly by the team: annual evaluation meetings with the full teaching staff (with casual staff paid to participate while project funding was available), qualitative student feedback focused on what students actually learned, detailed analysis of samples of student work and assessments, and a systematic internal review of units and materials. [DERIVED] The combination of Action Learning as both the investigative method and, apparently, part of the pedagogical intervention itself (used "as practical and methodological support for continuous formative evaluation and collective teacher learning") suggests the study may not cleanly separate the role of researcher from the role of practitioner-reformer — a reasonable inference from the description of the author/project leader as simultaneously the course's permanent academic leading the reform, though the note does not explicitly discuss researcher-practitioner positionality as a methodological issue.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Situated within critical curriculum literature, analyzing the relationship between knowledge, education, and power relations within the university. A multi-layered conceptual approach combining five distinct frameworks: Barnett and Coate's (2005) framework, defining curriculum orientation across three learning domains — "knowing" (knowledge of the field), "acting" (competencies and practices), and "becoming/being" (how learning integrates with the self, particularly in professional ethical terms); Threshold Concepts (Meyer & Land, 2012), used at the discipline/unit level to identify which disciplinary concepts are crucial, harder to master, and structurally important, enabling content to be "pruned" and depth of theoretical focus prioritized; the Community of Inquiry (COI) framework (Garrison & Arbaugh, 2007), adopted specifically to organize pedagogical interactions and learning in the digital education environment; the SOLO taxonomy (Biggs & Tang, 2007), used for constructive alignment between assessment tasks and expected conceptual learning; Action Learning (Boshyk & Dilworth, 2010; Weinstein, 1998), used as practical/methodological support for continuous formative evaluation and collective teacher learning.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — A single course (Bachelor of Youth Work) at a single institution, tracked across two complete Action Learning cycles over a two-year period (2013–2015); the lean teaching team (project leader, one part-time instructor, four casual/sessional teachers, partial instructional-designer involvement) as the group enacting and reflecting on the reform.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — Iterative Action Learning cycles (dialogic questioning, planning, action, data observation, reflection, replanning) applied to unit redevelopment across two years. Of 11 required units in the course, the team directly managed only 7; the remaining 4 were shared with other courses and taught by faculty outside the project, and attempts to modify them failed or met resistance. Institutional and team-collected data (retention, grades, student feedback, staff evaluation meetings, student-work samples, systematic unit review) fed back into replanning within each cycle.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — One full-time permanent academic (project leader); one part-time contracted instructor; four casual/sessional teachers working primarily as community field practitioners; a university instructional designer (partial involvement); faculty outside the project teaching the 4 shared units, who resisted modification attempts; university management, referenced in connection with quality-metric requirements and performance expectations; the government regulatory agency TEQSA, shaping internal documentation/quality systems.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The three-domain curriculum model (knowing/acting/becoming-being) as a basis for defining learning outcomes; Threshold Concepts as a criterion for pruning content and prioritizing disciplinary depth; SOLO-based constructive alignment between assessment tasks and expected conceptual learning; institutional quality metrics (retention, satisfaction) under a "student-as-client" logic, used by management to evaluate instructors.

**11. Contextual conditions** — A large, young (founded 1991) Australian public university in a state capital; a discipline (Youth Work) staffed substantially by field practitioners without formal pedagogical training; a university workload model allocating fixed hours for unit redevelopment; internal quality/record-keeping systems structured in response to requirements from the government regulatory agency TEQSA; project funding available only during the pilot period, after which paid participation in evaluation meetings became unfeasible.

**12. Barriers** — Lack of time, team stability, and curricular control: the university's workload model allocated 39 hours for major unit redevelopment, but the team found actual time required was 200–250 hours per unit, not including subsequent adjustments; of 11 required units, the team directly managed only 7, with attempts to modify the 4 shared units failing or meeting resistance; heavy reliance on casual/sessional staff prevented distribution of material-writing workloads and overburdened the sole permanent instructor due to staff turnover risk. Epistemological and institutional tension between curricular philosophies and university systems: internal record-keeping and quality systems (structured in response to TEQSA requirements) required learning outcomes to be described in purely behavioral/positivist terms, making it impossible to formally register the "becoming/being" domain, described as crucial to practical professional ethical formation, because it could not be reduced to observable behaviors in the university's rigid forms; internal quality evaluations focused excessively on easily collected quantitative metrics (retention, satisfaction, under a "student-as-client" logic), inhibiting pedagogical experimentation due to instructors' fear of receiving scores below management-required averages. Academic work "intensification and performativity," wherein instructors prioritize research over teaching to satisfy performance metrics, is argued to clash with the real time and collaborative spaces needed for effective implementation.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — Action Learning as a low-cost, high-utility strategy for continuous professional development, particularly valuable for instructors from market/professional practice lacking formal pedagogical training; annual evaluation meetings with the full teaching staff (while funding allowed paid participation of casual staff); systematic internal review of units and materials; the multi-layered theoretical model (Barnett and Coate plus Threshold Concepts, COI, SOLO) providing an integrated design and alignment toolkit.

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — Two complete Action Learning cycles over 2013–2015, each combining dialogic questioning, planning, action, data observation, reflection, and replanning; systematic internal review of units and materials; detailed analysis of samples of student work and assessments; use of institutional data systems (retention, grades, student feedback) alongside team-collected qualitative feedback; reduction of "over-stuffed" course content guided by Threshold Concepts-based pruning.

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — The lack of continued funding after the pilot project made it unfeasible to pay casual staff to participate in annual evaluation meetings, reportedly diminishing the reach of team improvement efforts — an explicit sustainability limitation. The text does not explicitly propose future studies in a dedicated section but indirectly suggests that university management should investigate ways to create "buffers" against external regulatory requirements, to prevent reporting and documentation systems from stifling pedagogical innovation and the flexibility of course coordination. [DERIVED] Universities seeking to sustain curriculum renewal of this kind may need to revise workload models to reflect realistic redevelopment time, reduce reliance on precarious casual staffing for curriculum-critical work, and create documentation/quality-assurance mechanisms flexible enough to register non-behavioral learning domains such as "becoming/being" — a reasonable extension of the explicit findings into institutional recommendations, though the source frames most of these as barriers identified rather than as a fully elaborated set of policy recommendations, apart from the explicit "buffers" suggestion.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - Lack of time, team stability, and curricular control: the university's workload model allocated 39 hours for major unit redevelopment, but the team found actual time required was 200–250 hours per unit, not including subsequent adjustments.
  - Of 11 required units in the course, the team directly managed only 7; the remaining 4 were shared with other courses and taught by faculty outside the project, and attempts to modify them failed or met resistance.
  - Heavy reliance on casual/sessional staff prevented distribution of material-writing workloads and overburdened the sole permanent instructor (the project leader), due to staff turnover risk.
  - Internal record-keeping and quality systems, structured in response to TEQSA requirements, required learning outcomes to be described in purely behavioral/positivist terms, making it impossible to formally register the "becoming/being" domain.
  - Internal quality evaluations focused excessively on easily collected quantitative metrics (retention, satisfaction, under a "student-as-client" logic), which inhibited pedagogical experimentation due to instructors' fear of receiving scores below required averages.
  - Positive results: despite the barriers, application of the methods reduced "over-stuffed" course content and raised first-year student retention to over 90% in on-campus classes, even amid a 30% increase in enrollment (from 102 to 134 students).

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - The scarcity of academic discussion about curriculum theories is argued to have allowed "atheoretical" technocratic views of curriculum to become integrated as if neutral within university bureaucratic systems.
  - There is a chronic misalignment between an institutional positivist epistemology (favoring total control, metric predictability, and treating the student as a purchasing client) and social-constructivist/critical approaches (favoring fluid learning, development of subjective ethical virtues, and flexibility in response to student needs).
  - Effective curriculum implementation requires real time and collaborative spaces for assimilation, which clashes with contemporary trends of academic work intensification and performativity, wherein instructors prioritize research over teaching to satisfy performance metrics.
  - [DERIVED] Taken together, these arguments position the barriers identified in the findings (workload underestimation, documentation rigidity, metric-driven quality systems) not as isolated administrative problems but as symptoms of a deeper epistemological conflict between institutional (positivist/market) and pedagogical (critical/constructivist) logics — a connective reading supported by the explicit pairing of the "epistemological and institutional tension" findings category with the discussion's language of "chronic misalignment" between epistemologies.

**18. Contribution** — Theoretical: the study proposes and validates a multi-layered curricular model that integrates high-level pedagogical frameworks (Barnett and Coate) with practical unit- and assessment-level design and alignment tools (Threshold Concepts, COI, and SOLO). Methodological/practical: the study demonstrates that Action Learning is a low-cost, high-utility strategy for the continuous professional development of higher-education instructors, particularly in fields where most instructors come directly from market/professional practice and lack formal pedagogical training.

**19. Limitations** — Two author-acknowledged limitations: the study was conducted in a single specific course at a single university, has an exploratory character, and requires that generalization of findings depend on the reader's own judgment of similarity to their context; the lack of continued funding after the pilot project made it unfeasible to pay casual staff to participate in annual evaluation meetings, which reportedly diminished the reach of team improvement efforts. The text does not explicitly propose future studies in a dedicated section. [DERIVED] The discipline (Youth Work) is staffed substantially by field practitioners without formal pedagogical training, which may make some findings (e.g., the value of Action Learning for teachers without pedagogical background) less transferable to disciplines with differently composed academic staff. [DERIVED] The study's own workload-hours comparison (39 vs. 200–250 hours) is presented as a striking quantitative finding, but the source does not specify how these hours were measured, tracked, or validated (e.g., self-report, timesheets, estimation).

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — Implementation gap (design vs. enactment); workload underestimation/hidden labor of curriculum work; casualization/precarity of academic staffing as a structural barrier; documentation rigidity vs. non-behavioral learning domains; student-as-client/market logic in institutional quality metrics; positivist vs. constructivist/critical epistemological conflict; regulatory/accreditation pressure (TEQSA) shaping internal systems; Action Learning as method and intervention; retention and enrollment growth as (partial) success indicators; institutional flexibility/buffering against external compliance demands; the three-domain curriculum model (knowing/acting/becoming-being); Threshold Concepts as a device for prioritizing disciplinary depth over content coverage; academic work "intensification and performativity" as a structural constraint on teaching-related time.

**21. Tensions or assumptions** — The study's generalizability rests on a single course, in a single discipline (Youth Work), at a single university, over one specific two-year window; the discipline is staffed substantially by field practitioners without formal pedagogical training, which may make some findings less transferable to disciplines with differently composed academic staff. The workload-hours comparison (39 vs. 200–250 hours) is presented as a striking quantitative finding, but it is not specified how these hours were measured, tracked, or validated, which is relevant to assessing the strength of this claim. [DERIVED] The positive outcome of retention above 90% is reported alongside the finding that the "becoming/being" domain could not be captured in documentation; this juxtaposition raises the question — not addressed in the source — of whether the reform's success is being partly evaluated using the very positivist/behavioral metrics (retention) that the article otherwise critiques as reductionist, creating a possible internal tension between the article's critique of metric-driven evaluation and its own use of a retention metric as evidence of success. [DERIVED] The same retention rise, combined with the reported "over-stuffed" content reduction, could also imply that curriculum reform on the "acting"/"knowing" domains had measurable positive effects even while the "becoming/being" domain remained bureaucratically invisible — suggesting that institutional quality metrics may register some positive effects of reform while systematically failing to register others; the source does not explicitly draw this connection between the metric-based results and the domain framework. The barriers are framed largely as originating from external/institutional constraints (workload models, TEQSA-driven documentation, quality metrics) rather than from the multi-layered curricular model's own design choices; whether the model itself contributed to implementation difficulty (e.g., through the complexity of integrating five distinct frameworks) is not explored and represents an assumption left unchallenged within the available material.
