## A16 — Integrative review synthesizing ten curriculum-development models into a four-step framework for undergraduate medical education

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study addresses the development and structuring of undergraduate medical curricula based on theoretical models. The problem is framed around a tension between the need for scientifically grounded, standardized curriculum development and the practical confusion medical educators face when trying to apply the many available theoretical models, given the differences between the stages/steps of each model. A specific figure is attributed to unspecified prior research: "nearly 40% of medical schools have a mismatch between curriculum content and teaching objectives due to inappropriate selection of generic models." The authors identify a gap in existing literature reviews, which focus predominantly on evaluation models and satisfaction indicators, lacking a systematic and comprehensive compilation of the specific theoretical models that guide curriculum development itself (as opposed to its evaluation). The relevance argument is resource-oriented: inadequate model selection is said to waste human, material, and financial resources, while scientific rigor and standardization in curricular design are presented as directly affecting teaching-learning effectiveness and student knowledge absorption.

**2. Object of investigation** — At a meta-level: the published theoretical models of curriculum development and the empirical studies that applied them to undergraduate medical curricula, rather than a single curriculum, student population, or institution. The primary analytic units are the 18 empirical articles that explicitly used a named theoretical model and reported development-process details, and the 10 theoretical models these articles instantiate.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — NOT APPLICABLE as a single setting: the reviewed body of empirical literature spans studies published between 2013 and 2024, drawn from multiple countries, explicitly listed as China, Iran, India, the USA, Brazil, Canada, Ireland, and Australia. This is the context of the 18 empirical studies synthesized by the review, not a single primary-research setting; the review itself is a secondary/integrative study operating over this multi-country literature rather than investigating one institutional or national context directly.

**4. Study or article type** — Integrative literature review.

**5. Methodological approach** — An integrative literature review following the five-stage methodological guidelines of Whittemore and Knafl (2005): problem identification, literature search, data evaluation, data analysis, and presentation of results. Eight electronic databases consulted: PubMed, Embase, The Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CINAHL, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP, searched from database inception through December 31, 2024. Eligibility criteria: studies focused on establishing curricula for undergraduate medical students that explicitly used a theoretical model in their design and provided specific process details; restricted to English- or Chinese-language publications. Screening yielded 1,672 initial records, reduced to 1,322 after deduplication, 115 selected for full-text review, and 18 empirical articles finally included in the synthesis; screening disagreements were resolved by consensus or a third reviewer. Profile of included studies: 3 qualitative, 6 quantitative, and 9 mixed-methods studies. Quality appraisal was performed independently by two researchers using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT, 2018 version); no eligible article was excluded on quality grounds. Data analysis used content analysis technique, evaluating logical connections and relationships between the steps of the different models in order to integrate them into a unified framework. The operational procedure by which "logical connections" between model steps were identified and merged into the four-step framework is NOT REPORTED beyond naming the technique as content analysis.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — The premise that curriculum development is a process of curricular reconstruction influenced by social and academic context (the source's own descriptor, rendered in the underlying Portuguese-language note as "social e acadêmico"). Ten principal theoretical models are mapped: Kern's six-step method, the ADDIE model, Fink's integrated course design model, the DACUM (Develop A Curriculum) model, the Taylor model, the Participatory Curriculum Development (PCD) model, a seven-step model, Steketee's four-dimensional framework, the Siragusa model, and the Talbot and Verrinder model. These models are segmented into two groups: models centered on student competencies (DACUM, PCD, Siragusa) and models centered on learning objectives (ADDIE, Fink, Taylor, Kern, seven-step, Talbot & Verrinder, Steketee). Whether this two-group typology is the authors' own analytical construction or a classification already established in prior literature is NOT REPORTED.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — The 18 empirical articles that explicitly used a named theoretical model and reported development-process details, and the 10 theoretical models these articles instantiate; the review's units are secondary (previously published studies), not primary curricula or institutions.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — NOT APPLICABLE as a single traceable process: the review synthesizes findings across 18 heterogeneous curriculum-development instances rather than documenting one institution's redesign. The synthesized output is an "Integrated Development Framework" (Fig. 2 in the source) unifying the steps of the 10 analyzed models into a four-step structure: Step 1 — Situational analysis of the curriculum (mapping social context, health policy, school structure, and student needs/characteristics; Fink's model is highlighted as the most robust for this phase); Step 2 — Definition of curricular objectives (establishing clear goals focused on knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values); Step 3 — Design of curricular content (planning of pedagogical content, teaching strategies/methods, and development of educational resources such as handouts and books); Step 4 — Curriculum implementation and evaluation (course execution with attention to motivation and interaction, and structuring of formative/ongoing and summative/final assessments).

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — Medical educators/curriculum developers (the review's intended practical audience); two independent researchers performing quality appraisal; a third reviewer resolving screening disagreements; students, as the source of qualitative feedback data within the 18 included empirical studies (e.g., dissatisfaction with scheduling, practical activities, and textbooks).

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The four-step Integrated Development Framework (situational analysis, objective definition, content design, implementation/evaluation) as the review's central decision structure. Model selection is argued to require alignment with course/discipline context and objectives — e.g., Kern as highly specific to general medical curricula, Fink as ideal for integrating theoretical and affective learning in general higher education, and DACUM as focused on professional-competency analysis in technical courses. The two-group model typology (competency-centered vs. objective-centered) also functions as a classification dimension for model selection.

**11. Contextual conditions** — Literature restricted to English- and Chinese-language publications; multi-country empirical base (China, Iran, India, USA, Brazil, Canada, Ireland, Australia) spanning 2013–2024; three study-design types represented (3 qualitative, 6 quantitative, 9 mixed-methods). Online/hybrid teaching modality is identified as a moderating condition affecting practical-skill acquisition within the underlying studies.

**12. Barriers** — Inadequate scheduling (courses too short or confusingly timed, harming comprehensive/systematic learning); insufficient offline practical activities (particularly in online/hybrid teaching, where lack of in-person simulation and real patient contact hindered translating theory into practical skill); inappropriate or inadequate textbook selection (absence of standardized textbooks impeding review, and student preference for concise handouts over dense research articles early in learning). At the field level: confusion among medical educators applying the many available theoretical models due to differences between each model's stages/steps, and a reported ~40% mismatch between curriculum content and teaching objectives attributed to inappropriate generic-model selection (source of this statistic NOT REPORTED beyond "some research data").

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — Model-context fit — selecting a theoretical model aligned with the specific course/discipline and its objectives. Attention to class time, in-person practical activities, and textbook quality, argued by the authors as necessary to achieve proposed implementation outcomes. Mixed-methods curricular evaluation logic: quantitative data is argued to highlight problems, while qualitative analysis is argued to be needed to ascertain their causes, jointly supporting ongoing curricular improvement (direct quotation preserved: "Quantitative data can highlight issues, while qualitative analysis can ascertain the causes, thereby enhancing the curriculum and promoting its ongoing development.").

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — The four-step Integrated Development Framework itself, intended as a simplified, systematic replacement for navigating the 10 source models individually. Formative (ongoing) and summative (final) assessment structuring within Step 4. Of the 18 articles, 13 tested the proposed curricula in practice, and all of these implementations reported significant quantitative efficacy, with substantial increases in theoretical knowledge and practical skill scores post-course.

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — NOT REPORTED as a tracked construct in this review; the source instead records that future field research is needed to empirically validate the proposed four-step integrated framework in academic medical practice, implying the framework itself has not yet been empirically tested as a unified whole within this study. The source frames the framework as intended to guide future pedagogical curricular reforms in a simplified and high-quality way, grounded in the synthesized scientific evidence, and records the implication that developers should specifically attend to scheduling/time adequacy, in-person practical activities, and textbook/material quality as implementation-stage priorities, since these were identified as sources of student dissatisfaction across the reviewed studies.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - An "Integrated Development Framework" (Fig. 2) unifying the steps of the 10 analyzed models into a four-step structure: Step 1 — situational analysis (mapping social context, health policy, school structure, student needs/characteristics; Fink's model highlighted as most robust for this phase); Step 2 — definition of curricular objectives (knowledge, skills, attitudes, values); Step 3 — design of curricular content (pedagogical content, teaching strategies/methods, educational resources); Step 4 — curriculum implementation and evaluation (course execution, formative and summative assessment).
  - Curricular efficacy: of the 18 articles, 13 tested the proposed curricula in practice; all of these implementations reported significant quantitative efficacy, with substantial increases in theoretical knowledge and practical skill scores post-course.
  - Deficiencies and dissatisfaction factors, drawn from qualitative student feedback: inadequate scheduling; insufficient offline practical activities (particularly in online/hybrid teaching); inappropriate or inadequate textbook selection.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - Model selection should align directly with the context and objectives of the course/discipline — e.g., Kern as highly specific to general medical curricula, Fink as ideal for integrating theoretical and affective learning in general higher education, and DACUM as focused on professional-competency analysis in technical courses.
  - The proposed four-step framework is argued to simplify and accelerate curriculum development relative to traditional models — described as containing "intricate procedures" and "protracted development cycles" — by eliminating redundancies while preserving systematicity; it is also argued to fill a gap left by theories that focused only on process sequencing without detailing the internal content of each step ("link").
  - Curriculum developers should attend strictly to class time, in-person practical activities, and textbook quality to achieve proposed implementation outcomes.
  - Quantitative data is argued to highlight problems, while qualitative analysis is argued to be needed to ascertain their causes, jointly supporting ongoing curricular improvement: "Quantitative data can highlight issues, while qualitative analysis can ascertain the causes, thereby enhancing the curriculum and promoting its ongoing development."

**18. Contribution** — Theoretical: the first systematic compilation of curriculum design models in medical education, presenting an integrated four-step framework said to resolve the dispersion of prior theories. Practical: a practical, evidence-based reference for educators to guide course-design decisions, plus an empirical warning about common errors compromising student satisfaction. Methodological: a detailed methodological example of integrating qualitative and quantitative data within integrative reviews in health education, using the MMAT framework.

**19. Limitations** — Exclusive inclusion of English- and Chinese-language publications, which may have excluded relevant curricular evidence published in other languages. Exclusive focus on studies that explicitly mentioned an applied theoretical model, which may have excluded curriculum-development approaches or models not yet formally reported in the medical education literature. The authors indicate a need for future field research to empirically validate the proposed four-step integrated framework in academic medical practice — implying, though not stated as an explicit limitation label, that the framework itself has not yet been empirically tested as a unified whole within this study.

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — Distinction between curriculum models centered on student competencies versus models centered on learning objectives; situational analysis as a foundational first step in curriculum design (mapping social/political/institutional context and student characteristics/needs); formative versus summative assessment as complementary evaluation components within curriculum implementation; mixed-methods curricular evaluation logic — quantitative data as a signal of problems, qualitative data as an explanatory mechanism for causes; scheduling/time adequacy as an implementation-stage barrier to curricular effectiveness; adequacy of hands-on/in-person practical activities as a factor in translating theoretical knowledge into practical skill, particularly relevant in online/hybrid teaching modalities; adequacy and format of instructional materials (standardized textbooks versus concise handouts versus dense research articles) as a factor in learner satisfaction; integrative review methodology (Whittemore and Knafl, 2005) as a synthesis approach for heterogeneous qualitative/quantitative/mixed-methods literature; use of the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) for quality appraisal across study designs; model-context fit as a selection criterion for choosing among multiple available theoretical frameworks. Additional candidate codes: curriculum development framework integration/synthesis; theoretical model selection criteria and model-context alignment; curriculum-objective misalignment as a systemic problem in medical education; competency-centered versus objective-centered curriculum design paradigms; implementation barriers (scheduling, practical/hands-on activity availability, instructional material adequacy); standardization and scientificity as normative goals for curriculum design; online/hybrid teaching modality as a moderating condition for practical-skill acquisition.

**21. Tensions or assumptions** — The "nearly 40% of medical schools have a mismatch" statistic is attributed only to unspecified "research data" ("Some research data show that..."); the source does not identify the source study, sample, or methodology behind this figure, so its evidentiary weight cannot be assessed — it functions rhetorically to establish problem severity without traceable support. [DERIVED] The claim that all 13 implementations tested in practice reported significant quantitative efficacy raises a possible selection/publication-bias concern: the eligibility criteria required studies that explicitly used a theoretical model and reported process details, which may itself select for published success stories (studies with null or negative results, or those not explicitly framed around a named model, would have been excluded by design) — the source does not address this possibility, so the 100% positive-efficacy result plausibly reflects the selection filter rather than a general property of theory-based curricula. [DERIVED] The four-step integrated framework is presented as simplifying and accelerating curriculum development while preserving systematicity, but the source indicates this framework has not yet been empirically validated as a unified whole (future field validation is called for); the claimed advantages (simplification, redundancy elimination) are thus argumentative/synthetic claims about the review's own output rather than findings independently tested within the study. How the four-step framework relates to or resolves the prior two-group typology (competency-centered versus objective-centered models) is not clarified — whether the new framework favors one orientation, merges both without tension, or leaves the distinction analytically unresolved is NOT REPORTED. [DERIVED] The restriction to English- and Chinese-language literature, combined with the requirement for explicit theoretical-model usage, may bias the resulting "comprehensive" framework toward curricular traditions and institutional contexts that are more likely to publish in these languages and more likely to formally theorize their design process, potentially underrepresenting informal, iterative, or regionally specific curriculum-design practices.
