## A06 — Language-versus-application gap in departmental enactment of a systemic curriculum reform (Kandiko Howson & Kingsbury, 2023)

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study (Kandiko Howson & Kingsbury, 2023, "Curriculum change as transformational learning," *Teaching in Higher Education*) addresses systemic curriculum change in higher education, specifically how central strategic policy guidelines are translated, interpreted, and enacted by different departmental cultures and communities. Despite growing regulatory pressures forcing universities to collect data and adopt large-scale reforms, the dynamics of how such centralized strategic policies are actually enacted in the everyday contexts of departments is poorly understood. Evaluating large-scale curricular reforms is described as notoriously complex due to long maturation periods and the large number of variables involved, and centralized policies risk remaining formal marketing-brochure rhetoric without altering the cultural and teaching practices of the disciplines.

**2. Object of investigation** — The documented (written, planned) curricular response of 15 undergraduate departments to an institution-wide strategic Learning and Teaching reform policy — specifically the textual traces of that response across strategy documents, departmental redesign forms, and programme specifications. The object is explicitly the planned/intended curriculum as represented in official documents, not the curriculum as taught or experienced.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — A highly decentralized ("highly devolved"), medium-sized, research-intensive institution in the United Kingdom (unnamed). The study was conducted in the second year of a systemic curricular reform program supported by a nine-year investment plan. Fifteen undergraduate departments distributed across three distinct Faculties were independently evaluated based on their documentation. The analytic focus is the "planned/intended curriculum" derived from official documents at a specific time cut, rather than the curriculum as enacted in classrooms.

**4. Study or article type** — Qualitative evaluative research adopting a linguistic ethnography approach and critical discourse analysis, structured as a longitudinal case study focused on planned/intended curriculum documentation. The research question investigates the extent to which departments transitioned from traditional curricula (based purely on disciplinary content transmission and high-stakes exams) toward active incorporation of active-learning approaches and broader institutional objectives into their own language, pedagogy, and practices. The objective was evaluating the degree of engagement and alignment of academic departments with the guidelines and actual intent of the reform policy, and capturing its impact on disciplinary and institutional culture at the early stage of the reform process.

**5. Methodological approach** — Three types of policy texts were cross-analyzed: (1) the Learning and Teaching Strategy, a centralizing document structured around four pillars — Assessment Reform, Active Learning, Diversity and Inclusion, and Digital Technologies; (2) Curriculum Redesign Forms, internal departmental documents justifying curricular changes and detailing their relation to the strategic pillars; (3) Programme Specifications, contractually binding official documents directed at students and quality assurance, described as functioning as a "negotiated pact." Researchers developed an evaluation rubric assessing the four strategic pillars along three dimensions — Language (use of strategy terminology), Intent (incorporation of the spirit of the reforms), and Application (declared practical implementation) — applied to 12 indicators in the Redesign Forms and 16 indicators across four sections of the Programme Specifications, totaling 28 indicators. Two independent analyses rated each section on a categorical 1–5 scale (Absent, Vague, Implicit, Present, Explicit).

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Transformational Learning Theory, drawing on Mezirow, Habermas, and Freire, used as the lens for conceptualizing corporate/institutional curriculum change as a "disorienting dilemma," distinguishing deep structural transformations in "habits of mind" from superficial formal adaptations ("points of view"). Policy Enactment, based on Ball et al., which analyzes policies not as instructions mechanically applied but as processes actively interpreted and recreated within institutional cultures by diverse actors. Curriculum conceptions drawing on Biggs' constructive alignment, Barnett and Coate's know-act-be framework, and Bernstein's framing of valid knowledge. Dynamics of curricular change balancing top-down centralized policy command and bottom-up local disciplinary engagement, described through the terms "introjection" (disciplinar) and "projection" of external demands.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — Fifteen undergraduate departments across three Faculties at a single research-intensive UK university, each independently evaluated based on its policy documentation (Redesign Forms, Programme Specifications) against the institution's Learning and Teaching Strategy. NOT APPLICABLE: individual student, classroom-level, or cross-institutional comparative analysis.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — The study evaluates the second year of a nine-year, institution-wide systemic curricular reform program, focusing on how the reform policy (organized around four pillars) is translated into departmental documentation. It is not itself an account of designing or implementing a specific curriculum, but an evaluative snapshot of a reform-in-progress across 15 departments, tracing textual uptake from the central strategy document through departmental redesign forms to programme specifications.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — Fifteen undergraduate departments across three Faculties; central institutional policy authors (Learning and Teaching Strategy); departmental staff completing Curriculum Redesign Forms; quality-assurance bodies overseeing Programme Specifications; external professional, statutory, and regulatory bodies (PSRBs), reported as exerting substantial influence on local curriculum definitions; "pedagogical translators" (educational designers, technologists, disciplinary pedagogy specialists) identified as intermediary actors bridging central goals and disciplinary specificities; two independent analysts who rated the rubric.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The three-dimensional rubric: Language (use of strategy terminology), Intent (incorporation of the spirit of the reforms), and Application (declared practical implementation), each rated 1–5 (Absent, Vague, Implicit, Present, Explicit); applied across the four strategic pillars (Assessment Reform, Active Learning, Diversity and Inclusion, Digital Technologies); 12 indicators in the Redesign Forms and 16 indicators across four Programme Specification sections (28 indicators total).

**11. Contextual conditions** — A highly decentralized, medium-sized, research-intensive UK university; a nine-year institutional investment plan supporting the reform; pre-existing institutional culture historically organized around high-stakes exams (70%–90% of final grades in many departments); digital technology historically treated as the responsibility of central technical support units rather than departmental pedagogical responsibility.

**12. Barriers** — A conflict between disciplinary identity and central structure: learning outcomes formulated largely around disciplines' own content with little institutional influence; external regulatory forces from professional accreditation bodies (PSRBs) exerting substantially more power over local curriculum definitions than central institutional policies; entrenched traditional assessment structures (high-stakes exams as 70%–90% of final grades) resisting the Assessment Reform and Active Learning pillars in most departments; the Diversity and Inclusion pillar's terminology absent in half of curricular proposals and rarely translated into classroom applications; the Digital Technologies pillar almost entirely omitted from departmental ownership.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — Linguistic incorporation of institutional terminology, argued by the authors to function as a necessary (if insufficient) threshold for mindset change — even cosmetic linguistic conformity in passive departments may represent the beginning of a minimal contractual agreement enabling later reforms; the presence of "pedagogical translators" (educational designers, technologists, disciplinary pedagogy specialists) bridging central goals and disciplinary specificities; disciplinary fusion of strategic pillars into departments' own identity and content, as observed in Active departments (e.g., Medicine, integrating social and health diversity into learning outcomes).

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — NOT APPLICABLE in the sense of a single implementation protocol — the study documents heterogeneous departmental mechanisms rather than one common process: Active departments created specific assessment plans integrating strategic pillars into disciplinary content; Engaged departments implemented isolated, punctual innovations (e.g., Team-Based Learning in some modules) without deeply restructuring general teaching conceptions; Passive departments enacted strictly bureaucratic compliance focused on regulatory adjustments (credits, module rearrangement), using reform terminology cosmetically ("linguistic mimicry") for approval and funding access while maintaining an exclusive focus on traditional disciplinary content.

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — NOT REPORTED as an achieved outcome — the study is an early-stage (second-year), time-bound, situated snapshot of a nine-year reform program, and by design does not track longer-term institutionalization. The authors' stated future-research agenda (see field 19) — investigating continued practical execution, transition into the classroom, and impacts on actual student learning outcomes — implies that institutionalization/sustainability had not yet been assessed within the reported study.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - A "gap" between language and application: engagement with the technical language of reform was significantly higher than engagement with its intent or practical application. On average, there was a full categorical decline in rubric rating (e.g., from "Present" to "Implicit") when moving from the linguistic level to concrete application across the four pillars.
  - Unequal engagement across pillars: Assessment Reform and Active Learning pillars achieved high receptivity at the language level ("Present" or "Explicit" in all departments), but practical execution was uneven — only about one-fifth of departments explicitly applied the guidelines, while others retained traditional structures based on high-stakes exams as the dominant method (with exams representing 70%–90% of final grades).
  - Diversity and Inclusion and Digital Technologies pillars registered low adherence: Diversity and Inclusion terminology was absent in half of curricular proposals and rarely translated into classroom applications; the digital area was almost entirely omitted, historically treated as the responsibility of central technical support units rather than a departmental pedagogical responsibility.
  - A three-cluster typology of departmental response emerged: Active departments (~1/3), which fused disciplinary identity and content with the strategic pillars and created specific assessment plans (e.g., Medicine, integrating social and health diversity into learning outcomes); Engaged departments (~1/3), which showed receptiveness through isolated, punctual innovations without deeply restructuring general teaching conceptions; and Passive departments (~1/3), which showed strictly bureaucratic compliance, using reform terminology cosmetically for approval and funding access while maintaining an exclusive focus on traditional disciplinary content.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - Linguistic incorporation of institutional terminology functions as a necessary but insufficient threshold ("Language as Threshold") for mindset change; linguistic conformity even in passive departments may represent the beginning of a minimal contractual agreement enabling later reforms.
  - There is a conflict between disciplinary identity and central structure: learning outcomes are formulated largely around disciplines' own content with little institutional influence, and external regulatory forces (PSRBs) exert substantially more power over local curriculum definitions than central institutional policies.
  - At systemic scale, curriculum review operates as a disorienting dilemma stimulating transformational learning, but real and sustainable pedagogical reform occurs only when institutional objectives are fused into each discipline's own pedagogy and epistemology through the action of disciplinary specialists and translators.

**18. Contribution** — Theoretical: an unprecedented application of Mezirow's Transformational Learning Theory to institutional governance and higher-education policy, explaining processes of resistance and reception of curricular innovations. Methodological: development and validation of a systematic evaluation instrument (a 28-indicator multi-criteria rubric) applicable for other universities to monitor and critically analyze their own curricular change processes. Practical: demonstration that reforms do not occur in a purely vertical (top-down) fashion, and identification of the role of "pedagogical translators" — such as educational designers, technologists, and disciplinary pedagogy specialists — in bridging central goals and disciplinary specificities.

**19. Limitations** — Author-stated: the linguistic-ethnography design studies exclusively written representations in formal documents, without directly observing the curriculum as enacted in classrooms, informal teaching dynamics, or informal modifications over time; there is a possibility of "political linguistic mimicry," where departments use the correct keywords cosmetically to secure promised additional funding or simply to satisfy formal bureaucratic requirements; the study represents a time-bound, situated snapshot analysis. Future-research agenda stated by the authors: investigating continued practical execution, the transition into the classroom, and impacts on actual student learning outcomes; evaluating how additional external disruptive factors (e.g., the Covid-19 pandemic or the Black Lives Matter movement) may have impacted or accelerated the diversity and technology guidelines initially mapped.

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — Curricular reform as multi-level (rhetorical, intentional, applied) phenomenon; gap/decoupling between formal policy language and enacted practice; disciplinary culture as mediator/filter of institutional policy; document-based rubric evaluation of policy enactment; typologization of organizational-unit responses to top-down change (active/engaged/passive); transformational learning applied at organizational/institutional scale; external accreditation/regulatory bodies as competing authority over curriculum; intermediary/translator roles in policy-to-practice pipelines; assessment-heavy traditional curricula as inertial structures resisting reform (70–90% exam weighting); uneven adoption across reform "pillars" depending on perceived disciplinary relevance/ownership; "disorienting dilemma" (Mezirow) as a lens for institutional-, not only individual-, level transformational learning; policy enactment (Ball et al.) as active interpretation/recreation rather than mechanical implementation.

**21. Tensions or assumptions** — The study's evidentiary base is exclusively formal written documentation; this is flagged by the authors as a limitation, since it cannot observe classroom enactment, informal practices, or informal changes over time. [DERIVED] This raises a tension between the study's strong empirical claims about a "gap" between language and application and the fact that "application" itself is measured only as declared/documented application, not observed practice — meaning the measured "application" score may itself be another form of textual representation subject to the same mimicry risk identified for "language." [DERIVED] The rubric-based methodology assumes that a five-point categorical scale, rated by two independent analysts, can reliably capture qualitative differences in "intent" — a notably interpretive and arguably more subjective construct than "language" (presence of terms) or declared "application." No inter-rater reliability statistics or resolution procedures for rating disagreements are reported. The three-way typology (Active/Engaged/Passive) partitions departments into roughly equal thirds; it is not indicated whether this even distribution is a substantive empirical finding or partly an artifact of how cluster boundaries were defined, since no explicit clustering methodology (e.g., statistical clustering criteria) is described beyond qualitative categorization. The claim that PSRB accreditation exerts more influence over curricula than central institutional policy is a strong comparative claim; how this comparison was operationalized or measured is not described beyond its appearance in the discussion section, so its evidentiary basis relative to the main rubric findings is unclear.

**22. Note-layer caveats specific to this study** — The note does not specify: the exact name or discipline scope of the university studied (anonymized as a research-intensive, highly devolved UK institution); the specific 15 departments beyond the example of Medicine; the inter-rater reliability or agreement statistics between the two independent raters; the precise procedure by which departments were sorted into the Active/Engaged/Passive clusters (e.g., whether based on aggregate rubric scores, qualitative judgment, or a defined threshold); sample excerpts or examples for the Diversity and Inclusion or Digital Technologies pillars comparable to the Medicine example given for Active departments; any statistical significance testing associated with the reported "full category drop" pattern; and details of how the study defines or bounds "the second year" of the nine-year reform program in relation to the document snapshot analyzed. The note also does not report whether the study included any interview, survey, or observational data beyond the three document types, which would be necessary to assess claims about actors' motivations (e.g., attributing "cosmetic" language use to funding-seeking behavior).
