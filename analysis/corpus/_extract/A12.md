## A12 — Mixed-methods study of how experienced educational developers initiate curriculum revision

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study investigates the process of initiating curriculum revision in higher education, examined through the lens and practice of educational developers (referred to in the source as "assessores pedagógicos"/"desenvolvedores educacionais"). The motivating problem is a gap between abundant theoretical literature on curriculum development and design and a scarcity of practice-oriented literature on how to actually begin a revision process — a gap that affects both academic staff and novice educational developers. Reviewing an existing curriculum is described as more intimidating and difficult than designing one from scratch, and educational developers often work in isolation, come from varied disciplinary backgrounds, and lack "role models" or theory-to-practice guides for this specific activity. The relevance argument is that, in a dynamic higher-education landscape, many academics are required to revise curricula without prior experience and turn to educational developers who may themselves lack empirical reference points for how to approach this initial mediating stage.

**2. Object of investigation** — The practices and approaches of educational developers at the point of initiating curriculum revision — i.e., professional practice rather than curriculum content or outcomes per se. A secondary, closely linked object is the set of contextual and dispositional factors that shape those practices. The study does not examine curricula themselves (their content, structure, or quality) as an object; it is explicitly restricted to the initiation stage of the revision process, not later implementation or evaluation — a restriction the source itself frames as a limitation.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — Higher education in Ireland and the United Kingdom. Phase 1 interview participants were six experienced educational developers active in Irish higher education, some with prior international experience; Phase 2 questionnaire respondents were drawn from the professional networks SEDA (UK) and EDIN (Ireland), with the 22 returned questionnaires coming from Ireland (10), the UK (10), the Netherlands (1), and South Africa (1). Institution types (e.g., research-intensive vs. teaching-focused), disciplinary settings of the academic staff being advised, and the historical/policy period in which the fieldwork occurred are NOT REPORTED (only that literature references extend to 2009).

**4. Study or article type** — Empirical mixed-methods study (qualitative interviews plus quantitative/qualitative questionnaire).

**5. Methodological approach** — Sequential mixed-methods design in two phases. Phase 1 (qualitative) involved six purposively ("amostragem seletiva") selected experienced educational developers, using in-depth, unstructured qualitative interviews prioritizing natural dialogue flow; data were recorded, coded with assistance of the Annotape software package, grouped into categories via constant comparison, and credibility-checked with another educational developer. Phase 2 (quantitative/qualitative) involved a semi-structured postal questionnaire (open and closed questions) sent to 74 educational developers in the SEDA and EDIN networks, yielding 22 completed returns (30% response rate); analysis consisted of descriptive frequency analysis of starting-point choices (reported in a Table 1) plus qualitative analysis of open responses. **6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — A product vs. process contrast in curriculum models: the product model (plans, transparent objectives, means-ends logic, attributed to Tyler and Popham & Baker, and linked to the Bologna Process in Europe) versus the process model (activities, flexibility, creativity, student experience, attributed to Stenhouse and Knight). Stark's (2000) two-phase curriculum-planning "filters" model, involving exploration of staff beliefs/considerations followed by filtering of student and contextual influences. Diamond's (1998) approach to needs assessment. Toohey's (2000) emphasis on debating staff beliefs, values, and pedagogical ideologies before fixing goals and content. Biggs's (2003) constructive alignment, linking learning objectives to teaching and assessment activities. Land's (2004) ecological model of educational developer professional orientations, specifically the "internal consultant" and "interpretative-hermeneutic" orientations, emphasizing situated cognition, attentive listening, and departmental context over managerial postures.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — Individual educational developers as practitioners: six in-depth interviewees (Phase 1) and 22 questionnaire respondents (Phase 2), analyzed for their practice approaches and the factors influencing those approaches, rather than specific curricula, programs, or institutions.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — The initiation stage of curriculum revision specifically (not design-from-scratch, implementation, or evaluation). Developers report needing to listen carefully and question staff about the real motivators for change; academics rarely request help with "the curriculum as a whole" but tend to focus on specific pain points (content overload, assessment). Key questions cited include "Por que você está fazendo mudanças?" and "O que os estudantes precisam saber?" (rendered in the source as translated Portuguese rather than as English direct quotes). Four broad initial pathways were identified in interviews: (1) focus on graduate profile ("graduateness"); (2) exploration of the program's educational philosophy; (3) presentation of research related to the change driver; (4) introduction of theoretical curriculum models. Questionnaire data showed a strong majority preference for starting with learning objectives/outcomes (43.47% at program level, 40.90% at module level), with graduate-profile exploration also prominent, but little interest in starting with research data on change motivators — in contrast to interview participants, among whom "presentation of research" was one of the four identified pathways. All participants agreed that discussion of specific subject content (disciplines/isolated topics) should be the last step in the planning process.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — Educational developers (six Phase 1 interviewees; 22 Phase 2 questionnaire respondents from SEDA-UK and EDIN-Ireland networks) as the primary study participants; academic staff/faculty as the "clients" being advised on curriculum revision (their disciplinary or institutional-type context is NOT REPORTED); students referenced indirectly through the developer question "What do students need to know?" but not as direct data sources.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The four identified initial pathways (graduate profile, educational philosophy, research presentation, theoretical curriculum models); the product-model vs. process-model orientation distinction; Stark's two-phase filters (staff beliefs/considerations, then student/contextual influences); the consensus principle that content-specific discussion should be sequenced last. Decision-making by developers is strongly influenced by staff discipline, team energy/motivation, institutional strategy, and available time.

**11. Contextual conditions** — Fieldwork situated in Irish and UK higher education, drawing on professional development networks (SEDA, EDIN); minority representation from the Netherlands and South Africa in the questionnaire sample. Literature references extend to 2009, the only available indicator of study currency, since publication year, venue, and DOI are NOT REPORTED. The sample is small (6 interviewees; 22 of 74 invited questionnaire respondents, 30% response rate) and geographically concentrated in Ireland and the UK, with only two of 22 respondents from elsewhere.

**12. Barriers** — Educational developers often work in isolation, come from varied disciplinary backgrounds, and lack "role models" or theory-to-practice guides for the initiation stage specifically. There is an absence of data demonstrating measurable effectiveness of the suggested methodological approaches; success is instead assessed only through informal, intuitive means by developers (e.g., "as pessoas retornam," paraphrased in the source rather than given as an English direct quote). There is a lack of systemic institutional evaluation to measure the effectiveness of the pedagogical-advising strategies adopted.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — Intensive preliminary dialogue and careful listening by developers as the mechanism enabling successful initiation ("a long, long dialogue," direct quotation preserved from the source). Methodological flexibility — developers show no allegiance to a single universal recipe and instead tailor approach to institutional and stakeholder context. Although most developers practically adopt the product model (oriented toward learning outcomes and constructive alignment), they simultaneously draw on process-model strategies (listening, contextualization, respect for staff's pedagogical philosophy) — framed as a hybrid or dual orientation rather than a pure allegiance to either model, which the source treats as functionally supportive of successful initiation.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - Initiating curriculum revision is a situated process benefiting from deep initial listening and should not be rushed, requiring "a long, long dialogue."
  - There are no universal recipes; developers must show methodological flexibility and tailor their approach to institutional and stakeholder context.
  - Although most developers practically adopt the product model (oriented toward learning outcomes and constructive alignment), they simultaneously draw on process-model strategies (listening, contextualization, respect for staff's pedagogical philosophy) — a hybrid or dual orientation rather than a pure allegiance to either model.
  - There is a lack of systemic institutional evaluation to measure the effectiveness of the pedagogical-advising strategies adopted.

**18. Contribution** — Three contributions are attributed to the study: (1) a practical contribution — an integrative conceptual model (Figure 1) systematizing the stages and factors of curriculum initiation, intended as a strategic guide for novice developers and academic staff; (2) a competency-development contribution — highlighting the centrality of interpersonal communication, listening, and negotiation skills in the educational developer's role; (3) an advocacy contribution — evidencing the urgent need for continuing-education programs and "role-modeling" practices in which senior developers make their actions transparent to support newcomers' formation.

**19. Limitations** — The small size of the Phase 2 questionnaire sample (22 respondents, 30% response rate); the strict restriction of the study to the initiation phase only, excluding implementation of new teaching methodologies, assessment strategies, or long-term curricular impact evaluation; and the absence of data demonstrating measurable effectiveness of the suggested methodological approaches, with success instead assessed only through informal, intuitive means by developers. Suggested future research includes investigating why particular starting approaches (outcomes, philosophy, or graduate profile) are chosen across different disciplinary/organizational contexts, and follow-up studies tracking later stages of curriculum development (implementation and systematic evaluation).

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — The product vs. process distinction in curriculum models (objectives/plans/means-ends logic vs. activities/flexibility/experience-orientation); constructive alignment (Biggs) as a framework linking learning outcomes, teaching activities, and assessment; Stark's two-phase curriculum-planning "filters" (staff beliefs/considerations, then student/context influences) as a heuristic for sequencing curriculum design conversations; the distinction between "internal consultant" and "interpretative-hermeneutic" professional orientations for educational/curriculum developers (Land's ecological model); the notion of curriculum revision initiation as a distinct, under-theorized phase, separable from design, implementation, and evaluation phases; "graduateness"/graduate profile as an entry point for curriculum discussions; the idea that content-level (disciplinary/topic) decisions should be sequenced last in curriculum planning discussions; role-modeling and mentoring as mechanisms for transmitting tacit professional practice knowledge among practitioners; dialogic/listening-based professional practice as a counterweight to managerial or purely instrumental advisory postures. Additional candidate codes: curriculum-revision initiation as a distinct process stage; educational developer professional identity and isolation; learning-outcomes-first vs. philosophy-first vs. graduate-profile-first entry points; product model vs. process model hybridity in practice; content sequencing (content-last consensus); contextual/situational factors shaping advisory decisions (discipline, team motivation, institutional strategy, time); absence of effectiveness measurement / reliance on informal success indicators; mentoring and role-modeling gap for novice developers; flexibility vs. formal curriculum models ("not a strait jacket").
