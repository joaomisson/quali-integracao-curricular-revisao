## A15 — Case-survey meta-analysis clustering 131 HEI cases into six patterns of sustainability curriculum institutionalization

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The article investigates curriculum change processes for embedding Education for Sustainable Development (ESD) in higher education institutions (HEIs), examining how sustainability is integrated institutionally and transversally into curricula. The research problem is framed as a gap: while numerous individual case studies describe successful sustainability integration in isolated courses or programs, little is known about the systematic implementation processes that lead to genuinely comprehensive curricular institutionalization (paraphrased from the source's own Portuguese framing: "pouco se sabe sobre os processos de implementação sistemáticos que levam à verdadeira institucionalização abrangente dos currículos de sustentabilidade"). Curriculum change processes are complex and vary enormously across institutions, which hinders comparison, generalization, and mutual learning among HEIs, and the literature reportedly lacks theorization of how multiple drivers and barriers interact in these processes. The relevance rationale rests on HEIs' role in preparing future decision-makers for a sustainable society transition.

**2. Object of investigation** — The aggregate pattern of curriculum change processes documented across 131 published case studies of HEIs implementing ESD. This is a second-order object: the study does not conduct primary fieldwork on institutions but treats already-published case narratives as its raw data, recoded into standardized variables for statistical pattern detection. The unit of analysis is therefore the individual HEI case as represented in existing published literature, not an institution studied directly by the authors.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — NOT APPLICABLE as a single institutional setting: the empirical material is 131 international case studies of sustainability curriculum implementation in HEIs, drawn from an initial pool of 230 cases identified in peer-reviewed journals, book chapters, and institutional websites. Of these, 133 were selected based on level of detail, and 2 were later discarded for insufficient data, yielding the final sample of 131 cases. A systematic breakdown of the geographic distribution of the full sample is NOT REPORTED, though India and Vietnam are explicitly named as contexts underlying specific cluster subgroups (cluster 4b and cluster 6a respectively), and geographic imbalance is explicitly recorded as an acknowledged limitation.

**4. Study or article type** — Meta-study / systematic case survey (quantitative synthesis of a qualitative case-study literature).

**5. Methodological approach** — A meta-study using the "case survey method," a meta-analytic technique that systematically synthesizes qualitative case studies and converts them into quantitative data through a coding scheme. The coding scheme comprised 111 variables, categorized predominantly as barriers (absence/weakness), medium level (ambiguous impact), drivers (high strength), or absence of information. Five trained coders performed the coding, with inter-rater agreement reported at 94% on a 10% test subsample. Analytic procedures: Ward cluster analysis (executed in R, version 3.6.2) to group HEIs by similarity across coded variables, and indicator species analysis to identify which variables were statistically significant characteristics of each resulting cluster.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — The authors synthesize concepts and theories from several research fields: general curriculum change theories (e.g., Cuban 1999; Fullan 2007), organizational change and innovation (e.g., Kotter 1996; De La Harpe and Thomas 2009; Verhulst and Lambrechts 2015), transformational change (e.g., Eckel and Kezar 2003; Kapitulčinová et al. 2018), transition network methodology (e.g., Pardellas Santiago et al. 2017; Stephens et al. 2008), social dynamics and cultural change focused on learning organizations and adaptive versus progressive change (e.g., Hoover and Harder 2015; Gaugh and Scott 2001; Avery and Nordén 2017), and Meadows' leverage points for system intervention (e.g., Lidgren et al. 2006; Meadows 1999). From this literature, the analysis is structured around five essential interrelated elements: (1) type of ESD implementation — vertical (explicit focus), horizontal (implicit/integrated), or combined (Lambrechts et al. 2013), and disciplinary, interdisciplinary, or transdisciplinary approaches (Barth 2013); (2) depth of curricular change — measured via the depth-and-diffusion scale (Eckel et al. 1999) and the four levels of Sterling and Thomas (2006): denial (no change), bolt-on (education about sustainability added to existing courses), build-in (education for sustainability in new interdisciplinary structures), and redesign (sustainable education integrated into the institution's vision and core); (3) stages and dynamics of curriculum change, from initiation to adoption (Lattuca and Stark 2009) and collaboration phases (Krizek et al. 2012); (4) impetus for change — internal versus external (Lattuca and Stark 2009), normative versus goal-oriented (Fumasoli and Lepori 2011), and intrinsic motivation/reflection (Hoover and Harder 2015); and (5) drivers and barriers — stakeholders, organizational tools (strategic plans, participation), and institutional culture (Velazquez et al. 2005; Barth 2015; Lozano and Barreiro-Gen 2019).

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — The individual HEI case as represented in published literature (131 cases from an initial pool of 230), not primary institutional fieldwork; clusters of cases (six identified patterns) function as the higher-order analytic unit for reporting results.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — NOT APPLICABLE as a single traceable process: the study characterizes, across the 131 aggregated cases, a two-phase process model rather than one institution's redesign. Phase 1 (Initiation): change can begin with any stakeholder or institutional area (teaching, research, management, outreach). Phase 2 (Sustaining): communication, collaboration, and leadership tools are crucial to reach a critical mass of supporters. Five key influences for sustaining broad implementation are explicitly listed: impetus, communication, sense of ownership, collaboration/coalitions, and coordination. Patterns are explicitly recorded as not linear, obligatory life-cycle stages — HEIs can move between patterns, and trajectories depend on institution-specific context.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — Across the aggregated cases: internal stakeholders (faculty, students, "sustainability champions," top management/leadership) and external stakeholders (governmental bodies, international research networks, Regional Centres of Expertise (RCEs), NGOs, and — in the India subgroup — the Supreme Court). Five trained coders served as the study's own methodological actors, performing the case-to-variable coding.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The five structuring analytic elements: type of ESD implementation (vertical/horizontal/combined; disciplinary/interdisciplinary/transdisciplinary); depth of curricular change (denial, bolt-on, build-in, redesign — Sterling and Thomas 2006); stages and dynamics of change (initiation to adoption; collaboration phases); impetus for change (internal vs. external; normative vs. goal-oriented; intrinsic motivation); and drivers/barriers (stakeholders, organizational tools, institutional culture). The 111-variable coding scheme itself functions as the study's operational decision/classification framework (barrier / medium / driver / absence of information).

**11. Contextual conditions** — Geographic imbalance in the underlying case-study literature (most cases concentrated in specific countries/continents, not globally uniform); heterogeneous original methodologies and theoretical perspectives across the 131 source case studies; reliance on secondary, often self-reported published material rather than primary fieldwork. India (governmental and Supreme Court mandates, lack of local interdisciplinary faculty competence) and Vietnam (cultural-understanding barriers, traditional didactics) are named as distinct national contexts underlying specific cluster subgroups.

**12. Barriers** — Absence of a unified vision statement, low sense of ownership, and faculty resistance (in top-down, mandated change); weak internal support/planning despite strong external propulsion (in externally driven initiatives); low cross-faculty collaboration, lack of internal coordination, and highly competitive university environments (in isolated initiatives); severe barriers, lack of support, absence of a strategic plan, weak leadership, lack of resources, and low interdisciplinary faculty competence (in limited institutional change) — with Vietnam-specific cultural-understanding barriers and traditional didactics, and other institutions limited by weak coordination and inadequate communication despite a long implementation history. Missing information in the underlying case texts was statistically treated as irrelevant by the authors, which the authors themselves acknowledge may mask real influences.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — Strong collaboration and support from internal and external stakeholders, formal participation processes, a widely accepted vision statement, distributed leadership, dedicated resources, and faculty training (in collaborative paradigm change). Bottom-up initiation by students and faculty through small projects, expanding via informal communication arenas, with formal top-management support and dedicated resources emerging later (in bottom-up, evolving institutional change). Sustainability champions leading initiatives locally, and support from international research networks or RCEs (in externally driven initiatives). Five key influences identified as sustaining broad implementation: impetus, communication, sense of ownership, collaboration/coalitions, and coordination. Informal collaboration and communication among "champions" can compensate for a lack of formalized leadership support from the top (direct quotation preserved). A unified and participatory vision statement fosters ownership and durability of change (direct quotation preserved).

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — The case survey method itself as an aggregative mechanism (converting qualitative narratives into 111 coded variables via barrier/medium/driver/absence-of-information categories). At the level of the underlying cases: formal participation processes, vision-statement development, distributed leadership structures, dedicated resource allocation, and faculty training (associated with the deepest "collaborative paradigm change" pattern); informal communication arenas expanding into formal top-management support (associated with "bottom-up, evolving institutional change").

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — The central analytic construct of the study: institutionalization is measured via the four-level depth-of-change scale (denial, bolt-on, build-in, redesign), with "redesign" (sustainable education integrated into the institution's vision and core) representing full institutionalization. The two-phase process model (Initiation, Sustaining) and the five key influences (impetus, communication, sense of ownership, collaboration/coalitions, coordination) are presented as the mechanisms governing whether and how broad implementation is sustained. The six identified patterns are explicitly framed as alternative processes rather than obligatory sequential stages toward institutionalization, and HEIs can move between patterns depending on institution-specific context.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - Cluster analysis revealed six distinct patterns of sustainability curriculum implementation processes: (1) Collaborative paradigm change (N=19) — reaches redesign, characterized by strong collaboration and support from internal and external stakeholders, formal participation, a widely accepted vision statement, systemic integration across teaching/research/campus management/outreach, distributed leadership, dedicated resources, and faculty training; (2) Bottom-up, evolving institutional change (N=26) — also reaches redesign (with some build-in tendencies), initiated bottom-up via small projects and informal communication, with formal top-management support emerging later; (3) Top-down, mandated institutional change (N=30) — results mostly in build-in, initiated by leadership due to external incentives, but with little formal participation, absent unified vision, low ownership, and faculty resistance; (4) Externally driven initiatives (N=22) — results in bolt-on or build-in, weak internal support but strong external propulsion, subdivided into (a) international-network/RCE-supported programs led by local champions and (b) Indian cases motivated by governmental/Supreme Court mandates with NGO/RCE material-development support; (5) Isolated initiatives (N=26) — leads to build-in or bolt-on, driven by a few fragmented champions with low cross-faculty collaboration in highly competitive environments; (6) Limited institutional change (N=8) — reaches bolt-on or build-in, involving bottom-up activity facing severe barriers, subdivided into (a) Vietnam cases with cultural/didactic barriers and (b) institutions with long implementation histories limited by weak coordination.
  - A two-phase process model: Phase 1 (Initiation) can begin with any stakeholder or institutional area; Phase 2 (Sustaining) depends on communication, collaboration, and leadership tools to reach a critical mass of supporters. Five key influences for sustaining broad implementation: impetus, communication, sense of ownership, collaboration/coalitions, and coordination.
  - Patterns are not linear, obligatory life-cycle stages — HEIs can move between patterns, and trajectories depend on institution-specific context.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - Institutionalization of sustainability curricula is not achieved through a single linear pathway but through several qualitatively distinct patterns shaped by the interaction of impetus, communication, ownership, collaboration, and coordination.
  - Strong informal collaboration and communication can compensate for a lack of formalized leadership support from the top (direct quotation preserved from the source).
  - Maintaining a unified vision statement and active participation of all stakeholders fosters a sense of ownership in ESD implementation and ensures that it will be long-lasting (direct quotation preserved from the source).
  - The six patterns can be seen as different processes used to achieve institutionalization of sustainability curricula, rather than different stages through which the HEI must transition (direct quotation preserved from the source).
  - An implicit methodological argument underlies the whole study: that the case survey method, combined with cluster analysis, can produce statistically robust, generalizable patterns out of an otherwise heterogeneous body of qualitative case-study literature.

**18. Contribution** — Theoretically, the article integrates curriculum-change and organizational-change literatures with ESD practice, proposing an empirically derived taxonomy of six patterns to help understand the complexity of change. Methodologically, the study is described as pioneering in applying the case survey method combined with cluster analysis in the context of sustainability in higher education, demonstrating how qualitative data from hundreds of dispersed case studies can be transformed into structured, highly reliable quantitative data for statistical analysis. Practically, the study provides a self-reflection scheme ("esquema de autorreflexão") for university managers, allowing them to identify which pattern their institution currently fits and extract practical lessons to evolve toward a full paradigm redesign.

**19. Limitations** — Five limitations are explicitly acknowledged by the authors: (1) geographic imbalance — most studied cases concentrate in specific countries/continents, not representing a globally uniform view; (2) heterogeneous secondary data — the analyzed case studies had widely varying foci, theoretical perspectives, and methodologies in their original publications; (3) success bias — because many case studies are self-reported, there is a strong tendency to publish success stories, omitting structural failures, severe barriers, and internal dynamics; (4) data gaps — missing information was statistically treated as irrelevant by the authors, which may mask real influences, and it was reportedly difficult to map long-term (decades-long) change processes based only on the analyzed texts; (5) small subgroups — clusters 4 and 6 depended heavily on multi-case studies, requiring broader sampling to validate the proposed subdivisions.

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — Vertical/horizontal/combined integration of sustainability into curricula; disciplinary/interdisciplinary/transdisciplinary distinction (Barth 2013) as applied to sustainability curriculum design; four-level depth-of-change scale — denial, bolt-on, build-in, redesign (Sterling and Thomas 2006); internal versus external impetus/drivers of institutional change; the figure of the "sustainability champion" as an informal change agent; sense of ownership generated through participatory, unified vision-building; case survey method as a technique for converting dispersed qualitative case literature into quantitative, comparable data; the distinction between formal and informal communication/leadership channels as functionally substitutable (at least partially) for sustaining change; a typological (non-linear) framing of institutional change patterns, explicitly contrasted with staged/life-cycle models. Additional candidate codes: institutionalization versus superficial/cosmetic ("bolt-on") integration of sustainability; top-down versus bottom-up initiation of curricular change; interaction/interplay of drivers and barriers as a joint explanatory mechanism; external mandates/policy pressure versus internal motivation as change triggers; coordination structures (units, committees, shared responsibilities) as enablers of synergy and non-redundancy; collaboration and coalition-building, internal and external, as compensatory mechanisms in competitive or under-resourced environments; self-reported case-study bias as a methodological concern in sustainability-in-higher-education research; context-specific/geographic subgroup dynamics (e.g., India's government/Supreme Court-mandated cases; Vietnam's cultural/didactic barriers).

**21. Tensions or assumptions** — [DERIVED] The coding scheme reduces qualitative case narratives to 111 categorical variables (driver/barrier/medium/absent). Converting rich institutional narratives into standardized categorical codes risks losing contextual nuance and interpretive detail present in the original case studies, even though the source reports a high inter-rater reliability (94%) for the coding process itself — reliability of coding is not the same as validity of the underlying reduction. Missing information was "statisticamente consideradas irrelevantes" (statistically treated as irrelevant) by the authors, which the authors themselves acknowledge may mask real influences — this is an authors'-acknowledged limitation. [DERIVED] It is simultaneously a methodological assumption worth flagging: treating absence of reported information as equivalent to absence of the underlying factor is a substantive interpretive choice, not a neutral default. [DERIVED] There is a latent tension between the explicit claim that the six patterns are not obligatory linear stages and the substantive framing of "redesign" as the deepest/most complete level and "denial"/"bolt-on" as comparatively superficial. Even if patterns are not temporally sequential, the depth-of-change scale itself is hierarchical, which creates ambiguity about whether the typology is purely descriptive/classificatory or implicitly normative/developmental (i.e., whether redesign is presented as a more desirable outcome that HEIs should aspire to) — this tension is inferred by juxtaposing these two explicit elements of the source, not asserted directly by the source itself. [DERIVED] Because the underlying sample is built substantially from published, often self-reported cases, and the authors acknowledge a "success bias" limitation, claims about what factors "work" to sustain implementation may be inflated relative to failure modes, which are structurally underrepresented in the source material the meta-analysis draws upon — the source does not quantify this bias, but the direction of the inference follows from combining the two explicit elements (self-reported sample composition, acknowledged success bias). The claim that internal stakeholders "são mais poderosos para viabilizar transformações" (are more powerful in enabling transformation) is presented as a finding as part of the "impetus" discussion, but the measurement or coding basis used to establish that comparative strength (i.e., how "power" to enable transformation was assessed or coded) is NOT REPORTED, leaving the evidentiary grounding for this specific claim underspecified.
