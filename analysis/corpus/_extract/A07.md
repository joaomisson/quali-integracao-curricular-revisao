## A07 — An AMEE Guide proposing a 10-window, 9-step curriculum mapping framework

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The subject investigated is curriculum mapping ("mapeamento curricular"), described as a method of spatial representation and management of the different components of an academic curriculum and the relations between them. The problem motivating the work is a communication gap in curriculum design: although medical education has focused intensely on curriculum-development strategies (student-centered, problem-based, and community-based learning), communication about the curriculum itself has been neglected. Teachers and students frequently do not know which topics are covered, where they are taught, what resources are available for each learning outcome, or how assessments connect to the overall program. The relevance of this problem is framed in terms of increasing curricular complexity: the shift toward more centralized curricula (as opposed to isolated department-based curricula) and the coexistence of mandatory and optional components have made teaching programs highly complex. Curriculum mapping is presented as the tool that addresses this complexity, optimizing time for students and teachers and helping ensure that planned learning actually occurs.

**2. Object of investigation** — Curriculum mapping itself as a conceptual and practical tool/method — its structure (10 windows), its terminology (expertise mapping), and its implementation process (9 steps) — rather than an empirical phenomenon studied through observation or measurement of learners or outcomes.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — Professional education, with a predominant focus on medical education. The author draws on case studies and accounts of practical implementation experiences at real medical schools: the University of Birmingham (based on Filemaker Pro databases and modified MeSH terms), Oslo (digital integration of electronic resources), and Tufts (an internet-based curricular database). NOT APPLICABLE: a single defined institutional setting — the article is not anchored in one institution's reform process but draws illustratively on several.

**4. Study or article type** — Conceptual and practical-guidelines article (an educational guide, specifically an AMEE Guide) that does not propose traditional empirical hypotheses. It seeks to answer operational questions posed by multiple actors in the school community, paraphrased from the note as: how do teachers and students know what is covered in the curriculum and where it occurs; how does assessment relate to the teaching program; what resources are needed to sustain each part of the program. The objective is presenting the concept of curriculum mapping in detail, illustrating how it makes the curriculum transparent and integrated, proposing a multidimensional structure based on 10 observation "windows," and describing nine practical steps for its development and successful implementation in an institution.

**5. Methodological approach** — Classified as a theoretical review article, conceptual modeling, and proposition of institutional guidelines (an AMEE Guide) — NOT framed as an empirical study with a conventional data-collection and data-analysis design. The analytical/developmental procedure is systematizing the creation of curriculum maps by unfolding them into 10 interconnected data dimensions and providing a structured plan of 9 consecutive steps to guide developers, from needs assessment to long-term system maintenance. [DERIVED] Because the work is conceptual/guideline in nature rather than empirical, its "results" (the 10 windows, the 9 steps) should be understood as the proposed content of the guide itself rather than findings derived from a data-analysis procedure.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Curriculum Mapping (English, 1978, 1979, 1984): the seminal concept of mapping the curriculum to present the "taught" curriculum as opposed to the "declared" (fictional) or "tested" curriculum. Concept Mapping (Novak & Gowin, 1984): used as a starting point for structuring curricular goals. Matrix representation systems (Du Bois & Kiewra, 1989; Kiewra, 1997): two-dimensional modeling of knowledge (hierarchical, sequential, and matrix structures) to facilitate spatial visualization of interrelations among ideas, contrasted with standard linear texts. Outcome-Based Education and the Three-Circle Model (Harden et al., 1999b): a framework composed of 12 categories of learning outcomes divided among what the physician is able to do, how they approach their practice, and the physician as a professional. Task-Based Learning (Harden et al., 2000): identifying specific professional tasks as the organizing focus of learning. The author also relies conceptually, per the limitations discussion, on Ausubel's framework of advance organizers to support the rationale for mapping, in the absence of direct empirical evidence.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — NOT APPLICABLE in the conventional empirical sense — the "unit" is the conceptual structure of a curriculum map itself (its 10 windows and their interrelations), illustrated rather than tested through three institutional examples (Birmingham, Oslo, Tufts). [DERIVED] These examples function as feasibility cases rather than as a defined analytic sample, since no systematic outcome-measurement methodology is reported for them.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — Not a review of one specific curriculum, but a proposed generic 9-step process for constructing and implementing a curriculum map at any institution: (1) assess needs; (2) define the scope of the task; (3) establish the links; (4) populate the windows; (5) decide the format; (6) consider past, present, and future; (7) define access levels; (8) familiarize staff and students; (9) evaluate and update the map.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — Teachers/faculty, students, curriculum developers/administrators, and institutional leadership, referenced generically as the intended users and audience of the mapping framework rather than as a studied sample. NOT REPORTED: any specific named individuals or a defined participant group, consistent with the article's non-empirical, guideline nature.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The 10 "windows" through which the curriculum can be viewed and organized: (1) expected learning outcomes; (2) curricular content or areas of expertise; (3) student assessment; (4) learning opportunities; (5) learning location; (6) learning resources; (7) timetable; (8) staff; (9) curriculum management; (10) students. A recurring criterion emphasized by the author: the essence of a curriculum map lies not in its nodes but in the links or relations between them.

**11. Contextual conditions** — A broader trend toward centralized (rather than isolated department-based) curricula, combined with coexisting mandatory and optional components, producing high curricular complexity. The three illustrative institutions vary in their technical implementation: Birmingham (Filemaker Pro databases, modified MeSH terms), Oslo (digital integration of electronic resources), Tufts (an internet-based curricular database).

**12. Barriers** — [DERIVED] Not framed by the author as "barriers" in a resistance/politics sense, but as a structural/resource challenge: integrating scientific evidence bases on educational effectiveness (BEME) into each curricular node is described as a herculean task exceeding the capacity of an individual institution. Scarcity of practical implementation experience with curriculum mapping in education more broadly is also acknowledged.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — Conditions the author argues are necessary for successful implementation: unrestricted institutional support (such as workload credit for engaged faculty), clear academic leadership with dedicated time, and a system presenting a simple, user-friendly interface despite its underlying structural complexity. The coordinated exchange of information among faculty about what is being taught is argued to be key to effective integrated teaching, ensuring that activities reflect the institution's core objectives.

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — The 9-step process itself (needs assessment through long-term maintenance, listed in field 8); a specification form ("pro forma") for organizing the distribution of responsibilities in collecting and updating information; integration of the map with a Learning Management System (LMS); "expertise mapping" as a proposed replacement for traditional concept mapping, joining clusters, areas, and units of expertise/learning (described in the note as analogous to "reusable learning objects"); the map's function in identifying unplanned redundancies (revisiting the same area twice) and gaps in learning.

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — Long-term system maintenance is explicitly named as the ninth and final step of the proposed process (evaluate and update the map), implying an ongoing institutional commitment rather than a one-time exercise. NOT REPORTED: any evidence, from the three illustrative institutions or elsewhere, of how long such maps were sustained in practice, who maintained them over time, or what maintenance costs/effort were required.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - The 10 Windows of the curriculum map, allowing the curriculum to be viewed from 10 distinct perspectives: (1) expected learning outcomes; (2) curricular content or areas of expertise; (3) student assessment; (4) learning opportunities; (5) learning location; (6) learning resources; (7) timetable; (8) staff; (9) curriculum management; (10) students.
  - Expertise Mapping: a term the author proposes to replace traditional "concept mapping," adapting it to a competency-based education model to explicitly include skills and attitudes alongside the cognitive domain.
  - A 9-step process for constructing the map: assess needs; define the scope of the task; establish the links; populate the windows; decide the format; consider past, present, and future; define access levels; familiarize staff and students; evaluate and update the map.
  - [DERIVED] Because the article is conceptual/guideline in nature, these "findings" constitute the proposed content of the framework rather than results demonstrated through empirical testing — reinforced by the article's own acknowledgment (field 19) that direct empirical evidence for the framework's effectiveness is scarce.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - The key to effective integrated teaching lies in the coordinated exchange of information among faculty about what is being taught, ensuring that activities reflect the institution's core objectives.
  - The curriculum map is not merely a static schedule or syllabus but the method for operationalizing outcome-based education, functioning as part of a Learning Management System (LMS).
  - Successful implementation requires unrestricted institutional support (e.g., workload credit for engaged faculty), clear academic leadership with dedicated time, and a simple, user-friendly interface despite underlying structural complexity.
  - The essence of a curriculum map is not the nodes but the links or relations between the nodes (quoted directly in the note).

**18. Contribution** — Theoretical: proposition of the concept of "expertise mapping" (joining clusters, areas, and units of expertise/learning, described as analogous to "reusable learning objects"), and expansion of the traditional curriculum map to incorporate management data and individual student portfolios. Practical/methodological: provision of an operational 9-step model, accompanied by a specification form ("pro forma") for organizing the distribution of responsibilities in collecting and updating information; the map is reported to help identify unplanned redundancies and gaps in learning.

**19. Limitations** — The author acknowledges that practical experience with curriculum mapping in education is still limited. From a scientific standpoint, there is little direct empirical evidence demonstrating conclusively the effectiveness of maps in directly improving student learning, with the argument relying primarily on the conceptual framework of Ausubel's advance organizers rather than direct empirical validation. A further acknowledged limitation: integrating scientific evidence bases on educational effectiveness (BEME) into each curricular node is described as a herculean task that exceeds the capacity of an individual institution. [DERIVED] The explicit acknowledgment of scarce direct empirical evidence, combined with the prescriptive and confident tone of the 9-step guide and the "glue" metaphor, suggests a gap between the conceptual/rhetorical strength of the argument and its empirical grounding.

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — Curriculum transparency/communication gap; horizontal and vertical integration of teaching; competency- and outcome-based education; digital/internet-based educational infrastructure; redundancy and gap identification in curricula; institutional support conditions for pedagogical tools; evidence-based curriculum design (aspirational, per BEME reference); faculty coordination and information exchange; operationalization of abstract educational frameworks into practical tools; curriculum mapping as a distinct construct from curriculum design/development; the taught/declared(fictional)/tested curriculum distinction (English, 1978); the nodes-versus-links distinction as a criterion for what constitutes a genuine "map" rather than a static database; advance organizers (Ausubel) as conceptual justification for curricular visualization tools.

**21. [DERIVED] This is a tension between the confident, prescriptive framing of the tool (the "glue" metaphor, the definitive 9-step methodology) and the admittedly weak empirical base supporting its efficacy. [DERIVED] The reliance on Ausubel's advance organizer theory as the primary conceptual justification, rather than direct empirical testing of curriculum mapping itself, suggests the argument for efficacy is analogical/theoretical rather than demonstrated. [DERIVED] The case examples from Birmingham, Oslo, and Tufts are described as implementation experiences/case studies rather than as controlled or comparative evaluations; no systematic learning-outcome measurement is indicated for these cases, so they appear to function as illustrations of feasibility rather than as evidence of effectiveness. The claim that curriculum mapping is "the" method to operationalize outcome-based education presents mapping as uniquely suited to this role, without indication that alternative operationalization methods were considered or compared.
