## A01 — Two-year non-linear participative curricular review in a Merchandising/Fashion Design department

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study addresses curricular review and reform in higher education applied to a Merchandising and Fashion Design (MFD) program. The motivating problem operates at two levels. Practical/managerial: institutions face continuous pressure to keep curricula current and relevant, driven by accreditation requirements and rapid market change. Conceptual/methodological: curricular review is commonly described in the literature as exhausting, complex, and emotionally draining for faculty, generating resistance and interpersonal conflict rooted in differing views and power dynamics; the authors identify a gap in that most proposed curricular reform models are strictly linear, which ignores that institutional practice is messy, dynamic, and iterative.

**2. Object of investigation** — The curricular review process itself — its design, sequencing, and stakeholder dynamics — rather than a specific measurable educational outcome such as student learning gains or employment rates. Secondary objects captured within this process: stakeholder perceptions (students, alumni) gathered via survey; faculty self-perceptions and interpersonal dynamics captured through self-assessment questionnaires and reflective meetings; and comparative curricular structures at peer institutions used as an external benchmark.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — Single institutional context: the Merchandising and Fashion Design (MFD) department of a public, metropolitan, medium-to-large university in the Midwestern United States, with 24,390 students enrolled as of fall 2018. The reform spans two years and involves the department's full faculty body, plus external stakeholder groups (recent graduates/seniors and alumni) and comparison data from peer, competitive, and aspirant institutions. Single-case, single-discipline, single-institution.

**4. Study or article type** — Descriptive case study using a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative survey data with qualitative analysis of perceptions and discussions. No formal, statistically testable hypotheses are presented; the article instead reports practical guiding questions posed to faculty during meetings. [DERIVED] The design is practitioner-driven and largely qualitative/process-descriptive, with a modest quantitative component (descriptive statistics on a 50-respondent survey) embedded within it — reasoning: no inferential statistical testing, control condition, or independent evaluator external to the department is described.

**5. Methodological approach** — Participants and data: the entire departmental faculty participated actively in plenary meetings and a self-assessment process; a structured opinion survey was completed by 50 respondents (18 graduating seniors and 32 alumni), a 49% response rate out of 103 invited; curricular data from peer institutions were collected via internet research. Data collection occurred across two years. Year 1 (industry focus and internal diagnosis): documentary research on external curricula found online; faculty travel to interview alumni in person across various U.S. cities about technological trends and emerging careers; mixed questionnaires administered to students and alumni/employers; an internal Continuous Improvement Process (CIP) self-assessment questionnaire completed individually by each faculty member regarding their own course syllabi. Year 2 (benchmarking and peer review): systematic collection of comparison data (program names, required credit hours, course syllabi) from peer institutions; intensive debate meetings structured around open-ended question scripts addressing individual courses and the curriculum as a whole. Analytical procedures: direct comparative (benchmarking) analysis between the department's curricular structure and reference programs; thematic content analysis and simple descriptive statistics applied to student/alumni survey data; structured reflective sessions governed by pre-established conduct norms (mutual listening, respect for ideas, no interruptions) used to manage disagreement and build consensus.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Several distinct conceptual anchors rather than a single unified framework: the tripartite model of Garner and Buckley (1988) as the guiding structure, holding that a strong curriculum must focus on three core constituencies — educators who deliver it, students who experience it, and employers who hire graduates; continuous environmental scanning, attributed to Laughlin and Kean (1995), to identify technological, demographic, and market trends in fashion; a Continuous Improvement Process (CIP) inspired by Deming's (2000) continuous-quality model; guidelines and competencies from the International Textile and Apparel Association (ITAA) as general goal parameters; a body of theory on behavioral barriers to change, addressing resistance among senior faculty (comfort with prepared courses) and junior faculty (time constraints tied to promotion and tenure), and departmental political/power dynamics; peer review concepts attributed to Osborne and Purkey (1995), on the importance of mutual evaluation among faculty. [DERIVED] This is an eclectic combination of applied management/quality concepts and organizational-behavior concepts, loosely organized around the Garner and Buckley model rather than integrated into a single explanatory theory — reasoning: no integrating theoretical statement is described; the foundations function as practical scaffolding.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — The two-year deductive, participative, iterative process undertaken by one department's faculty (single case, single discipline, single institution). Secondary units: stakeholder perceptions via the 50-respondent survey; faculty self-assessment via CIP questionnaires per faculty member per course syllabus; comparative curricular structures at peer institutions.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — A two-year, systematic, non-linear, deductive, participative, and iterative curricular review process, structured as two complementary phases (Year 1: industry focus/internal diagnosis; Year 2: benchmarking/peer review) and guided by practical questions posed to faculty during meetings, paraphrased as: whether the program offered the most up-to-date curriculum and served students best; what differentiated the program from others; whether the most current industry techniques were taught; how up-to-date information was delivered in the classroom; and how individual faculty kept their own materials current. Concrete process outputs: addition of a logistics/supply-chain course to the core curriculum (direct result of benchmarking); redesign of the capstone course into an experiential real-market simulation called "Senior Market," in which design students develop full collections evaluated by a three-person industry-expert panel while merchandising students handle market positioning and presentation to an external public audience; identification of an imminent need for new industrial machinery and software.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — Full departmental faculty (plenary meetings, self-assessment); 50 survey respondents (18 graduating seniors, 32 alumni) of 103 invited (49% response rate); peer, competitive, and aspirant institutions (compared via internet research on curricula); a three-person panel of industry experts evaluating "Senior Market" student collections; an external public audience for merchandising presentations; academic managers/facilitators who must navigate faculty resistance.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The Garner and Buckley (1988) tripartite model (educators, students, employers) as the organizing structure for weighing input; the five practical guiding questions (paraphrased in field 8) used in faculty meetings; alternating between a macro perspective (the program as a whole) and a micro perspective (detailed analysis of each individual course), presented by the authors as what ensures a coherent and genuinely relevant curriculum.

**11. Contextual conditions** — Public, metropolitan, medium-to-large university in the Midwestern United States; 24,390 students enrolled as of fall 2018; single MFD department; accreditation requirements and rapid market/technological change in the fashion field as external pressures driving the review.

**12. Barriers** — Resistance to change among senior faculty (comfort with prepared courses) and junior faculty (time constraints tied to promotion and tenure processes); departmental political/power dynamics; faculty fatigue in the face of an overwhelming volume of data; severe budgetary and internal resource constraints; difficult debates marked by resistance to change and departmental power disputes.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — Structured conduct norms (mutual listening, respect for ideas, no interruptions) governing reflective sessions to manage disagreement and build consensus; the participative, multi-stakeholder nature of the process; peer review (Osborne & Purkey, 1995) as a consensus-building mechanism; benchmarking against peer/competitive/aspirant institutions as an external legitimation device; the internal CIP self-assessment; alternating macro/micro analysis as a structuring device.

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — Year 1 mechanisms: documentary research on external curricula online; faculty travel to interview alumni in person across U.S. cities on technological trends and emerging careers; mixed questionnaires to students and alumni/employers; individual CIP self-assessment questionnaires per faculty member on course syllabi. Year 2 mechanisms: systematic peer-institution data collection (program names, required credit hours, course syllabi); intensive debate meetings structured around open-ended question scripts on individual courses and the curriculum as a whole. Resulting concrete changes: logistics/supply-chain course integrated into the core curriculum; capstone course redesigned into "Senior Market."

The note records only the authors' suggested future work: investigating the long-term effectiveness of institutionalizing the curricular changes adopted, and exploring post-reform assessment of learning — [DERIVED] this suggests that institutionalization/sustainability had not yet been evaluated within the reported study, since it is framed as a direction for future research rather than a finding.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - The survey of students and alumni indicated strong demand for greater incorporation of technology into courses, addition of logistics and supply-chain content, and implementation of a textiles laboratory.
  - Benchmarking confirmed that the absence of a logistics course was a real curricular gap relative to competing programs; as a direct result, a logistics/supply-chain course was integrated into the program's core curriculum.
  - The capstone course was redesigned into "Senior Market," an experiential real-market simulation in which design students develop full collections evaluated by a three-person industry-expert panel, while merchandising students handle market positioning and presentation to an external public audience.
  - The process identified an imminent need to acquire new industrial machinery and software to keep courses aligned with current market technologies.
  - Despite difficult debates marked by resistance to change and departmental power disputes, adherence to listening norms and the participative nature of the process are reported to have re-established more honest relationships and increased mutual understanding among faculty from different specialties (merchandising and design) — described as an unexpected behavioral benefit.
  - The technology-integration and textiles-laboratory demands identified by students and alumni are not reported as having been acted upon or resolved; only the logistics course addition and the capstone redesign are explicitly described as concrete curricular outcomes.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - Successful curricular reform must abandon the rigidity of purely linear models and instead embrace an iterative, cyclic, and adaptive process that mirrors how reform actually unfolds inside institutions.
  - Facilitators and academic managers must understand the intrinsic motivations and behavioral barriers that generate resistance among junior and senior faculty in order to navigate difficult conversations constructively.
  - Alternating between a macro perspective (the program as a whole) and a micro perspective (detailed analysis of each individual course) is what ensures faculty build a coherent and genuinely relevant curriculum.
  - Student success and employability are treated as the ultimate validation metric for the reform project — though such outcome data are not reported as having been collected within this study.

**18. Contribution** — Theoretical/methodological: presentation of a non-linear framework, described as innovative and replicable, built on two complementary phases and integrating the perspectives of multiple stakeholder groups (faculty, alumni, market, academia). Practical/organizational: evidence that adopting transparent behavioral norms and peer review reduces faculty defensiveness, resulting in re-established, healthier professional relationships aligned around departmental goals.

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — Tripartite stakeholder framing of curriculum design (educators, students, employers); environmental scanning; Continuous Improvement Process (CIP) applied to academic curricula; behavioral-barriers framing of resistance to change, differentiated by career stage; peer review as consensus-building/mutual-evaluation mechanism; benchmarking against peer/competitive/aspirant institutions as gap-identification method; linear vs. iterative/cyclic/messy reform-process conceptualizations; alternating macro/micro levels of analysis; curricular reform as ongoing process rather than discrete event; participatory governance and multi-stakeholder consultation; behavioral/relational outcomes of change processes (distinct from structural/curricular outcomes); structured conduct norms as conflict-management mechanism; accreditation and market-relevance pressure as external drivers; case study as evidentiary basis for generalizable process frameworks.

**21. Tensions or assumptions** — Labeling tension: the process is described as "deductive" while also heavily participative and iterative, incorporating extensive bottom-up stakeholder input (student/alumni surveys, faculty self-assessment, open-ended debate); a deductive process would typically proceed from established theoretical premises (e.g., the Garner and Buckley tripartite model) applied downward, whereas the process as described appears to combine top-down structuring with substantial inductive, ground-level data gathering and negotiation — whether "deductive" accurately characterizes the process, or it is better understood as a hybrid, is not resolved in the note. Tension between the claim to offer a "replicable" framework and its evidentiary basis: a single department, single institution, single two-year cycle, described through a descriptive case study without any reported comparison case or independent replication; the replicability claim rests on the authors' own characterization rather than demonstrated cross-context application. The positive relational/behavioral findings are reported without a described independent measurement instrument or external evaluator; given that the faculty who underwent the process include or overlap with those reporting on its results, this raises an unresolved question about whether these findings reflect structured evaluation versus a retrospective, participant-generated account. The study designates student success and employability as the "final validation metric," yet no such outcome data are reported as collected or analyzed, creating a gap between the articulated evaluative standard and the evidence actually produced. The 49% response rate (50 of 103 invited) is treated in the findings as indicative of stakeholder demand, without any reported assessment of whether the responding subgroup differed systematically from non-respondents — an assumption worth flagging when weighing how representative the identified "gaps" are of the broader population.
