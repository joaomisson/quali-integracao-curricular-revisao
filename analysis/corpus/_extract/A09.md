## A09 — Scoping review synthesizing curricular quality standards and hidden "inhibitors" into a viability framework

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study addresses "curriculum viability," defined as the general state of health, well-being, and operational sustainability of a curriculum. Traditional curriculum evaluations restrict themselves to measuring quality by comparing a curriculum against established quality standards, an approach the study characterizes as documental/audit-based and functioning like a checklist (a "reviewer posture"). The problem identified is that this kind of formal verification does not identify the inhibitors that directly affect the practical attainment of such standards, so a curriculum can be formally approved in external audits for meeting basic standards while simultaneously harboring dysfunctions and chronic problems that go entirely unnoticed. The stated rationale for relevance is that curriculum is a living, organic, and highly dynamic entity that, without structured and constant review and renewal, tends to deteriorate, becoming obsolete or riddled with operational problems; exposing hidden barriers to curricular success is presented as essential to guide effective pedagogical reforms and ensure the sustainability of educational improvements.

**2. Object of investigation** — The body of published academic literature reporting curricular quality standards and inhibiting factors — i.e., a scoping review of secondary literature rather than a primary empirical study of a specific curriculum or institution. The unit of analysis for the review itself is the set of 13 articles that survived screening and quality appraisal.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — Situated in medical/health professions education, evidenced by references to accreditation bodies cited in the theoretical foundation (WFME, LCME, GMC) and by inclusion criteria restricting to educational research literature in English from 1992–2017. No particular country, institution, or specific level of medical education (undergraduate vs. postgraduate) is specified for the underlying primary studies; the review scopes the broader academic literature rather than a single institutional setting.

**4. Study or article type** — Scoping review of secondary literature.

**5. Methodological approach** — Conducted under the Arksey and O'Malley methodological framework. Primary systematic search in Web of Science (covering 8 integrated sub-databases, including Web of Science Core Collection, MEDLINE, and SciELO Citation Index), cross-checked with Google Scholar to capture grey literature and ensure reproducibility; manual search, snowballing, and direct consultation with experts were also used. Search terms combined via Boolean OR/AND operators (e.g., curriculum, viability, quality, indicators, problems, diseases, inhibitors). Inclusion criteria: original research articles and systematic reviews in the field of educational research, written in English, published 1992–2017. Exclusion criteria: grey literature outside WOS peer-review standards, abstracts, isolated citations, editorials, conference reports, letters to the editor, book chapters, and purely conceptual/position essays. Screening: initial search yielded 1233 studies; after initial filtering and duplicate removal, 36 full-text articles were reviewed by two independent reviewers. Quality appraisal used the validated Qualsyst checklist, with a minimum cutoff of 50% methodological quality adherence; inter-rater agreement reached Cohen's Kappa of 0.682 (described as good correlation). Final sample: 13 articles after qualitative exclusions. Two authors performed qualitative thematic analysis of the 13 articles, using structured open, axial, and selective coding to consolidate themes.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Classical curriculum theory anchored in Bosco's (1971) four pillars: objectives, content, teaching methods, and evaluation. Learning theories/paradigms shaping curriculum: behaviorism, cognitivism, constructivism. Curriculum organization models: Tyler's (1949) prescriptive/structured, behaviorist-based model; Hilda Taba's (1962) interactive model emphasizing mutual interaction of components; Walker's (1971) descriptive/naturalistic model (platform of beliefs, deliberation, design). Modern teaching models/guidelines: Harden's (1986) ten-question model; Mager's instructional objectives; Gagne's (1965) learning principles and the ADDIE instructional design model; van Merrienboer's 4C/ID model for professional competency development via whole tasks. Abrahamson's (1978) "curriculum diseases" metaphor, using pathological/medical terminology: curriculo-sclerosis (extreme departmentalization and disputes over course-hour allocation), curriculum carcinoma (disproportionate overgrowth of one discipline over others), curriculo-arthritis (absence of communication forums among faculty), and iatrogenic curriculitis (abrupt, excessive modifications made in reaction to external pressures). Global accreditation/standards systems: WFME global standards, LCME accreditation guidelines, GMC clinical training norms.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — The 13 full-text articles that survived screening (from an initial 1233 identified and 36 full-text reviewed) and passed the Qualsyst quality-appraisal threshold; the review synthesizes findings across this article set rather than analyzing a single curriculum, program, or institution.

**10. **11. No single institutional or national setting; the "context" is the accumulated academic literature meeting these inclusion criteria.

**12. Barriers** — The framework's 19 inhibitors, distributed across the 8 domains (specific counts per domain not detailed beyond the total). Named examples include, under Leadership: lack of clearly assigned roles and leaders acting as monopolistic "gatekeepers" of information. Under Students: student resistance to new curricula, described as not mechanically affecting documental/technical quality but constituting a long-term impediment that sabotages reforms, blocks engagement, and compromises multiple sectors simultaneously (e.g., assessment and school climate). **15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — Sustainability is the study's central organizing construct via "curriculum viability" itself: curriculum is framed as a living, organic entity that, absent structured and constant review and renewal, deteriorates or becomes obsolete. Structured inhibitor identification is framed as a "prophylactic" procedure allowing decision-makers to address hidden problems before they culminate in complete collapse of the educational program, positioning ongoing diagnostic monitoring (rather than a one-time compliance audit) as the condition for sustained curricular health.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - Scarcity of joint treatment of inhibitors: all 13 sampled articles addressed and reported quality standards, but only 2 studies (Bendermacher et al., 2017 and Olson et al., 2013) discussed quality standards and inhibiting factors together.
  - Thematic/structural analysis identified eight major areas of curricular impact, systematized into a "Framework for Assessing Curriculum Viability" compiling 37 quality standards and 19 inhibitors across these areas: (1) Educational Strategy and Content, (2) Students, (3) Faculty, (4) Assessment, (5) Educational and Work Environment, (6) Curricular Communication, (7) Technology, (8) Leadership.
  - For each area specific example standards and inhibitors are given (e.g., under Leadership, inhibitors include lack of clearly assigned roles and leaders acting as monopolistic "gatekeepers" of information).

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - A conceptual distinction is drawn between "reviewers" and "interpreters" in program evaluation: the traditional audit-based approach ("reviewers") is limited to a judgment of whether a given standard was met, whereas the viability-oriented approach ("interpreters") operates analogously to a clinical diagnosis performed by a doctor, investigating deep structural causes and dynamic variables leading to pedagogical failure or success.
  - The action of certain inhibitors is not necessarily obvious or linear — e.g., "student resistance to new curricula" does not mechanically affect the documental/technical quality of the curriculum, but constitutes a long-term impediment that sabotages reforms, blocks engagement, and compromises multiple sectors simultaneously (such as assessment and school climate).
  - Structured inhibitor identification is framed as a "prophylactic" procedure, allowing decision-makers to address hidden problems before they culminate in a complete collapse of the educational program.

**18. Contribution** — Three contributions are attributed to the authors: (1) formulation of a new concept — introducing and consolidating "curriculum viability" in the pedagogical literature as an indispensable measure of ongoing well-being that goes beyond conventional bureaucratic quality; (2) development of the Viability Framework — an applicable instrument uniting WFME-type standards and inhibiting factors under 8 integrated dimensions, enabling complex institutional diagnostics; (3) integration with curriculum mapping — the framework is presented as advanced analytical support that works in symbiosis with traditional curriculum mapping, helping managers understand not only where operational discrepancies exist between planned, delivered, and learned curriculum, but the real causes behind those gaps.

**19. Limitations** — Two limitations are explicitly attributed to the authors: (1) the scarcity of literature robustly and empirically addressing inhibiting factors jointly with standards prevented the creation of systemic cause-and-effect connections for each individual component of the proposed framework; (2) restricting the scoping review search exclusively to English-language studies may have excluded crucial empirical findings produced in other linguistic and geographic contexts. A future-research recommendation is also recorded: developing and validating new psychometric tools, questionnaires, and quantitative inventories to measure not only the degree of technical-standard compliance but also the degree of curriculum exposure to hidden inhibiting factors, generating preventive institutional diagnostics.

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — The distinction between "quality standards" (compliance-oriented, checklist-based) and "inhibitors" (dynamic, often invisible factors impeding standard attainment); "curriculum viability" as a composite construct combining standard attainment and inhibitor exposure, distinct from "curriculum quality" alone; the "reviewer vs. interpreter" evaluative posture distinction (compliance auditing vs. diagnostic interpretation); the organic/pathological metaphor of curriculum as a living entity subject to "diseases" (Abrahamson's terminology); the notion that formal/documental compliance can mask underlying dysfunction; the idea of non-linear, cross-sectoral inhibitor effects (a single inhibitor affecting multiple curricular domains simultaneously); the eight-domain structuring of curricular impact areas as a possible organizing taxonomy; the distinction between planned, delivered, and learned curriculum, referenced in connection with curriculum mapping; quality-standards-vs-inhibitors dichotomy; curriculum-as-living-entity metaphor / curriculum pathology; hidden/latent institutional dysfunction beneath formal compliance; preventive/prophylactic institutional diagnostics; leadership as gatekeeping/centralizing barrier; faculty exclusion from decision-making as structural inhibitor; student resistance to curricular innovation as cross-sectoral barrier; scoping review methodology in medical education research; framework integration of standards and inhibitors (37 standards / 19 inhibitors, 8 domains); scarcity of empirical literature jointly addressing standards and inhibitors.

**21. Tensions or assumptions** — Only 13 articles survived quality appraisal from an initial 1233 identified, and only 2 of those 13 discussed inhibitors jointly with standards. [DERIVED] This narrow empirical base is in tension with the 37-standard/19-inhibitor framework across 8 domains that the authors nonetheless derive from it; the authors themselves acknowledge that the scarcity of joint literature prevented establishing systemic cause-and-effect connections, and juxtaposing this acknowledged evidentiary thinness against the scale of the resulting framework supports an inference of a scope-versus-evidence tension, though the authors' own stated limitation is phrased specifically about the absence of causal connections rather than about the framework's overall scope. The framework's inhibitor list appears to be substantially synthesized/interpreted by the reviewing authors' thematic analysis (open/axial/selective coding) rather than drawn from a large body of directly comparable primary studies reporting inhibitors; it is not clarified how many of the 13 articles actually contributed to the 19 inhibitors versus the 37 standards, beyond the "2 studies discussed both" finding, leaving ambiguous how inhibitors were extracted from articles that did not explicitly address inhibitors. The English-language-only restriction, acknowledged by the authors, creates a linguistic/geographic bias that sits in tension with the framework's implicit claim to generalizability across curricular contexts (WFME, LCME, GMC are bodies with different geographic jurisdictions, and representativeness across these systems is not clarified). The "reviewer vs. interpreter" clinical-diagnosis analogy is a rhetorically strong framing ("interpreters are like doctors of the curriculum"), but there is no indication that the study empirically tested whether an "interpreter" approach produces better outcomes than a "reviewer" approach — this is a conceptual/normative argument rather than an empirically demonstrated one. The claim that formal standard compliance can coexist with severe hidden dysfunction is presented as a foundational premise rather than as a finding independently verified within this scoping review's own primary data (the review synthesizes secondary literature); no original empirical evidence generated by these authors demonstrating this coexistence is recorded, only the argument built from cited literature.
