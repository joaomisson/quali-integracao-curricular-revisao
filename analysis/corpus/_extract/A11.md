## A11 — Institutional curricular-coherence intervention combining structural complexity analysis and faculty-reaction survey at UNLV

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study addresses curricular reform and curricular coherence in undergraduate programs within U.S. higher education. The problem is framed as "curricular drift": over four decades, degree programs originally designed as rational, interdependent sequences of learning experiences have expanded, contracted, and morphed into aggregates of isolated courses whose only unifying feature is reaching the 120-credit threshold or satisfying disciplinary accreditation requirements. This produces pedagogical incoherence, hinders development of broad competencies demanded by the labor market (e.g., critical thinking, communication), and generates frustration among both students (who cannot perceive the coherence of their academic trajectory) and faculty (who feel a loss of control over their curricula). The study's relevance is framed around a "moral imperative" facing institutions given changes in the student population served (including minority and first-generation students), and the need to ensure efficient academic pathways enabling timely graduation, minimal debt, and preparation for 21st-century demands.

**2. Object of investigation** — [DERIVED] A curricular reform intervention — its design, implementation process, and consequences — across nine undergraduate degree programs at a single university (UNLV), examined along two axes: (a) measurable structural complexity of course sequences (via the Curricular Analytics quantitative model) and (b) faculty perceptions, confidence, and concerns regarding the reform process (via survey instrument). This dual framing synthesizes elements described piecemeal across the objective, methodology, and results content of the source, making explicit that the study's object is simultaneously a structural/curricular artifact and a human/institutional process; it is not stated as a single unified sentence in the source itself.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — University of Nevada, Las Vegas (UNLV), designated by the U.S. Department of Education as a minority-serving and Hispanic-serving institution, with more than 31,000 enrolled students. The intervention tracked nine undergraduate programs across seven academic departments in five different colleges, between fall 2016 and spring 2019. For the faculty-reaction component, data were collected from 18 faculty members across six departments who responded to post-reform questionnaires.

**4. Study or article type** — Institutional curricular-intervention case study combining qualitative and quantitative methods (empirical article).

**5. Methodological approach** — Data collection procedures: (1) A "Curricular Coherence Framework" — a non-prescriptive reflective guide organized into three axes: Design (e.g., reinforcement of key competencies throughout the program), Implementation (e.g., pass rates, excessive need for waivers), and Evaluation (e.g., pedagogical use of institutional assessment data). (2) Support and compensation — customized data (failure rates, flow reports), administrative assistance to speed proposal approval, and $1,500 financial compensation for departmental activities. (3) A reaction survey (adapted Stages of Concern Questionnaire) using a 5-point Likert scale measuring perceived usefulness, personal confidence, and level of concern. (4) A custom evaluation rubric — a 3-point qualitative classification measuring progress of structured changes across eight goal categories. (5) Complexity analysis — four-year degree plan data for each program loaded into the University of New Mexico's Curricular Analytics platform to compute structural complexity scores pre- and post-reform. Sampling procedures for the 18-faculty survey (e.g., response rate, invitation method) and qualitative coding procedures, if any, are NOT REPORTED.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Models of change in higher education (Trowler et al., 2003): five approaches to organizational change differentiated by drivers and power flow — technical-rational, resource allocation, diffusionist, Kai Zen/bricolage, and complexity. Communities of Practice (Wenger, 1998 — rendered "Wegner" in the source, reproduced as it appears there, which may reflect a transcription artifact in the underlying literature note rather than the original article): used to frame the academic department as the primary locus where teaching practices, assessment criteria, and a sense of intellectual ownership over the curriculum develop. Curricular Tensions (Kandiko & Blackmore, 2010): focused on intrinsic dilemmas of curriculum redesign, specifically breadth versus depth and structure versus choice. Historical Curricular Processes (Bernstein, 1996): addresses curricular evolution through processes of introjection (internal disciplinary demands) and projection (external pressures and regulations). Curricular Complexity (Heileman et al., 2017): a mathematical model quantifying structural complexity of course sequences via two per-course factors — blocking factor (number of subsequent courses whose access depends on that course) and delay factor (length of the longest prerequisite chain the course belongs to).

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — Nine undergraduate degree programs across seven academic departments in five colleges at a single university (UNLV); within this, a sub-sample of 18 faculty members across six departments for the reaction-survey component.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — A multi-year (fall 2016–spring 2019) institutional curricular-coherence intervention led by a multidisciplinary team of faculty and administrators, applying the three-axis Curricular Coherence Framework (Design, Implementation, Evaluation) as a non-prescriptive reflective guide to nine undergraduate programs. Departments received customized diagnostic data (failure rates, flow reports), administrative assistance to speed proposal approval through governance, and $1,500 financial compensation for departmental participation. Progress on structured changes was tracked via a custom 3-point evaluation rubric across eight goal categories, and structural outcomes were quantified pre- and post-reform using the Curricular Analytics platform's complexity scoring.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — A multidisciplinary project team of faculty and administrators (based in the provost's office, described in the source as technical-rational in leadership structure but including faculty from academic departments); 18 faculty respondents across six departments providing reaction data; nine program-level departments as the units undergoing reform; students referenced indirectly as the population affected by pass rates, waivers, and structural complexity, though no direct student-level data collection is reported.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The three axes of the Curricular Coherence Framework: Design, Implementation, Evaluation. The eight goal categories in the custom evaluation rubric (specific category names not detailed in the source beyond the rubric's existence). Survey-measured dimensions: perceived usefulness, personal confidence, level of concern (5-point Likert, adapted Stages of Concern Questionnaire). Complexity-scoring dimensions per the Heileman et al. (2017) model: blocking factor and delay factor per course.

**11. Contextual conditions** — Federally designated minority-serving and Hispanic-serving institution status; large enrollment (31,000+ students); multi-year intervention window (fall 2016–spring 2019); an equity- and access-oriented "moral imperative" rationale tied to the changing student population served (including minority and first-generation students) and to timely, low-debt graduation pathways.

**12. Barriers** — The most frequently cited obstacles to faculty were difficulty reaching internal departmental consensus and bureaucratic barriers in the university/state approval system. No department attempted to introduce "signature work" opportunities or methods to make learning outcomes transparent to students, in contrast to near-universal uptake of low-effort structural changes — deep pedagogical changes are argued to require intensive negotiation of time and effort plus sustained external resources and instructional designers, unlike the "low hanging fruit" of removing bureaucratic obstacles. Participants tended to select only low-effort, low-time-impact curricular changes (an authors'-acknowledged limitation). A possible self-selection bias affected the 18-respondent faculty survey: the source records the authors' hedge that more engaged and confident faculty were "provavelmente" (probably) more likely to complete the instrument — presented explicitly as a probabilistic inference rather than a confirmed mechanism, with no comparative respondent/non-respondent data reported to verify it.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — Customized diagnostic data (failure rates, flow reports) provided to departments; administrative assistance to speed proposal approval through governance; $1,500 financial compensation for departmental activities; a change-management model based on listening and decentralized empowerment of departments, reported to have produced favorable and durable experiences for faculty; a hybrid governance structure combining technical-rational, centrally-led origin (situated within the provost's office) with participatory elements (faculty inclusion on the project team, explicit sensitivity to negative perceptions of top-down mandates).

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — The three-axis Curricular Coherence Framework (Design, Implementation, Evaluation) as a non-prescriptive reflective guide; the adapted Stages of Concern Questionnaire (5-point Likert scale) for faculty reaction data; a custom 3-point qualitative evaluation rubric across eight goal categories tracking progress of structured changes; the Curricular Analytics platform (University of New Mexico) computing structural complexity scores from four-year degree-plan data pre- and post-reform, using the blocking-factor/delay-factor algorithm.

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — NOT REPORTED as an explicit institutionalization mechanism beyond the intervention period itself; the source records only forward-looking suggestions rather than confirmed institutionalized practices: (1) creating a tiered financial-incentive structure (larger incentives for programs adopting deep revisions of learning outcomes or signature work); (2) adopting a multi-stage intervention, where an initial phase addresses course-sequencing (simpler) and subsequent phases progressively advance toward complex discussions of systemic departmental assessment and consolidation of professional competencies; (3) conducting longitudinal analyses to track the real empirical impacts of these curricular changes on student success and indicators such as retention, time-to-degree, and reduction of exceptional course waivers. The source explicitly acknowledges that long-term outcome data (retention, time-to-degree, waiver rates) are absent and left to future longitudinal work.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - Faculty reaction: high perceived usefulness (mean 4.23 ± 0.75), moderate confidence in implementation (3.5 ± 0.55), and very low levels of concern (1.27 ± 0.46). The most frequently cited obstacles were difficulty reaching internal departmental consensus and bureaucratic barriers in the university/state approval system.
  - Curricular actions taken: departments focused almost unanimously on eliminating bureaucratic structural barriers (e.g., unnecessary or obsolete prerequisites) — attempted by 8 programs, completed by 100% of them (maximum progress score of 3.0). Removal of "boutique" courses (not directly serving overall program goals) also succeeded highly (3 programs attempted, 100% completed). By contrast, no department attempted to introduce "signature work" opportunities or methods to make learning outcomes transparent to students.
  - Complexity impact: no average reduction in structural complexity scores was observed pre- versus post-revision, and total required credits remained unchanged. Four programs reduced their complexity scores, four increased, and one stayed the same. In STEM programs, average complexity rose from 316 to 322, driven by the voluntary decision to split unified 4-credit courses (e.g., General Chemistry) into separate 3-credit theory and 1-credit corequisite lab components — a change that increases complexity under the mathematical algorithm (by creating new requirement links) but is described as practically beneficial because it prevents a student who fails only one part from having to retake the entire combined course.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - Eliminating peripheral structural obstacles (prerequisites) constitutes "low hanging fruit" — easy for departments to accept and execute — whereas deep pedagogical changes (competency transparency, signature work) require intensive negotiation of time and effort plus sustained external resources and instructional designers.
  - Blindly reducing complexity should not be established as a final goal of educational reform; an excessively unstructured curriculum shifts the entire burden of coherence onto the student, potentially resulting in fragmented course choices or in hidden, systematically uncommunicated requirements.
  - Metric mapping tools like Curricular Analytics proved useful for locating technical publication errors in degree plans (e.g., courses recommended for the same semester that had a prerequisite relationship between them) and for revealing that formal catalog requirements were routinely waived by advisors and faculty in actual practice.
  - A change-management model based on listening and decentralized empowerment of departments produced favorable and durable experiences for faculty.

**18. Contribution** — Practical contribution: a functional consultative approach that reduces departmental resistance to central-administration interference in faculty curricular-design autonomy. Theoretical/methodological contribution: empirical demonstration that managing complex structural change in higher education is enhanced by hybridizing technical-rational, diffusionist, financial-incentive, and complexity-based approaches; the article also contributes to demystifying the "low complexity" metric as an absolute synonym for learning quality.

**19. Limitations** — The faculty-reaction survey had a small sample of respondents (18 respondents from 6 departments), introducing a possible self-selection bias — the source hedges (attributed to the authors, phrased "provavelmente"/probably) that more engaged and confident faculty were more likely to complete the instrument; this remains an authors'-stated caveat rather than a verified mechanism, since no comparison of respondent versus non-respondent departments is reported. Participants tended to select only low-effort, low-time-impact curricular changes. Limitations concerning generalizability beyond the single-institution case, absence of a comparison/control group, or possible confounds in the complexity metric itself (e.g., whether Heileman et al.'s algorithm captures pedagogically meaningful complexity) are NOT REPORTED as acknowledged limitations by the authors.
