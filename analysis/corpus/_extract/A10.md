## A10 — Action research on educational developers facilitating a faculty Community of Practice during curriculum review

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study, by Derek Thurber and Karen Bossen, published in *To Improve the Academy* (Vol. 44, No. 1, Winter 2025), titled "Exploring the potential role of educational developers in facilitating curriculum review processes" (DOI: https://doi.org/10.3998/tia.4500), addresses the role of educational developers (learning designers / teaching-and-learning specialists) as facilitators of curriculum review processes in higher education, specifically through the cultivation of faculty communities of practice. Although curriculum review offers recognized academic benefits, it is not a widely consolidated practice in many areas of higher education. This is attributed to faculty commonly facing scarcity of time and lack of specialized methodological knowledge/expertise needed to plan and conduct curricular reviews autonomously, compounded by curriculum design and review tasks being frequently unrewarded or unrecognized within traditional academic workload models, generating resistance and confusion about who should take on this responsibility. The stated relevance rationale is that accreditation bodies (e.g., the Higher Learning Commission in the US), government agencies, and external demands for transparency and market competitiveness exert increasing pressure on higher education institutions to demonstrate program quality through measurable continuous improvement.

**2. Object of investigation** — [DERIVED] The process and effects of embedding educational developers as facilitators within a structured curriculum-review action-research cycle involving a specific three-person faculty team in one small graduate program; the unit of analysis is the developer-facilitated review process itself and its downstream effects on faculty community formation, rather than the curriculum content per se. This framing is not stated verbatim in the source but follows from combining the stated objective, the described intervention, and the results emphasizing CoP formation and the report's role.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — Conducted at the Mary Lou Fulton Teachers College (MLFTC), Arizona State University (ASU), during the spring 2021 semester. Participants were three full-time, tenured faculty responsible for the directive and teaching functions of a small, two-year, in-person master's program that graduated between 10 and 20 students annually. The environment investigated proceeded without institutional interference or pressure for compulsory reform — a comparatively unusual condition of stability that the source states facilitated the study's success (flagged later as a limitation).

**4. Study or article type** — Qualitative action research (empirical journal article).

**5. Methodological approach** — Qualitative action research ("pesquisa-ação qualitativa"). The intervention adapted Yonnie Chyung's (2018) ten-step cyclical evaluation model, organized into three phases across four collaborative meetings over three months: (1) Identification phase — alignment of interests with faculty, who defined three priority analytical foci: students' sense of belonging, coherence of the academic curriculum, and employability pathways of alumni; (2) Planning phase — adaptation of Malone et al.'s (2012) general belonging scale to the program's context, and design of a curricular alignment mapping plan (based on Veltri et al., 2011) measuring dimensions of communication, saturation, and curricular engagement; (3) Implementation phase — systematic collection of student responses, faculty responses (assessing alignment of course-level objectives with overall program objectives), and extraction of historical ASU alumni survey data. Findings were compiled into a 15-page final report delivered to faculty, followed by a two-hour reflective discussion session in which developers facilitated but intervened minimally (less than 10% of total speaking time), intending to encourage autonomous faculty interaction. After the intervention, the three faculty participated in individual semi-structured interviews focused on perceptions of belonging, curriculum design, and the role of the review in the CoP. Recordings were transcribed verbatim and subjected to systematic thematic coding analysis using Atlas.ti software, structured according to descriptive and in vivo coding steps described by Saldaña (2021).

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Communities of Practice (CoP) theory, attributed to Jean Lave (1991) and elaborated by Etienne Wenger (1998), with Wenger's three structuring pillars: domain (collective interest and shared identity), community (interactions, discussions, and mutual learning relationships), and practice (development of a shared repertoire of resources and intervention tools). Faculty Learning Communities (FLCs), a concept from Milton Cox (2004), described as an interdisciplinary variant of CoPs within higher education, oriented toward fostering evidence-based pedagogical practices. Prior curriculum-mapping research (e.g., Uchiyama & Radin, 2009; Salmona & Smart, 2017), which shows that CoPs support curricular restructuring by enabling faculty to rethink everyday practices, but that such processes commonly fail due to practical implementation challenges: lack of faculty ownership, chronic scarcity of time and resources, and traditional barriers between disciplines. The concept of "boundary object," attributed to Wenger (1998) in the discussion section, used to interpret the function of the curricular review report as an artifact bridging faculty perspectives.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — Three tenured faculty (the entire directive and teaching staff) of one small, two-year, in-person master's program at a single institution (MLFTC, ASU), supported by two educational developers; program graduates 10–20 students annually.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — A single-semester (Spring 2021), developer-facilitated curriculum-review cycle built on an adaptation of Chyung's (2018) ten-step model, run across three phases (Identification, Planning, Implementation) and four collaborative meetings over three months. Faculty jointly selected three analytical foci (belonging, curricular coherence, employability pathways); developers then produced a belonging-scale instrument adaptation, a curricular alignment map, and analysis of historical alumni survey data; results were compiled into a 15-page report delivered to faculty and discussed in a two-hour reflective session where facilitators deliberately limited their own speaking time to under 10% to encourage autonomous faculty interaction.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — Three full-time tenured faculty holding the directive and teaching roles of the program; two educational developers who designed and facilitated the intervention (produced instruments, maps, and the report, and led the reflective discussion with minimal intervention); students and alumni as indirect data sources (via belonging-scale responses and historical ASU alumni survey data) though no student-level findings are reported directly.

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — The three faculty-selected analytical foci structuring the review: students' sense of belonging; coherence of the academic curriculum; employability pathways of alumni. Measurement dimensions within the curricular alignment mapping plan (based on Veltri et al., 2011): communication, saturation, and curricular engagement.

**11. Contextual conditions** — A comparatively stable institutional environment free of interference or pressure for compulsory reform — an unusual condition the source states facilitated the study's success, later flagged as a limitation on generalizability. Broader field-level pressure exists from accreditation bodies (e.g., Higher Learning Commission), government agencies, and external transparency/competitiveness demands, though these did not manifest as direct pressure within the studied program itself.

**12. Barriers** — Faculty commonly face scarcity of time and lack of specialized methodological expertise for autonomous curricular review; curriculum design/review work is frequently unrewarded or unrecognized in traditional academic workload models, generating resistance and confusion about ownership. Despite localized success, participants expressed clear concern about continuing and maintaining the curricular goals after the facilitators' departure, indicating faculty considered the likelihood of pursuing improvements on their own, without continued facilitation, to be low, due to competing daily demands. Prior curriculum-mapping literature (Uchiyama & Radin, 2009; Salmona & Smart, 2017) cited in the theoretical foundation reports that CoP-based curricular restructuring commonly fails due to lack of faculty ownership, chronic scarcity of time and resources, and traditional barriers between disciplines.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — The facilitator's neutrality role: faculty highlighted that having external, neutral educational developers avoided the process appearing as an imposition of particular power agendas, fostering a comfortable and safe environment for self-analysis and qualified professional development. The report/alignment map functioned as a "point-at-able object" — a tangible anchor that, beyond the statistical data itself, established conditions guiding faculty reflection on students' academic trajectories, later interpreted via Wenger's "boundary object" concept as providing a practical basis for faculty learning by visually illustrating how individual courses interrelate from students' perspective. Developer time investment in producing the diagnostic and detailed maps is argued to be the very condition enabling CoP viability, since faculty could not mobilize such resources on their own given competing research and governance duties.

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — Chyung's (2018) ten-step model adapted into three phases (Identification, Planning, Implementation) across four collaborative meetings over three months; production of a 15-page final report; a two-hour reflective discussion session with facilitator speaking time deliberately capped below 10%; post-intervention individual semi-structured interviews with all three faculty, analyzed via Atlas.ti using descriptive and in vivo coding (Saldaña, 2021).

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — Barriers to sustainability were explicitly reported: participants expressed concern about continuing curricular goals after the facilitators' departure and indicated a low likelihood of independent follow-through given competing daily demands. [DERIVED] The finding that approximately 100 developer-hours were needed for a single small-program cycle implies that scaling this model to larger departments would require proportionally larger investments of specialized staff time — a concern raised as a limitation and future-work item rather than resolved. [DERIVED] The emphasis on facilitator neutrality as vital for building faculty trust suggests curriculum review processes should be structured with external, non-evaluative facilitators rather than administered by supervisors or administrators internal to the reviewed program. [DERIVED] The insufficiency of a single-semester intervention format to perpetuate ongoing engagement and proposed curricular changes suggests sustained, cyclical facilitation models (e.g., recurring annual/semesterly follow-ups) are needed for durable curricular reform, rather than a one-off cycle. These implications are reasonable extrapolations connecting explicit resource-intensity, neutrality, and insufficiency findings to their institutional consequences, but are not phrased as a distinct "implications" section in the source.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - Fostering and consolidation of a CoP: all participants reportedly affirmed that participating in the process unified the group and activated a productive space for horizontal conversations about course improvement.
  - The report functioned as a "point-at-able object": the alignment map and report served as tangible anchors; beyond the statistical data itself, the physical existence of the document established conditions that guided faculty reflection on students' academic trajectories.
  - The facilitator's neutrality role: faculty highlighted that having external, neutral educational developers avoided the process appearing as an imposition of particular power agendas, fostering a comfortable and safe environment for self-analysis and qualified professional development.
  - Barriers to sustainability: despite localized success, participants expressed clear concern about continuing and maintaining the curricular goals after the facilitators' departure, indicating the likelihood of pursuing improvements on their own, without continued facilitation, would be low, due to competing daily demands.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - The curriculum review report functioned as a "boundary object" (per Wenger, 1998), providing a practical basis for faculty learning by visually illustrating how individual courses interrelate from students' perspective.
  - Facilitator political neutrality is presented as vital: curricular processes in higher education generally arrive in a top-down, imposed manner, which tends to erode faculty trust and undermine CoP formation potential.
  - The time investment of the developers is argued to be the very condition enabling CoP viability: producing the diagnostic and detailed maps consumed resources that faculty could not mobilize on their own given competing research and governance duties; developer technical support "é a própria condição que permite a manifestação de uma CoP viável" (paraphrased: is the very condition that permits the manifestation of a viable CoP).
  - The single-semester intervention format is argued to be insufficient to perpetuate ongoing engagement and proposed curricular changes.

**18. Contribution** — Practical: the study offers a replicable methodological design for pedagogical partnership between administrative instructional-design units (e.g., digital learning offices or faculty development centers) and academic departments, demonstrating how to relieve faculty of time and data-processing burdens to open space for refined pedagogical design discussions. Theoretical: the study expands understanding and practical application of Wenger's Community of Practice concepts within higher education, documenting in detail the utility of mixed-data analytic reports as direct catalysts ("boundary objects") for collective academic decision-making.

**19. Limitations** — Reduced sample scale: the study centered on a single small master's program with only three faculty members. High consumption of specialized resources: the process required approximately 100 collective hours of work from the two educational developers over one semester, described as a considerable operational amount that may be infeasible for some institutions. Non-political context: the environment investigated proceeded without institutional interference or pressure for compulsory reform, an unusually stable scenario stated to have facilitated the study's success. Future-work directions recorded: (1) evaluating replicability with larger undergraduate/graduate departments and larger faculty bodies; (2) scientifically measuring the comparative impact of this deep-advisory model against other standardized faculty-development center services; (3) structuring and examining new long-term continuous facilitation formats, e.g., using commitment tools generated in meetings to establish annual or semesterly follow-ups.

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — Communities of Practice (CoP) — Lave/Wenger framework, with the three pillars of domain, community, and practice; Faculty Learning Communities (FLCs) as an interdisciplinary variant of CoPs in higher education (Cox, 2004); boundary object / "point-at-able object" as a concept for artifacts that anchor collective reflection and bridge disciplinary or role-based perspectives; facilitator neutrality as a condition for trust-building in top-down-vulnerable institutional change processes; curriculum mapping/alignment as a methodological device linking course-level objectives to program-level objectives and to student experience data; the distinction between one-time/single-cycle interventions versus sustained, cyclical facilitation models for durable institutional or pedagogical change; the structural tension between faculty time/workload scarcity and the demand for continuous, evidence-based curricular improvement; the role of specialized intermediary staff (educational/instructional developers) in absorbing operational and technical burden to enable substantive faculty engagement; educational developer as facilitator/intermediary role; curriculum review as an under-incentivized academic labor category; sustainability and continuity of reform after facilitator withdrawal; resource intensity of deep-advisory models; action research as a methodology for institutional pedagogical change; top-down vs. bottom-up curricular change dynamics; accreditation/external accountability pressure as an institutional driver of curriculum review.

**21. Tensions or assumptions** — [DERIVED] The study's claims about CoP formation, facilitator neutrality, and boundary-object effects rest on interview data from only three tenured faculty in one small graduate program; the source itself flags this as a limitation, but the discussion section's stronger causal-sounding claims (e.g., that developer support "is the very condition" enabling a viable CoP) go beyond what a three-participant, single-cycle action research design can typically establish as generalizable causation. [DERIVED] The institutional context was explicitly non-political and free of compulsory-reform pressure — a condition the authors themselves acknowledge may have facilitated success. This raises an internal tension: the study's central argument for the value of developer-facilitated neutrality was tested precisely in a setting where political neutrality was less likely to be contested, potentially limiting the strength of the "neutrality is vital" claim under more politically fraught conditions. [DERIVED] The reliance on faculty self-report (post-intervention interviews with the same three faculty who were the direct beneficiaries of the intervention) raises a question, not addressed in the source, about potential response or social-desirability bias in participants' evaluations of a process the developers themselves also facilitated and the researchers apparently co-designed. [DERIVED] Participants themselves stated they are unlikely to sustain the reforms without continued facilitation, which sits in tension with the practice contribution claim that the model is "replicable" — replicability of the facilitation process does not necessarily imply sustainability of outcomes once facilitation ends; this tension is not resolved explicitly, though it is flagged as a limitation/future-work item. [DERIVED] The resource-intensity finding (~100 collective developer-hours per semester for a 3-faculty program) sits in tension with the practical contribution claim of offering a broadly "replicable" model; scalability for larger departments is raised as an open question rather than asserted as resolved.
