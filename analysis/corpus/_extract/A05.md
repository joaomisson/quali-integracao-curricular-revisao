## A05 — Ten-step development of an interdisciplinary Sustainability Science B.S. at Kean University

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The article investigates the process of developing, implementing, and evolving an interdisciplinary undergraduate curriculum focused on Sustainability Science. The study is motivated by three explicitly stated gaps/problems: (1) a scarcity of dedicated programs — fewer than 3% of roughly 2,900 four-year U.S. higher-education institutions were found to offer programs containing "sustainable" or "sustainability" in their degree titles; (2) a pattern of superficial or "cosmetic" reformulations, in which institutions rearranged existing courses from other departments rather than designing a single new, centrally focused course, risking trivialization of the field into a cliché; (3) a widespread perception among academics that designing a dedicated sustainability-science bachelor's program from scratch was an excessively difficult undertaking. Stated relevance rationale: Sustainability Science had matured into an autonomous discipline, and the study's setting, New Jersey, faced rigorous state greenhouse-gas mitigation targets and severe pollution problems that the authors frame as urgently requiring a prepared scientific workforce.

**2. Object of investigation** — The design, approval, implementation, and revision process of a specific undergraduate degree program (curriculum) as an organizational and pedagogical artifact — not a theoretical construct being tested empirically. A secondary object is student demand and receptivity toward a sustainability-focused degree, assessed through two opinion surveys and later through enrollment and qualitative feedback data.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — Kean University, a public higher-education institution in New Jersey, USA, specifically the College of Natural, Applied and Health Sciences (departments of Biology, Chemistry-Physics, Computer Science, and Earth Sciences). Temporal scope: early program development (opinion surveys, spring and fall 2008) through governmental approval (September 2009), hiring of a program director, curricular revision, founding of the Center for Sustainability Studies (January 2011), and enrollment tracking through a projected fall 2012 cohort.

**4. Study or article type** — Descriptive case study of curricular and organizational development at a single institution. The article does not set out to test traditional scientific hypotheses; instead, the curriculum's restructuring and sequential logic were guided by four foundational questions about the relationship between humanity and the biosphere: (1) what unique characteristics of Earth allowed life to develop and evolve; (2) what humans are doing to compromise those characteristics; (3) why humans behave in ways that compromise Earth's life-supporting characteristics; (4) what corrective actions are necessary to achieve sustainability.

**5. Methodological approach** — Data sources: a formulation group (an interdisciplinary faculty committee plus the college dean); a first student opinion survey (spring 2008, convenience sample, 366 students, electronic Qualtrics questionnaire administered at a campus computer terminal during Earth Week); a second student opinion survey (fall 2008, 50 students, mostly from biology and biotechnology, administered by the FOCUS student group to gauge direct interest and career intentions); an external evaluation in which an independent consultant reviewed the Program Approval Document (PAD). Ongoing monitoring procedures: direct evaluation through analysis of capstone-course projects (to verify attainment of program goals) and indirect evaluation through a Graduating Student Survey. [DERIVED] This is a single-institution, largely narrative/descriptive case study relying on non-probabilistic convenience samples rather than a comparative, experimental, or statistically representative design — an interpretation of the methodology as reported, not a statement the note itself makes about the design's rigor.

**6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Five anchors: (1) the emergence of Sustainability Science as a mature, transdisciplinary research field, attributed to Clark & Dickson (2003), Kates et al. (2001), and Clark (2007); (2) the moral responsibility of universities to lead awareness-building and practical capacity for a viable future, grounded in The Essex Report (1995), Francisco & Steinfeld (2010), and van Dam-Mieras et al. (2008); (3) the stages-of-Environmental-Education model of Hungerford, Peyton, and Wilke (1980) — dividing learning into ecological foundations, conceptual awareness of issues, investigation/evaluation, and action skills — which inspired the curriculum's four guiding questions; (4) the concept of maladaptive human behavior from Maloney and Ward (1973), used to analyze why humans act contrary to their own life-support system; (5) a critique of curricular trivialization aligned with Blewitt (2004), concerning the risks of using "sustainability" as a label without structural innovation.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — Institutional/programmatic: a single new degree program (B.S. in Sustainability Science) at a single public university, tracked from planning through early post-launch enrollment. NOT APPLICABLE: multi-institution comparison or individual-learner-level analysis — neither is part of the design.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — A 10-step process (plus an 11th results item covering post-launch evidence): (1) definition of program objectives (workforce demand, inquiry-based learning, potential combined MBA option); (2) regional mapping showing no specialized competing sustainability programs in the NY/NJ region at the time; (3) vocational analysis showing high demand for environmental careers (citing the Kaplan career guide); (4) student demand evidence via the two surveys; (5) creation of an original curriculum with 72 science/mathematics credits and 11 new courses; (6) governmental approval, including unanimous approval by the New Jersey Council of Presidents in September 2009; (7) hiring of a program director (the article's first author), who identified the need to improve the program in response to criticism that it resembled a modified biology degree; (8) a full scope-and-sequence revision making sustainability the priority focus of each individual course rather than a topic addressed within traditional courses, including new courses such as "Mathematical Principles for Sustainability" and "Calculus Applied to Sustainability," mandatory integrative seminars across all four years, and division into two student concentrations (Earth Systems and Human Systems); (9) founding of the Center for Sustainability Studies (CSS) in January 2011 as an independent academic home facilitating interdisciplinary faculty affiliation from other colleges (e.g., design, arts); (10) institution of continuous outcome-assessment mechanisms integrated into the curriculum; (11) reported evidence of program success — continuous enrollment growth from 13 students (2010 inaugural cohort) to 25 (2011), 31 (early 2012), and a projected 40 (fall 2012), alongside strongly positive student feedback.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — The formulation group (interdisciplinary faculty committee plus college dean); the newly hired program director (article's first author); an independent external consultant reviewing the Program Approval Document; the New Jersey Council of Presidents (governmental/state approval body); student survey respondents (366 in Survey 1, 50 in Survey 2, the latter recruited via the FOCUS student group); interdisciplinary faculty affiliated with the Center for Sustainability Studies from other colleges (design, arts); partner two-year technical/technologist programs and high schools via the New Jersey Sustainability Science Education Partnership (NJSSEP).

**10. Decision dimensions or criteria** — Workforce/vocational demand (citing the Kaplan career guide); absence of regional competing programs; student demand evidence from opinion surveys; degree of centrality of sustainability as the organizing lens for each course (versus sustainability as a peripheral topic within traditional courses); state regulatory/environmental-policy urgency (New Jersey greenhouse-gas mitigation targets); avoidance of "cosmetic" reconfiguration in favor of newly authored courses.

**11. Contextual conditions** — Public university in New Jersey operating under state-level greenhouse-gas mitigation targets and facing severe regional pollution problems; absence of specialized regional competitor programs at the time of development; Sustainability Science described as a maturing, autonomous transdisciplinary field at the time of the study.

**12. Barriers** — The initial curriculum too closely resembled a lightly modified Biology or Environmental Studies program, with insufficient practical emphasis on the human and conceptual dimensions specific to sustainability; an early lack of intellectual cohesion among cohorts; "academic silos" named as an institutional barrier to transdisciplinary education; the widespread perception, prior to the project, that designing such a program from scratch was excessively difficult.

**13. Facilitators / drivers** — Strong student demand signals from both surveys (99% support in Survey 1; 77% career interest among biology students in Survey 2); absence of regional competitor programs; unanimous governmental approval; creation of a dedicated interdisciplinary organizational home (the Center for Sustainability Studies) enabling cross-college faculty affiliation; institutional partnership mechanisms (NJSSEP) creating a student pipeline via automatic transfer agreements with two-year programs and high-school outreach; continuous, curriculum-embedded outcome assessment.

**14. Implementation mechanisms** — Formulation-group design work culminating in an original 72-credit science/mathematics curriculum with 11 new courses; formal governmental approval process (New Jersey Council of Presidents, September 2009); external consultant review of the Program Approval Document; post-launch scope-and-sequence revision restructuring existing and new courses around a sustainability-first pedagogical lens; mandatory integrative seminars across all four years; division of students into two concentrations (Earth Systems and Human Systems); founding of the CSS as an institutional structure; ongoing assessment via capstone-course project evaluation (direct) and Graduating Student Survey (indirect).

**15. Institutionalization / sustainability conditions** — The program's institutionalization is evidenced by governmental approval, hiring of a dedicated program director, founding of a dedicated organizational center (CSS), formal partnership agreements (NJSSEP) creating a recruitment pipeline, and continuous enrollment growth through the reported window (13 to a projected 40 students, 2010–2012). [DERIVED] The claim of "program success" rests on continued enrollment growth over a short window and positive qualitative feedback; longer-term outcome measures (graduation rates, employment outcomes, learning-outcome assessment results) that would more directly substantiate sustained programmatic success are not reported in the note.

**16. Principal findings** —
  - Regional mapping showed no specialized competing sustainability programs in the NY/NJ region at the time of development.
  - Vocational analysis showed high demand for environmental careers (citing the Kaplan career guide).
  - Student demand evidence: 99% of Survey 1 respondents (n=366) supported institutional promotion of sustainability; 77% of biology students in Survey 2 (n=50) expressed interest in pursuing sustainability-related careers.
  - An original curriculum was created with 72 science/mathematics credits and 11 new courses, receiving unanimous approval by the New Jersey Council of Presidents in September 2009.
  - The newly hired program director identified that the initial program resembled a modified biology degree and led a full scope-and-sequence revision making sustainability the priority focus of each course, adding courses such as "Mathematical Principles for Sustainability" and "Calculus Applied to Sustainability," mandatory integrative seminars across all four years, and two student concentrations (Earth Systems and Human Systems).
  - The Center for Sustainability Studies (CSS) was founded in January 2011 as an independent academic home facilitating interdisciplinary faculty affiliation from other colleges.
  - Continuous outcome-assessment mechanisms were instituted, integrated into the curriculum.
  - Reported evidence of program success: continuous enrollment growth from 13 students (2010) to 25 (2011), 31 (early 2012), and a projected 40 (fall 2012), alongside strongly positive student feedback on the systemic approach and integrated redesign of science coursework.

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - Sustainability should function as the priority integrating lens for teaching, such that when calculus and science courses are taught within practical sustainability contexts, students better understand and accept the mathematical and theoretical challenges of the coursework.
  - The traditional fragmented educational model — historically rejected by theorists such as Nathaniel Shaler in 1905 — fails to give students a sense of the unity and interconnection of nature.
  - Genuinely innovative and robust training requires breaking down "academic silos" and promoting permanent transdisciplinary forums.

**18. Contribution** — Methodological/practical: a structured, applied 10-step roadmap intended as a reference for other universities seeking to navigate the bureaucratic and pedagogical challenges of creating their own program. Pedagogical: the concept of teaching rigid traditional scientific content (calculus, chemistry, etc.) within a sustainability context to optimize student engagement. Institutional-partnerships: creation of the New Jersey Sustainability Science Education Partnership (NJSSEP), enabling automatic transfer agreements for students from two-year technical/technologist programs and outreach connections to high schools.

**19. Limitations** — Two author-acknowledged limitations: the initial curriculum too closely resembled a lightly modified Biology or Environmental Studies program, with insufficient practical emphasis on the human and conceptual dimensions specific to sustainability, necessitating a deep revision led by the newly hired director; an early lack of intellectual cohesion among cohorts, which led to the creation of mandatory seminars across all years. The article explicitly does not list formal methodological limitations regarding the evaluation of the student questionnaires. [DERIVED] This absence is notable given that both surveys relied on convenience/self-selected samples (366 students recruited at a single Earth Week event; 50 students recruited through a sustainability-oriented student group, mostly from biology/biotechnology) — suggesting a gap between the limitations the article foregrounds (curricular design shortcomings) and limitations an external reader might identify in the evidentiary basis for demand claims (sampling bias, absence of response rates, lack of a comparison group).

**20. Candidate concepts / themes** — Curriculum design and development process in higher education; interdisciplinarity and transdisciplinarity as institutional goals; program legitimation and external/governmental approval processes; assessment of student demand via opinion surveys in program planning; institutional change management and organizational restructuring in universities; sustainability science as an emerging autonomous academic discipline; pedagogical reframing of STEM course content around sustainability contexts; continuous program evaluation and iterative curricular revision; academic organizational structures — departmental silos versus dedicated interdisciplinary centers; workforce/vocational demand as justification for new degree programs; sustainability as primary pedagogical lens versus supplementary/add-on theme; risk of "cosmetic"/superficial curricular relabeling.

**21. Tensions or assumptions** — The article generalizes a 10-step process derived from a single institution operating within a specific state political and regulatory context (New Jersey's greenhouse-gas mitigation goals) into a prescriptive guide for other institutions; no discussion is reported of how institutional, regulatory, or resource differences across contexts might limit transferability. [DERIVED] The central pedagogical argument — that teaching quantitative/scientific content through a sustainability lens improves student comprehension and acceptance of difficulty — is presented by the article as a conclusion, but the reported evidentiary basis appears to be enrollment growth and qualitative student praise rather than a controlled or outcome-based comparison (no reported data on learning outcomes, grades, or retention before versus after the scope-and-sequence revision); treating it as an established causal finding rather than an authors' interpretive claim would overstate what the evidence shows. [DERIVED] Both opinion surveys used non-probabilistic, convenience/self-selected samples (Earth Week attendees; a sustainability-interest student group's respondents already predisposed toward the topic), which plausibly inflates supportive figures such as the 99% endorsement rate, since self-selection toward an already-engaged population is a recognized source of upward bias in attitudinal survey results — the article itself does not draw this inference. The article does not list formal methodological limitations for these surveys, itself a notable gap given the demand-justification role these figures play. The claim of "program success" rests on continued enrollment growth over a short window (2010–projected 2012) and positive qualitative feedback, without longer-term outcome measures (graduation rates, employment outcomes, learning-outcome assessment results). The critique of "cosmetic" sustainability programs is a strong normative framing positioning the authors' from-scratch approach as inherently superior, without any reported direct comparative evaluation between the authors' program and the "cosmetic" alternatives being criticized.

**22. Note-layer caveats specific to this study** — The note does not report: response rates (the eligible population size relative to the 366 and 50 respondents), demographic composition beyond departmental affiliation, or the exact survey question wording used to elicit the 99% and 77% figures; details of the capstone-course assessment rubric or criteria used to verify attainment of program learning goals, nor findings from the Graduating Student Survey; learning-outcome, retention, graduation, or post-graduation employment data for students in the revised program; the specific criteria or findings of the external consultant's review of the Program Approval Document beyond noting that such a review occurred; funding sources, author institutional roles beyond "first author became director," or conflict-of-interest information; how "sustainability" was operationally defined for survey respondents, which bears on how the demand figures should be interpreted; comparative data from other institutions' sustainability programs beyond the general claim that no specialized regional competitors existed at the time; and the specific mechanism by which the Hungerford, Peyton, and Wilke (1980) model was translated into the four curricular questions (the note states only that the model "inspired" the questions).
