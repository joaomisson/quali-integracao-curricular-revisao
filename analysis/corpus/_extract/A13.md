## A13 — Program Director's autoethnographic account of whole-of-program accounting curriculum reform during a financial/pandemic crisis

**1. Research problem / problem framing** — The study addresses large-scale, whole-of-program curriculum reform in higher education, specifically in accounting degree programs, examined through the leadership lens of a Program Director (an intermediate academic leadership role). The research problem is framed as a chronic tension between the need to modernize higher education curricula and the structural barriers imposed by traditional university culture. Accounting curricula are historically criticized for being excessively technical, outdated relative to market digitalization, and isolated from related disciplines, while reform efforts face systemic resistance to change, slow bureaucratic processes, internal political disputes, and workload-allocation systems that reward pure research over pedagogical innovation. A claimed literature gap is recorded: the scientific literature rarely details the practical process of integral curriculum reforms led by program directors under conditions of uncertainty and severe financial constraint. The study's relevance is situated in financial pressures from reduced public funding, dependence on international student revenue, and the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, concurrent with new global and national professional accreditation guidelines.

**2. Object of investigation** — The process of leading a whole-of-program curriculum reform — encompassing curriculum review, redesign, and bureaucratic approval — as experienced and narrated by the intermediate academic leader who drove it. The object is thus simultaneously the curriculum reform process itself and the leadership practice/reflective experience of conducting it; this dual framing is directly supported by the source's repeated emphasis on both curriculum outcomes and the leader's "reflections on challenges and successes," per the article's own subtitle recorded in the reference section.

**3. Educational / institutional context** — A single Australian university (referred to pseudonymously as "Australian University" / AU) undergoing corporate restructuring, mass layoffs, and severe budget cuts due to the pandemic crisis. The study period is October 2019 to May 2021 (18 months). The disciplinary context is accounting education within a business school.

**4. Study or article type** — Practitioner action research / autoethnographic self-study (empirical, first-person account).

**5. Methodological approach** — Collaborative action research combined with qualitative reflective practice, based on critical self-study narrated by the project's leader (the author herself, in her role as Program Director). Participants and data: a 4-person steering committee (including two senior academics); a team of 17 accounting discipline faculty who systematically reviewed 11 accounting programs comprising 51 compulsory subjects; subsequently, 13 discipline specialists who cooperated in redesigning 25 core compulsory subjects. Primary and secondary data sources: subject outlines, course structures, student surveys, institutional retention data, student focus-group interviews, employer interviews, national teaching guidelines, and the author's personal reflective diary maintained during the project. The analytic procedure is reflective thematic analysis along two axes: (a) alignment of subjects with market/employability demands, and (b) internal consistency of pedagogical outlines, evaluated through the lens of Constructive Alignment (coupling of learning outcomes, assessments, and teaching practices). **6. Theoretical / conceptual foundations** — Reflective Practice and Transformative Learning, attributed to Jack Mezirow and Edward Taylor, holding that constant critical reflection allows educators to reinterpret experience and transform problematic scenarios into more effective teaching actions. Constructive Alignment (CA), attributed to John Biggs and Catherine Tang, concerning systematic harmony between intended learning outcomes (ILOs), teaching activities, and assessment methods. Action Research (AR) and Action Learning (AL), oriented toward solving real problems through continuous spirals of planning, action, observation, and joint reflection, drawing on David Kember, Ortrun Zuber-Skerritt, Harvey & Jones, and McGill & Beaty. Situational factors and adaptive leadership, drawing on L. Dee Fink's integrated course design models and contemporary adaptive leadership approaches for crisis periods.

**7. Unit or level of analysis** — A single whole-of-program curriculum reform effort at one university, spanning 11 accounting programs (51 compulsory subjects) rationalized into 2 flagship programs (25 redesigned compulsory subjects), narrated from the single vantage point of the Program Director who led it.

**8. Process of curricular review / change / redesign investigated** — Organized into three phases. Phase 1 (Curriculum Review): the existence of 11 separate programs generated content redundancy and strong student confusion regarding pathway choice; a serious gap was mapped in the integration of digital technologies (e.g., data analytics and business intelligence tools) and critical professional competencies (critical thinking, ethical judgment, interpersonal communication skills); assessment systems were found to be misaligned and excessively limited, relying largely on multiple-choice tests (measuring low cognitive levels), take-home essays, and traditional closed written exams; prior curricular decisions were reportedly made "behind closed doors" for purely political leadership reasons, without grounding in concrete data. Phase 2 (Curriculum Redesign): rationalization of 11 former courses into just 2 flagship programs, modernized, highly flexible, and containing interdisciplinary and work-integrated learning options; structured pedagogical redesign of 25 compulsory subjects; introduction of new business analytics subjects co-designed with IT specialists, data analysts, and market partners; creation by the author of a plain-language "Reference Guide" ("Guia de Referência") to enable subject specialists to define ILOs and assessments without technical jargon; diversification of assessment methods to include practical assessments, interactive simulations, and presentations alongside traditional written tests. Phase 3 (Bureaucratic Approval): submission of a total of 103 curriculum review documents, successfully approved by the institution's Academic Board, substantially exceeding the historical average of 9 submissions for the same period at the institution; this reportedly required approximately 200 hours of direct bureaucratic writing work by the author, on top of her regulated teaching duties. A consolidated organizational-culture change is also recorded: destabilization/simplification ("desburocratização") of the internal peer-review rite within the business school, attributed to the author's feedback and advocacy.

**9. Actors / stakeholders involved** — The Program Director (author/narrator); a 4-person steering committee including two senior academics; 17 accounting discipline faculty who reviewed the 11 programs; 13 discipline specialists who redesigned the 25 core subjects; IT specialists, data analysts, and market partners co-designing new business analytics subjects; students (via surveys, retention data, and focus groups); employers (via interviews); the institution's Academic Board (approval body).

**11. **12. Barriers** — Systemic resistance to change, slow bureaucratic processes, internal political disputes, and workload-allocation systems that reward pure research over pedagogical innovation. Assessment systems were found misaligned and excessively limited prior to reform. Prior curricular decisions were made "behind closed doors" for political reasons without data grounding. Traditional higher-education governance and bureaucratic approval structures are described as excessively slow, inflexible, and exhausting, functioning as an active disincentive for faculty to initiate teaching reforms because of the exhausting administrative workload involved (approximately 200 hours of bureaucratic writing work fell on the author alone, on top of her regulated teaching duties, leading to a temporary sacrifice of her own disciplinary research).

**17. Principal arguments** —
  - Real-world action research processes in unstable environments are profoundly complex, dynamic, overlapping, and non-linear, in contrast to the simplified cyclical schemas formally presented in the literature.
  - The COVID-19 crisis, although traumatic and productive of work overload and faculty layoffs, functioned as a catalyst that broke habitual individual academic resistance, accelerating agile pedagogical innovations such as online teaching and flexible modern assessments.
  - Traditional higher-education governance and bureaucratic approval structures are excessively slow, inflexible, and exhausting, functioning as an active disincentive for faculty to initiate teaching reforms because of the exhausting administrative workload involved.
  - University leadership should support, protect, and value the Scholarship of Learning and Teaching and pedagogical design innovations in promotion decisions and workload allocation, which remain systematically obscured by favoritism toward conventional scientific research.

Practical: consolidation of obsolete and misaligned courses into integrated, digital-data-analysis-focused degrees, combined with organizational gains from rationalizing and simplifying peer-review norms at the university.
